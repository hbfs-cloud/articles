# Wednesday's rate rise is priced. The sentence after it isn't.

*Prediction markets give 78.5% to a hike. The Fed delivers it into a war that closed the Strait of Hormuz in March and shut Saudi Arabia's only detour last Thursday.*

Almost everything fell together last week. Stocks, bonds and gold all went down. The only move of any size went the other way: oil, up 9.1%.

Wednesday 16 September the Federal Reserve announces at 2:00pm New York time, with a press conference at 2:30pm. Prediction markets — the Polymarket contracts on the outcome — give 78.5% to a 25 basis point rise, 20.5% to no change, 0.4% to a cut.

So the direction isn't the question. What the Fed says about prices it can't reach — that's the question.

## Why tighten at all

August inflation came out on 11 September, the same day as the closing prices in this letter. Headline: 3.4% over twelve months. Core, stripping out food and energy: 2.4%, down from 2.5% the month before.

Core is slowing. Energy is what's holding the headline up — it rose 2.1% on the month after falling in July, with gasoline up 3.9%.

The gap between those two numbers is the part monetary policy doesn't reach. That's the whole problem with Wednesday.

## The strait, and the detour

Hormuz carried roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas. It's been effectively closed to commercial traffic since 2 March, after US and Israeli strikes on Iran. Traffic went from more than 100 vessels a day to about five. Gulf crude exports are down roughly 47% from before the war — around 17 million barrels a day, now about nine.

Saudi Arabia had a way around it: the East–West pipeline, running crude across the country to Yanbu on the Red Sea. It was moving roughly 5 million barrels a day.

On 10 September, drones launched from Iraq hit that pipeline near Riyadh and Medina and damaged pumping stations. Saudi Arabia shut it down as a precaution. Nobody has claimed responsibility.

Two things cut the other way and they belong right here. The pumping-station damage is reported by news agencies, not confirmed first-hand — Reuters' own direct element on 11 September was satellite imagery showing smoke near the pipeline. And oil fell on Friday: the oil fund closed at $154.90 after $158.38 on Thursday. The premium had already started deflating before the weekend.

That gives a level worth writing down. A close back below $149.97 — the 9 September close — erases most of the premium and takes this whole reading off the table. It's 3.3% away. One session does it.

Meanwhile the cost is landing on shore. Reuters, citing fuel tracker GasBuddy, reports the average US diesel price passed $6 a gallon for the first time. Diesel moves trucks, trains and tractors.

## The volatility curve, and what I got wrong

Nine-day implied volatility is 14.47 against 15.84 for one month. It's tempting to call that complacency ahead of a heavy week. It isn't. The curve slopes up with time almost always, so the short end is the cheapest point in an ordinary week too.

| Window | Implied volatility |
| --- | --- |
| 9 days | 14.47 |
| 30 days | 15.84 |
| 3 months | 18.60 |
| 6 months | 20.39 |

What moved is the relationship. The nine-day-to-one-month ratio was 0.82 a week ago. It's 0.91 now. The short end got more expensive relative to the rest, not less.

Trip.com reports Tuesday 15 September after the close. Lennar reports Wednesday 16 September after the close — hours after the Fed.

Those percentages are priced to options expiring Friday 18 September — a window holding the Trip.com report, the Fed, and the Lennar report. Neither is a clean earnings number, Lennar's least of all.

The shape is still striking. On both stocks the move being paid for is larger than the distance left to the lowest price of the past year. If either falls anything like the amount priced, it makes a new one-year low that week. Nothing underneath.

![The options cost more than the floor is worth](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d67b12e2-47ae-4005-983e-c431c2957048_2400x1240.png)

**Trip.com** trades at 9.2 times expected earnings, below its 20-, 50- and 200-day averages of $42.70, $44.29 and $52.77. Over 90 days it took three broker downgrades and zero upgrades, the latest on 3 September. Two cautions. The unusual options activity flagged on it is deep in-the-money puts, which is as often plumbing as a directional bet. And I don't have a history of how this stock reacts to its own results — that's not the same as a reassuring history. It carries a $1.5bn convertible bond due June 2029 settling its principal in cash, so potential dilution is capped, and it's a Cayman company trading as depositary shares with its China exposure held through a contractual structure. None of that moves Tuesday's print. All of it sits in the multiple.

**Lennar** fell after seven of its last eight reports. Average move −4.7%, worst −8.6%, the single gain +0.5%. Typical size ignoring direction: 4.9%, below the 6.8% priced today. One beat in the quarters available, average surprise −3.1%. No broker action at all in 90 days.

The short base is where people will oversell the story, so here it is carefully. As of 31 August, 9.07% of the free float was sold short — the highest of six readings, but the series has run in a narrow 8.33–9.07% band throughout, so it's a short base sitting still rather than building. Days to cover actually fell, to 7.4 from 9.01. Crowded, not getting more crowded. Enough to cancel whatever edge you thought the bad history handed you; not a reason to be long. Lennar also has an automatic shelf registration filed 10 April 2026 covering stock and debt — routine at this size, no raise announced, but it belongs in any conversation about crowded shorts.

## What I'm doing

Nothing new before the press conference. The reason is arithmetic. On both stocks the move being priced is larger than the distance to the yearly low, so a stop under that floor gets jumped by a gap of exactly the size everyone expects.

I'd want stocks and long bonds rising together, and staying up, after Wednesday's conference before I looked at adding. Both at once — stocks alone prove nothing, they rise on relief all the time and give it back by Friday. And with a strait shut and 5 million barrels a day of detour offline, bonds and growth rallying together could just as easily be a growth scare, which isn't a reason to add either. So I'd want the oil premium still intact alongside it, or cyclicals holding up against defensives, to tell the two apart.

The other side: a firm message on the path while supply stays broken. Then the thing to watch is whether energy keeps leading and whether the growth-versus-defensives gap closes.

On diversification, because it fails in a particular way here. Spreading money across growth, gold, long bonds and housing looks like four decisions. Measured over the last 30 sessions it partly is — the Nasdaq against long bonds correlates +0.34, gold against long bonds +0.34, and at those levels spreading genuinely cuts variance. The claim that they're one bet is a hypothesis about Wednesday, not a property of last week, when technology rose while bonds and gold fell.

The real doubling-up is inside each sleeve, not across them: the S&P against the Nasdaq is +0.91, gold against silver +0.89.

Energy is the one leg that doesn't share the rate factor, and it's measurable — oil runs −0.68 against big US equities and −0.71 against long bonds. It's also the most dangerous thing on this page to buy right now. Oil is up 21.6% in eleven sessions and sits 3.3% above the level that breaks the whole supply-shock reading. If I were carrying anything here it would be that leg rather than a hedge on the duration side, but on a pullback toward $149.97, not at Friday's price.

## Method and limits

Prices are daily closes through Friday 11 September. Weekly changes compare the previous Friday's close to that one. Labor Day made it a four-session week. Returns ignore dividends and fees. USO holds oil futures, so its return drifts from the price of crude.

The crypto funds — Bitcoin −3.2%, Ethereum +3.5%, Solana +0.5% — close with the US stock market, so they miss the weekend.

Last week I set a specific, checkable condition on how Oracle would react to its results and said I'd grade it here. Oracle reported 10 September after the close and my reference close is 11 September, so the window was complete and I simply didn't measure it. My omission, not missing data. It carries to the next letter rather than disappearing.
