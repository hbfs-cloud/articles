# Winning six times out of eight and still losing money

Oracle reports on Thursday after the close. The options market is charging for a move of about 11.7% in either direction.

Is that expensive? There is a way to check, and it does not involve an opinion.

Oracle has reported eight times before. Each of those dates comes from a filing with the regulator, so we know exactly when the company spoke. The middle-sized move across those eight was **10.1%**. The market is asking 11.7%. So yes — on the face of it, the price of the move is above what the stock normally does.

That is where most people would stop, and it is where they would be wrong.

## Do the arithmetic all the way

Say you sold that 11.7% move each of the last eight times. You keep the money if the stock moves less than that.

Six of the eight times, it did. You win six.

Then look at the two you lose. One of them, the stock moved **37.7%** in a single reaction. That one loss is bigger than all six wins put together. Run the whole exercise and you end up **down** — after being right three times out of four.

Being right most of the time and losing anyway is one of the most useful things a set of numbers can tell you. It means the price is not a mistake. It is the cost of insurance against the one day you cannot afford.

## And about that one day

The 37.7% reaction happened on 9 September 2025.

September. The same quarter of the company's year as Thursday.

There is one other September in the record — 9 September 2024 — and the stock rose 9.9%. Two Septembers, two gains, and one of them the biggest move in the whole history.

Eight prints is a small sample and two is smaller still. We are not telling you the stock will go up. We are telling you where the tail lives, and it lives in this month.

## "But it already went up 12%"

You will hear this all week. Oracle rose 12.4% in three sessions before the report, so the good news must already be in the price.

Two problems with that.

First, the window. Measure five sessions instead of three and the gain is **5.3%**. The three-session number starts from the lowest close the stock has had in a month. Pick the low as your starting point and you always get the biggest number available.

Second, the volume. Those three up days traded between **0.85 and 1.02 times** the normal amount. That is not a stampede. That is an ordinary week. No analyst changed their view on the stock either.

A move with no volume behind it and no new opinions attached looks like people adjusting positions, not like people learning something. It does not mean the stock is stretched. It means you should not confuse a bounce with a signal.

## Broadcom is not the warning everyone thinks it is

Broadcom reported last week and the stock fell. People are already using it as the cautionary tale: high expectations, disappointment, punishment.

Check it.

Going into the report, Broadcom was **down** 0.5% over three sessions and **down** 5.7% over the month. Expectations were not high. The stock was already sliding.

And the famous "Broadcom is down 14.9% this month"? Of that, **12.1 points happened before the company said anything**. The report itself cost 3.4%.

Blaming a whole month on one evening is the kind of story that sounds right and measures wrong.

## What the other reports actually did

Three companies in the same business reported in this window. Everyone is talking about the one that fell. Here are all three:

- Salesforce, 26 August: **+22.5%**
- Dell, 1 September: **+7.9%**
- Broadcom, 2 September: **−3.4%**

Two big gains, one modest loss. If your rule is "always get out before earnings to avoid the gap," that rule cost you a great deal more than it saved over these three.

Wider still: of the twelve infrastructure companies we track, **nine are up** over the month. Of the three that are down, **two never reported at all**. So reporting is not what separates the winners from the losers here. Something else is going on, and we do not have a clean explanation for it — so we are not going to invent one.

## What we would do

Nothing, before Thursday night. Not because earnings are scary, but because the record gives no directional edge whatsoever: four gains, four losses.

If you already own it, the question is size, not direction. The position has to survive a move like that 37.7% going the wrong way. If it cannot, it is too big — regardless of what you think will happen.

And if the stock jumps on the news, resist the reflex to sell at the opening bell. In seven of the eight past reactions, the stock closed **higher** than it opened that day — including all four of the times the reaction was negative. For this particular stock, the opening print has been the low of the day, not the high.

## How we will know we were wrong

If Oracle reports and the stock moves up by more than that 11.7%, our reading was wrong. We said the pre-report rise was people shuffling positions. A move that size would mean it was information, and we missed it.

We will say so next week, in these words, without rewriting them.
