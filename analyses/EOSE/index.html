The Two-Minute Verdict

D
Speculative • Bearish structure

Eos Energy builds zinc-based batteries for long-duration grid storage — the unglamorous hardware that holds solar power for hours after the sun goes down. The stock has lost about 76% from its $19.19 high and now sits just above its 52-week low of $4.39. On 26 February it dropped 39% in one session after full-year revenue came in at $114.2M against guidance of $150–160M, with management owning up to manufacturing problems. Securities class actions followed within weeks.

So why look at it at all? Because the same report that scared everyone also carried a $701.5M backlog (2.8 GWh, up 9% in a quarter), a $303M federal loan guarantee behind the factory, and, on 5 July, the actual start of commercial production. Thirty percent of the float is sold short, and climbing. Real demand, a crowded short book, government money, and a price pinned at multi-year lows: that is the kind of setup that can jump 40% on a single believable headline.

Squeeze fuel

  • 30.1% of float short and rising — 101M shares
  • $701.5M / 2.8 GWh backlog, +9% quarter over quarter
  • Commercial production live since 5 July; $303M DOE guarantee
  • RSI 32.7, near the $4.39 low — oversold and cheap on the tape

Structural bear

  • −101.9% gross margin — loses money on every unit
  • −$267.5M EBITDA burn; FY net loss near $970M
  • Negative equity (book value −$2.56/share)
  • Rights offering priced above the market; open litigation
The number that caps the raise: Eos is raising money at $5.481 a unit. The stock trades at $4.625. Nobody rationally pays $5.481 for something available at $4.63 in the open market, so that raise risks coming up short — and $5.481 now hangs over the price like a ceiling. My read: a trade, not an investment.

What Just Happened — Two Weeks of Fireworks

26 February 2026: The break that started it all. Full-year revenue landed at $114.2M against $150–160M guidance. The fourth quarter alone printed $58.0M versus $93.7M expected, with EPS of −$0.72 (a $0.48 miss). Management disclosed line downtime and quality delays. The stock fell 39.4% to $6.74 that day. Securities class actions alleging misstatements on production scale followed.
5 July — Commercial production begins 2026

Eos started commercial production at its new facility, ramping the Z3 line. This is the proof point the bulls have waited for: the factory that missed 2025 guidance is now shipping. Execution from here is the whole thesis.

7 July — Frontier Power converts a 920 MWh portfolio 2026

Under the “Stella Strategic Framework,” Frontier Power USA is converting a 920 MWh battery-storage project portfolio. More demand behind the backlog number.

2 July — Rights offering launched at $5.481 2026

Up to 27,367,171 units at $5.481 (common plus fractional warrants), open to existing holders at roughly a 10% discount, expiring 21 July. Potential proceeds near $150M. The problem: the stock trades below the subscription price. See the Capital section for why that matters.

30 June — $75M direct offering to Hudson Bay Capital 2026

13.7M common shares plus 6.0M warrants at $5.481 per unit, funding Frontier Power USA. Combined with the rights offering, Eos is raising up to roughly $225M of its own equity if fully subscribed — that is the dilution Eos holders wear. Hudson Bay is separately putting about $125M straight into FPUSA ($50M already in). These 2026 equity raises fund FPUSA and are separate from the DOE-guaranteed loan behind the Turtle Creek factory. Short Interest & Who Owns It

Short interest is the heart of this setup, and it has climbed every reading this year: 24.0% of float in mid-January, 26.6% in February, 28.1% in March, and 30.1% at the latest count. In share terms the short book grew from 80M to 101M. Days to cover sit between 3.7 and 4.5, so it would take the shorts several sessions of normal volume to buy back. That is the raw material of a squeeze.

But the squeeze is not primed. Cost to borrow has run about 6–8% — moderate, and shares are available. A hard squeeze usually needs borrow to lock up and costs to spike past 50%. That is not happening here. The shorts are comfortable, not trapped. It takes a genuine surprise to force them.

Insiders are selling into the raise

Not a great look. After option exercises on 26 June (CEO Mastrangelo added 130,570 shares, among others), the team sold on 30 June: the CEO parted with 60,703 shares at $6.09 for tax withholding, and three other officers sold between roughly 8,800 and 35,300 shares each near $5.86–$5.87. Small relative to their holdings, but it is net selling into the offering, not buying the dip.

The Underwater Raise (The Whole Ballgame)

Eos needs cash to build. It is getting it in three pieces: the $75M Hudson Bay direct deal (13.7M shares plus 6.0M warrants at $5.481), the rights offering of up to 27.4M units at the same $5.481, and the DOE loan guarantee behind the factory. Fully subscribed, those two equity pieces raise up to roughly $225M — the fresh Eos stock that dilutes you. Add Hudson Bay's separate ~$125M going straight into FPUSA and the venture's total equity backing climbs further, but only the $225M is Eos dilution.

The mechanical problem. The rights let you buy stock at $5.481. The stock is $4.625. Why would you pay $5.481 for shares you can grab 16% cheaper on the open market? A rational holder mostly does not exercise. So the $150M rights raise risks falling short of target, and the $5.481 price becomes a wall: sellers who need to raise cash lean on it, and the stock struggles to push through until the overhang clears. New units keep landing on a 354.8M share base — 13.7M direct, 6.0M warrants, up to 27.4M rights units and their warrants. That is ongoing, low-double-digit dilution while the company still burns cash.
