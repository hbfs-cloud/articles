Business Overview

AMD designs high-performance processors and graphics chips and has them manufactured by third-party foundries. Four segments matter, but only one currently sets the share price.

Data Center is what sets the price. It generated $5.8 billion in Q1 2026, up 57% year on year, and is now the majority of revenue. It contains two very different businesses that investors routinely conflate. EPYC server CPUs, where AMD has taken share from Intel for eight consecutive years and has real pricing power. And Instinct AI accelerators — MI300X, MI325X, and the new MI400 series — where AMD is the challenger to NVIDIA and competes largely on price, memory capacity and bandwidth rather than on software ecosystem.

That distinction is the crux of the case. The CPU franchise is a durable share-gain story with defensible economics. The accelerator franchise is a land grab against an incumbent whose CUDA software stack remains the industry default. Today's price assumes both work. At its Advancing AI event, management unveiled the full MI400 line, the Helios rack-scale platform and an MI500 roadmap preview; the flagship MI455X is specified at 40 petaflops of FP4 compute, 432 GB of HBM4 memory and 19.6 TB/s of bandwidth.

Client (Ryzen PC processors), Gaming (console semi-custom and Radeon) and Embedded (the Xilinx FPGA franchise) make up the rest. They are cyclical, slower-growing, and matter mainly as a source of cash and as a cushion when the AI narrative wobbles.

Regime: recovery

AMD is a maximum-duration asset: 2.47 beta, no dividend, and a valuation that discounts cash flows many years out. The macro backdrop of the past week has been quietly hostile to exactly that profile. The 30-year Treasury closed at 5.275%, its highest since July 2007, and the 10-year at 4.745%. When the discount rate on long-dated cash flows rises, the stocks that lose most are those whose value sits furthest in the future — which is precisely a company at 107x EV/EBITDA. Note too that three Federal Reserve officials dissented in favour of a rate hike on 29 July, so the familiar reflex of buying weak macro data on the expectation of cuts is not currently reliable. That is the backdrop into which AMD reports.

Risk Analysis

Risk Profile: High

A high-beta, high-multiple asset reporting binary results in two sessions, in a broken short-term trend, with unanimous insider selling, a defensively positioned options chain, and a 160-million-share warrant struck at a cent sitting above the price. The balance sheet is clean; the share count is not. Nothing here threatens solvency. Everything here is about what you pay and when.

The share count is not clean: a 160M-share warrant sits above this price

High
  • On 6 October 2025 AMD issued OpenAI a warrant for up to 160,000,000 shares at a $0.01 exercise price, alongside an agreement to deploy 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs. That is roughly 10% of the 1.63B shares outstanding, struck at a cent.
  • Tranches vest against Instinct GPU purchase milestones — the first gigawatt for the first tranche, 6 gigawatts for the full amount — and against specified AMD share-price targets escalating to $600 for the final tranche. Exercisable through 5 October 2030.
  • The structure has a consequence worth spelling out: the dilution vests precisely if the bull case works. The better the stock does, the more of the 160 million shares are handed over at a cent.
  • On a fully diluted 1.791B share count, every per-share metric worsens by about 9%, and the enterprise value the market is really paying rises accordingly.
  • This is disclosed, commercially rational and arguably a good trade for AMD — locking in an anchor customer for a challenger accelerator. It is not a governance red flag. But a valuation that ignores it is understating what a buyer actually pays.

The +235% growth headline is a base-effect artefact

High
  • Consensus for Tuesday is $1.55 to $1.62 non-GAAP EPS against $0.48 a year ago. Nearly every headline will say growth of roughly +235%.
  • But Q2 2025 absorbed approximately $800M of inventory and related charges tied to US export controls on the Instinct MI308, and AMD disclosed that this reduced earnings per share by about $0.43.
  • A clean base is therefore roughly $0.91, not $0.48. Against $0.91, a $1.62 print is about +78% — excellent, and roughly one third of the number that will lead the coverage.
  • The same trap sits in the margin line. AMD stated non-GAAP gross margin would have been about 54% excluding the charge, against the 43% reported. A 54% print on Tuesday will be written up as eleven points of expansion when the underlying move is close to flat.
  • This is not an accusation of manipulation. It is a disclosed one-off sitting in a comparison base. But if you buy the headline growth rate, you are buying an accounting comparison, not a business inflection.

Valuation: the most expensive, the least profitable

High
  • 107.5x EV/EBITDA against NVIDIA at 28.6x, Marvell at 56.8x and Broadcom at 44.4x.
  • 34.5x forward earnings against NVIDIA at 15.6x, Broadcom at 20.0x and TSMC at 18.7x. AMD trades at more than twice NVIDIA's forward multiple.
  • Operating margin 14.4% against NVIDIA's 65.6%. Return on equity 8.1% against NVIDIA's 114.3%.

  • We do not publish a price target here, and the reason is worth stating. Run a discounted cash-flow with AMD's own beta of 2.469 and you get a 19.3% cost of capital; discount $6.7–8.6B of free cash flow at that rate against a conservative terminal growth cap and the model returns a value around 6% of the market capitalisation. That output is an artefact of applying a mature-company discipline to a hyper-growth company, and we would not put it in front of a reader as a target.
  • The useful version is the same arithmetic run backwards. To justify $776B at a 19.3% cost of capital, free cash flow has to compound at about 18% a year in perpetuity. Halve the beta to 1.5 and the requirement is still about 12.5% forever. Revenue is currently growing 37.8%, so the next few years clear that bar comfortably. Whether anything clears it in perpetuity is the actual question the price is asking.

NVIDIA's software moat is the structural risk

High
  • AMD competes on price, memory capacity and bandwidth. NVIDIA competes on CUDA, where a decade of developer tooling lives.
  • Hyperscalers have a strong commercial interest in funding a second source. That is a real tailwind, but it is a customer preference and preferences get repriced.

Risk Synthesis

Why downgrade the same company from B to C+ while its revenue accelerates? Because a grade is not a verdict on a business, it is a verdict on the risk and reward available to a buyer today. In February we graded AMD B at $207, with a forward multiple that left room. Since then the business improved and the share price tripled off its low. The four eliminatory criteria in our A-grade screen now read: guidance raised — pass; five consecutive earnings beats — fail, our verified history shows three beats and one marginal miss across four quarters; forward P/E under 35x — pass, at 34.45x, by half a point; price within 3% of its 20-day average — fail, at 5.52% below. Two of four fail, so the A grades are off the table by construction, and the composite lands at 50 out of 100. The facts that made the company more attractive are the same facts that made the entry less attractive. We grade the entry.
