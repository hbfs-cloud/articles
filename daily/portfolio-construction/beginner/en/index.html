How Many Stocks?

How Many Stocks Should You Own?

In Simple Terms

Think of your portfolio like a basket of eggs. If you put all your eggs in one basket and that basket drops, you lose everything. But if you have too many baskets, you cannot keep an eye on all of them. The sweet spot depends on how much money you have and how much time you can spend.

How Many Stocks for Your Budget?

Your BudgetIdeal Number% Per StockWhy
< $2,0003-520-33%Small budgets should stay focused. Trading fees eat into tiny positions.
$2,000 - $10,0005-812-20%The sweet spot for beginners. Enough to spread risk without losing track.
$10,000 - $50,0008-157-12%Real diversification across different industries and countries.
> $50,00015-254-7%Institutional-style portfolio. Beyond 25, adding stocks does not reduce risk further.

Key Takeaway: The 90% Rule

Academic research shows that 90% of the benefit of diversification is achieved with just 15-20 stocks from different industries. Beyond that, you are adding complexity without meaningfully reducing risk. With 30+ stocks, you are basically copying an index fund — so you might as well just buy an ETF instead (which is cheaper and easier).

ETFs & Automatic Investing: The Foundation
How to invest while you sleep (seriously)

In Simple Terms: What Is an ETF?

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) is like a pre-made basket of stocks you can buy in one click. Imagine going to a grocery store: instead of buying 500 individual items one by one, you buy a single "variety pack" that contains a tiny piece of each one. For example, an S&P 500 ETF owns shares in all 500 of America's largest companies. It is the simplest and most effective way to invest for 90% of people.

What Is DCA? (Dollar Cost Averaging)

In Simple Terms

DCA means investing a fixed amount of money on a regular schedule, no matter what the market is doing. Think of it like a gym membership for your money: you show up every month, rain or shine. Instead of trying to guess the "perfect" time to invest $6,000, you invest $500 every month for 12 months. When prices are low, your $500 buys more shares. When prices are high, you buy fewer. Over time, this averages out to a good price.

Key Takeaway: After 6 Months

Total invested: $1,800 — Current value: $1,905 — Gain: +5.8%
Your average purchase price was $472 even though the final price was $500. You took advantage of the dips in February and March to buy more shares at lower prices. This is the magic of DCA: market dips become your friend instead of your enemy.

But Wait — Is It Better to Invest Everything at Once?

Statistically, investing everything at once ("lump sum") beats DCA about 2 out of 3 times, because markets tend to go up over time. However, DCA is psychologically much easier — it prevents you from investing your entire savings right before a crash, and it stops you from panicking and selling at the bottom. For beginners, DCA is almost always the smarter choice.

How to Set Up Automatic Investing

Most brokers let you set up automatic recurring investments. Here is how:

  1. Pick your ETF (e.g., VOO or VTI)
  2. Set a schedule (e.g., every 1st of the month)
  3. Choose your amount (e.g., $200/month)
  4. Enable fractional shares (so you can invest exact dollar amounts)
  5. Forget about it — let it run automatically

On Fidelity, this is called "Automatic Investments." On Schwab, it is "Automatic Investing." On Robinhood, it is "Recurring Investments." All major brokers support this feature at no extra cost.

Different Ways to Trade
From day trading to buy-and-hold: which one fits your life?

In Simple Terms

Not everyone trades the same way. Some people stare at screens all day. Others check their portfolio once a month. The style you choose should match your available time, not the other way around. Think of it like exercise: you need a routine you can actually stick to.

Key Takeaway: The Winning Combo for Busy People

80% Buy & Hold (automatic ETF investing via DCA) + 20% Swing Trading (2-3 active positions you manage). The buy-and-hold portion builds your wealth on autopilot. The swing trading satisfies your desire to be active without requiring you to quit your job. They complement each other perfectly.

  • Monday: Often quieter. Good for reviewing your plan.
  • Tuesday-Wednesday: Most active days. Economic data often released.

How to Read a Stock Chart
The 4 things to check on any chart before investing (5 minutes is all you need)

In Simple Terms

A stock chart is like a weather forecast for a stock's price. It shows where the price has been and gives you clues about where it might be going. You do not need to be an expert — just learn these 4 things and you will be ahead of most beginners. Use a free tool like TradingView.com to follow along.

1

The Trend: Is the Stock Going Up or Down?

Look at the moving averages (the smooth lines on a chart). The two most important are the 50-day and 200-day moving averages. If the price is above both lines, the trend is up (bullish). If it is below both, the trend is down (bearish). Think of it like a river: you want to swim with the current, not against it. On TradingView, type "MA" in the indicators box and add 50 and 200.

2

The Volume: How Many People Are Trading?

Volume is shown as bars at the bottom of the chart. It tells you how many shares were traded. High volume + rising price = real, healthy move. Low volume + rising price = weak move that might not last. Think of volume like fuel: without fuel, the car (price) cannot keep going.

3

Support & Resistance: The Floor and the Ceiling

A support is a price level where the stock tends to stop falling and bounce back (like a floor). A resistance is a price level the stock has trouble breaking above (like a ceiling). When a stock finally breaks above resistance with high volume, that is often a great buy signal (called a "breakout").

4

RSI: Is the Stock Overbought or Oversold?

The RSI (Relative Strength Index) is a number from 0 to 100. Above 70 means the stock has gone up a lot recently and might pull back (overbought). Below 30 means it has dropped a lot and might bounce (oversold). Between 40-65 is the sweet spot for buying. Think of RSI like a speedometer: too fast and you might crash, too slow and you are stalling.

Key Takeaway: Your 5-Minute Chart Checklist

  • Is the price above the 50-day and 200-day moving averages? (Trend is UP)
  • Is volume increasing along with the price? (Move is REAL)
  • Is the stock near support (good entry) or near resistance (be careful)?
  • Is the RSI between 30 and 70? (Not overheated or crashing)

If all 4 check out, you have a healthy stock worth considering!

Important Terms Explained

Important Terms You Will See Everywhere

In Simple Terms

The investing world loves jargon. Here are the most common terms you will encounter, explained in plain English. Bookmark this section — you will come back to it.

TermWhat It MeansWhy You Should Care
BetaHow much a stock moves compared to the market. Beta of 1 = moves the same. Beta of 2 = moves twice as much.If the market drops 5% and your stock has a beta of 2, expect about a 10% drop. Higher beta = more risk but more potential reward.
Bull MarketWhen the market is going up (like a bull charging upward).Most stocks rise in a bull market. It is the best time to be invested.
Bear MarketWhen the market drops 20%+ from its peak (like a bear swiping downward).Do NOT panic-sell. Bear markets are temporary. Historically, they last about 9-14 months.
Meme StockA stock that goes viral on social media (like GameStop or AMC).Extremely risky. Can go up 300% then crash 90%. They are not investing — they are gambling. Stay away as a beginner.
Sector RotationMoney flows between industries depending on the economy. Boom = tech. Slowdown = utilities and healthcare.If one sector is crashing, another is likely rising. Diversification protects you.
Earnings ReportA company's quarterly "report card" showing revenue and profit.Stocks can jump or drop 5-20% overnight on earnings. Know when your stocks report!
Short SellingBetting that a stock will go DOWN (borrowing shares, selling them, hoping to buy back cheaper).This is advanced and risky. As a beginner, just know it exists. When many people short a stock, a "short squeeze" can send prices rocketing up.

Common Mistakes
!
10 Common Mistakes That Cost Beginners Money
Every mistake here has cost millions of real people thousands of dollars

In Simple Terms

The stock market is not hard to understand, but it is easy to make emotional mistakes. These are the traps that catch almost every new investor at some point. Read them now so you recognize them when they happen to you.

No Stop-Loss

"It will come back up!" — No, sometimes it does not. Some stocks dropped 90% and never recovered (SPCE, WISH). A stop-loss is like a seatbelt: set it at -8% to -10% and it automatically sells before a small loss becomes a disaster.

FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out)

Buying a stock AFTER it has already gone up 50% because "everyone is talking about it." By the time it is in the news, the early buyers are selling to YOU. If you missed the train, wait for the next one.

Averaging Down Blindly

"It dropped 30%, so I will buy more!" — if the company's fundamentals are getting worse, you are just doubling down on a losing bet. Only buy more if your original reason for buying is still valid.

Over-Trading

Buying and selling 10 times a week = fees + taxes + stress + bad decisions. Studies show the most active traders underperform by 6.5% per year. Less is more.

Ignoring the Economic Calendar

Opening a position on the day of an inflation report (CPI) or a Federal Reserve meeting (FOMC) is like playing roulette. These events can move the market 3% in 5 minutes.

Following Social Media "Gurus"

Many stock influencers on YouTube and TikTok are paid to promote the stocks they talk about. Always do your own research. If it sounds too good to be true, it usually is.

No Diversification

Putting 100% in one sector (e.g., all tech stocks in 2022) = -33% when that sector crashes. Spread your money across at least 3-4 different industries.

Investing Money You Cannot Lose

Your rent money, emergency fund, and debt payments are OFF LIMITS. Only invest money you could lose without it affecting your daily life. Have 3-6 months of expenses saved FIRST.

Ignoring Taxes

In the US, short-term capital gains (held < 1 year) are taxed as regular income (up to 37%). Long-term gains (held > 1 year) are taxed at only 0-20%. Use tax-advantaged accounts (Roth IRA, 401k) whenever possible! In the UK, use your ISA. In France, use your PEA.

Revenge Trading

Losing $500 and immediately trying to "win it back" by making a bigger trade is like a gambler chasing losses at a casino. After a loss, take at least 24 hours off before making another trade.

Key Takeaway: The Golden Rules

  • Always use a stop-loss (your seatbelt)
  • Never invest money you need for bills or emergencies
  • Do not chase stocks that already went up 50%
  • Less trading = better returns (counterintuitive but proven)
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts first
