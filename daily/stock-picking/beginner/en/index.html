What Is Stock Picking?

Imagine you're at a farmer's market...

You have $20 to spend and there are 50 different fruit stands. You could buy a tiny bit from every stand (that's like buying an index fund), or you could carefully choose the 3 best stands — the ones with the freshest, ripest fruit at the best price. Stock picking is exactly that: instead of buying every company in the market, you handpick the ones you believe will grow the most.

Stock picking means choosing individual stocks (shares of companies) instead of buying an entire index (like an S&P 500 ETF that holds 500 companies at once). It takes a bit more effort, but it can be more rewarding if you follow a clear method.

Why not just buy everything?

Buying an index fund is a great starting point and many experts recommend it. But stock picking lets you aim higher. Think of it like this: if the whole class gets a B+ average, stock picking is trying to find the students who will get an A+. The trade-off? Some of those "A+ students" might actually flunk — that's the risk. This guide teaches you how to reduce that risk.

This guide presents 3 beginner-friendly methods, from easiest to most active, that you can follow in 15-30 minutes per week using free websites and a simple spreadsheet.

What Kind of Investor Are You?

Think about roller coasters...

Some people love huge, terrifying roller coasters. Others prefer the gentle rides. And some are happy with something in between. Investing is the same way — your personality determines which kind of "ride" is right for you. There are no wrong answers! The important thing is to be honest with yourself.

Before choosing a method, you need to know what type of investor you are. Your profile determines which methods are best for you and how much risk you can handle.

Method 1 — The Easiest
1
Index Picking
Choose the best stocks from a well-known list

Imagine your class has 30 students...

The teacher ranks everyone by their math grades. Instead of giving a prize to the whole class, you give prizes only to the top 5 students. Index Picking works the same way: instead of buying all 500 stocks in the S&P 500, you only keep the 5-15 best performers. The index already filtered out bad companies for you — you're just picking the cream of the crop!

What is an "index"?

An index is simply a list of companies grouped together. The S&P 500 is a list of the 500 biggest US companies. The Dow Jones has just 30 of the biggest and most important. Think of indices as "playlists" — someone already curated the best songs (companies) for you!

How To Do It (3 Simple Steps)

1

Pick a well-known index

Start with the S&P 500 (500 biggest US companies) or the Dow Jones (30 biggest US companies). These indices already filter out weak companies for you.

2

Filter with 3 easy checks

Check 1: Is the company's profit growing over the last 2 years? Check 2: Does it pay a dividend above 2%? Check 3: Is the company's debt reasonable (not drowning in loans)? If all 3 are yes — that's a winner!

3

Buy 5-10 of the best and hold

Split your money equally between them. Check in once every 3 months (after companies report earnings) and replace any that no longer pass the 3 checks. That's it!

Method 2 — Follow the Wave
2
Momentum Investing
Buy what's already going up — ride the wave

Think about a snowball rolling downhill...

A small snowball at the top of a hill starts rolling. As it goes down, it gets bigger and faster. Stocks work the same way: a stock that has been going up for several weeks tends to keep going up for a while. Momentum investing means hopping on the snowball while it's still growing — and jumping off before it hits a wall at the bottom!

What is "momentum"?

Momentum is the idea that what's been going up tends to keep going up (at least for a while). It's been proven by over 200 years of stock market data! The key is knowing when to get on the ride and when to get off. That's what the steps below teach you.

How To Do It (3 Simple Steps)

1

Find stocks that went up a lot in the last 3 months

Use a free screener like Finviz.com or TradingView. Look for stocks that gained more than +15% over 3 months. Not 1 week — that's too short. Three months shows real momentum.

2

Check: Is the price ABOVE its 50-day average price?

The 50-day average price (sometimes called "SMA 50" or "50-day moving average") is simply the average closing price over the last 50 trading days. If the stock's current price is ABOVE this line, it means the trend is still going up. If it's BELOW — the momentum might be fading, so skip it.

3

Set an automatic safety order (stop-loss) at -8%

This is the most important rule. Before you buy, decide: "If this stock drops 8% below my purchase price, I will sell automatically." No exceptions. No hoping it'll come back. This one rule protects your money from big losses. Think of it as a seatbelt — you always wear it, even if you never crash.

Why the safety order matters so much

Imagine you buy a stock at $100. Without a safety order, if it drops to $50 (that's -50%), you'd need a +100% gain just to get back to even! But with a -8% safety order, the worst that happens is you sell at $92. The math changes completely: you only need +8.7% to recover. Protecting your money is more important than making money.

Warning: Don't chase stocks that already skyrocketed!

If a stock already went up +50% or more in one month, it's probably too late. That's like trying to jump on a train that's already leaving the station at full speed — dangerous! The best time to buy a momentum stock is early in the move (after 2-4 weeks of steady climbing), not at the end (after 2-3 months of explosive gains). Patience pays.

Method 3 — Find Bargains
3
Value Investing
Buy stocks that are "on sale" compared to their true worth

Imagine a bicycle that's worth $200...

One day, the store puts it on sale for $120. That's a great deal! You know the bike is worth $200, so you buy it at $120 and you're happy. Value investing works the same way: you look for companies whose stock price is cheaper than what the company is actually worth. You buy "on sale" and wait for the market to realize the true value.

What is the Price-to-Earnings ratio (P/E)?

The P/E ratio tells you how many years of profits you're paying for. If a company earns $10 per share and the stock price is $100, the P/E is 10 (you're paying for 10 years of profits). A lower P/E usually means the stock is cheaper. If similar companies have a P/E of 20 but this one has a P/E of 10, it might be "on sale" — or there might be a good reason it's cheap! That's why Step 2 below is so important.

How To Do It (3 Simple Steps)

1

Find stocks with a low P/E compared to their peers

The Price-to-Earnings ratio (how many years of profits you're paying for) should be lower than the average for similar companies. If steel companies average a P/E of 10 but one has a P/E of 5, that could be a bargain! Use Yahoo Finance to check.

2

Make sure it's a real bargain, not a trap!

A cheap stock can be cheap for a bad reason (the company is failing!). Check these 3 things: (a) Profits are stable or growing. (b) Debt is not too high. (c) They still pay dividends. If all 3 are true, it's likely a real bargain. If not, the stock is cheap because it deserves to be — stay away!

3

Buy and be patient (6-18 months)

Value investing is the slow-cooker method. It takes time for the market to notice a bargain. Warren Buffett (the most famous investor in history) sometimes waits years! For you, aim for 6 to 18 months. Don't panic if the price doesn't move right away.

Watch Out!

6 Common Beginner Mistakes

Everyone makes mistakes — that's how we learn!

Even the best investors in the world made mistakes when they started. The difference is they learned from them quickly. Here are the most common traps beginners fall into, so you can avoid them from day one.

1. Falling in love with a stock

You buy a stock, and even when everything goes wrong, you refuse to sell because you "believe in it." Love is for people, not stocks!

Fix: Set rules BEFORE you buy. Write them down. Follow them no matter how you feel. If the safety order triggers, sell. Period.

2. FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out)

A stock jumped +60% and everyone on social media is talking about it. You rush in at the top... and it crashes.

Fix: If you missed the move, let it go. There will always be another opportunity. The stock market is open every day — you don't need to catch every wave.

3. Putting all your eggs in one basket

You invest all your money in a single stock. If it goes down 50%, you've lost half your savings.

Fix: Never put more than 10-20% of your money in any single stock. Own at least 5-10 different stocks across different industries.

4. Checking your portfolio every 5 minutes

You obsess over every tiny price change. This causes stress and leads to panic selling on small dips.

Fix: Set a schedule. Check once a day or even once a week. If you set a safety order, your downside is already protected — no need to stare at the screen.

5. No safety order (stop-loss)

"It'll come back up" — famous last words. Without a safety order, a -20% loss can become -50% or worse.

Fix: ALWAYS set a safety order. -8% for momentum stocks, -10 to 15% for value stocks. This is non-negotiable.

6. Following "hot tips" from strangers

Someone on Reddit or TikTok says "This stock will 10x!" — so you buy without doing any research.

Fix: Always do your own research. Use the 3 methods in this guide. If a "tip" doesn't pass your method's checklist, ignore it — no matter how exciting it sounds.

The Secret Most People Don't Understand

The method matters less than the discipline. An average investor who follows their rules every single time will beat a brilliant investor who changes their mind every day. Pick a method, write down your rules on paper, and follow them no matter what. It's boring. And that's exactly why it works. The stock market rewards patience and consistency, not excitement and cleverness.

Learn the Words

Glossary — Stock Market Terms in Plain English

Don't worry about memorizing everything!

Come back to this glossary whenever you see a word you don't understand. Over time, these terms will become second nature. The stock market has its own language, but once you learn the basics, it's not as scary as it sounds.

Stock / Share

A tiny piece of ownership in a company. If a company has 1,000 shares and you own 1, you own 0.1% of the company.

Index

A list of companies grouped together for tracking. Example: the S&P 500 tracks the 500 biggest US companies. Think of it as a "playlist" of stocks.

ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)

A basket of stocks you can buy as one unit. An S&P 500 ETF lets you buy a piece of all 500 companies at once — like a fruit salad vs. buying each fruit separately.

P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings)

How many years of profits you're paying for. Stock price / annual earnings per share = P/E. A P/E of 15 means "you're paying for 15 years of current profits." Lower = potentially cheaper.

Dividend

Cash that a company pays to its shareholders (you!) from its profits, usually every 3 months. Like getting a small bonus for owning the stock.

50-Day Average Price (SMA 50)

The average closing price of a stock over the last 50 trading days. If the current price is above this line, the trend is generally "up." If below, the trend is "down."

Momentum

The tendency of a stock that's been going up to keep going up (and vice versa). Like a snowball rolling downhill — it builds speed.

Stop-Loss (Safety Order)

An automatic sell order that triggers if a stock drops to a certain price. If you buy at $100 and set a stop-loss at $92, the stock automatically sells if it drops to $92. Your seatbelt in investing.

Market Cap (Market Capitalization)

The total value of all a company's shares. Stock price x number of shares = market cap. It tells you how "big" a company is. Apple's market cap is ~$3 trillion.

Large-Cap / Mid-Cap / Small-Cap

Size categories: Large-Cap > $10B (giant companies), Mid-Cap $2-10B (growing companies), Small-Cap < $2B (small, risky but potentially high-reward companies).

Volume

How many shares of a stock are bought and sold in a day. High volume = lots of interest. Low volume = few buyers/sellers. Volume is the "fuel" behind price moves.

Earnings

The profits a company reports, usually every 3 months (quarterly). "Earnings beat" = profits were better than expected. "Earnings miss" = profits were worse than expected.

RSI (Overbought/Oversold Indicator)

A number from 0 to 100 that shows if a stock has been bought too much (above 70 = "overbought") or sold too much (below 30 = "oversold"). Between 50-70 = healthy upward momentum.

Bull Market / Bear Market

Bull market = prices are going up (think of a bull charging upward with its horns). Bear market = prices are going down (think of a bear swiping downward with its paw). Fun way to remember!

Diversification

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket." Owning stocks in different companies and industries so that if one goes down, the others can balance it out.

FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out)

The panicky feeling of seeing others make money on a stock and rushing to buy without thinking. One of the biggest causes of beginner losses. Stay calm!
