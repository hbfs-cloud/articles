A beat is a comparison, and a comparison is only honest when both sides were fixed before the news landed.

So build the timeline first. Official release time. EDGAR acceptance. The conference call. The 10-Q or 10-K. Then the publication time of every analyst note. Compare reported results only with estimates frozen before the release. A screen that shows a raised price target sitting next to last night's earnings has quietly folded a reaction into a forecast.

![Between the event and your screen, time passes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/information_clock.png)

The filings run on staggered clocks. Companies commonly furnish earnings information under Item 2.02 of Form 8-K, and the instructions generally allow four business days after the public announcement, so the 8-K usually follows the press release rather than creating it. Item 2.02 information is normally "furnished", not "filed", unless the company specifies otherwise. Different word, different legal weight. The EDGAR acceptance timestamp and the attached exhibit are what tell you exactly what entered the public record, and when.

The quarterly report is slower and heavier. Form 10-Q covers the first three fiscal quarters and carries financial statements, notes, management discussion and other required disclosures. Form 10-K is the annual version, with audited statements, and it takes longer still. Both usually arrive weeks after the earnings release that moved the price, which is precisely why the uncomfortable details tend to surface in the notes rather than in the announcement.

Then the adjusted figures. For every non-GAAP number, find the most directly comparable GAAP measure and the reconciliation. SEC guidance requires that comparable GAAP presentation and warns against giving the adjusted measure greater prominence. Two companies can print the same label, "adjusted earnings", while excluding different costs from it. Compare the definitions before comparing the values.

Analyst research sits outside the issuer's sequence entirely. A rating or price target is an opinion, produced by a person or firm with its own methodology and its own possible conflicts, and the population of ratings is visibly lopsided: in a 2024 snapshot of sell-side coverage, buy-type calls ran around 55% of ratings while sell-type calls came to roughly 7%. That skew is a structural fact about the industry, not a market forecast. Preserve the author, the firm, the old view, the new view and the timestamp. Consensus figures are vendor-built too, so save the cutoff time and the contributor rules.

The worked case is deliberately dull. A company releases results after the close. An analyst raises a target the next morning. By lunchtime, a data screen displays the higher target beside the reported earnings, and the two look like one story. Chronology settles it. The pre-release estimate belongs in the surprise calculation. The morning note belongs in post-event evidence, filed nowhere near it.

Earnings chronology:

- Freeze estimates and guidance before publication.
- Save the issuer release and the EDGAR accession.
- Read GAAP results before the adjusted reconciliation.
- Time-stamp the call, the filing and every analyst revision.
- Keep pre-event forecasts apart from post-event reactions.

What ordering cannot do is explain why the price moved, or make management and analyst judgments objective. The SEC sets disclosure rules; it does not vouch for a company's filing. An analyst's conflicts do not automatically invalidate the research. And an earnings surprise cannot separate the effect of guidance from positioning or valuation. Those explanations stay conditional, and they stay labelled as yours.
