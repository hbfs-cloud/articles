The plan gets written the night before, while nothing is happening.

The morning after a bad open is the worst possible moment to invent one. The first fifteen minutes are stop orders firing in a chain, algorithms leaning on the move, and the thinnest liquidity of the day. A lot of people sell the low of the session and call it discipline. No rule fits every case, which is why the thinking has to happen in advance: waiting is expensive when the cash on the balance sheet turns out not to exist, and firing an unbounded market order into a disorderly auction is expensive too.

![Seal the evidence, not the story](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/evidence_chain.png)

Six mornings, same starting point, opposite endings.

Apple opened about 9.9% lower on 2 January 2019 after Tim Cook cut his company's quarterly guidance. iPhone sales in China were weak; services were still growing above 20% a year. The stock traded around $142 after the gap and above $230 by the end of 2024. Meta gapped 26.4% lower on 3 February 2022 on the first fall in users plus heavy metaverse spending, bottomed at $88 that November, and was above $530 in 2024. Bitcoin never gapped at all after the FTX collapse — it bled down to $15,500 and traded above $100,000 by December 2024.

Now the other half. Wirecard opened 62% lower on 18 June 2020 when €1.9bn of reported cash was found not to exist; it lost 99.8% within ten sessions and filed for insolvency seven days later. Silicon Valley Bank gapped 60% on 9 March 2023 after announcing a $1.8bn bond loss and a $2.25bn capital raise; depositors pulled $42bn the next day and the FDIC seized the bank on 10 March. Roughly 48 hours, start to finish. Terra's LUNA fell from $80 to $0.0001 in five days, about $40bn, because the mechanism meant to hold its stablecoin at a dollar was minting the token instead.

The size of the first print sorted none of that. LUNA never gapped once. Neither did bitcoin. <mark>What separated the two groups was whether the cash, the network or the customers were still there the next morning — a question about evidence, not about a percentage on a screen.</mark>

Which is why the real work happens before the close.

### The pre-close pass

1. Check tonight's calendar position by position: earnings, a regulator's decision, a central bank meeting.
1. Cap concentration. More than 10% in one stock, or 30% in one sector, is a single bet wearing several names. Two companies in one industry plus an ETF that holds both is three lines and one bet; each can pass its own loss budget while the group breaches yours.
1. Keep leverage under two times for a stock book, and hold 10 to 15% in cash. That cash is not idle; it is what lets you act the morning after.
1. Stress the whole book at once. If everything you own gapped 10% against you tonight, what is the loss, and can the account absorb it? If not, the sizes are wrong tonight, not tomorrow.
1. Write the response per position, in advance: if it opens X% down, I do Y.

If the gap comes, work in order. Confirm account access, positions, resting orders, margin. Verify the catalyst at the issuer or the regulator, and check for a halt or an auction. Cancel anything priced off yesterday. Rank by threat to solvency first, thesis second. Document the evidence and fills before you place anything new.

The map does not foresee an exchange outage, a broker raising house margin overnight, or correlations that snap. Hedges miss. Survival comes from carrying exposure that stays tolerable when the fill is worse and slower than you planned.
