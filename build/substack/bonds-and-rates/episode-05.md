One rate view, three vehicles, three different sets of risk. Choose by what you intend to own, not by what is easiest to buy.

A cash bond gives you contractual payments and a maturity date. If that date matters to your plan, that is worth something. Default, a call or an early sale can still change the outcome.

A fund gives you a portfolio that never matures. Shares trade all day at a market price that can sit above or below net asset value. What the ticker actually represents comes down to duration, credit quality, fees, holdings and index rules. A core aggregate fund charging 0.03% a year with duration near 6 behaves nothing like a long Treasury fund with duration near 17, even though both say "bonds" on the label.

![Many tickers can be one bet](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/exposure_lookthrough.png)

Futures are not a purchase at all, and the CFTC is precise about why: margin is collateral, a performance bond, not a partial payment for the underlying. One ten-year Treasury note contract covers $100,000 of notional exposure on initial margin of roughly $2,200. That is about forty-five times leverage. A ten-basis-point day in the ten-year, routine around a policy decision, is worth roughly $780 on that contract, close to a third of the margin posted. Losses can exceed the deposit, margin calls arrive quickly, and contracts expire and have to be rolled.

Write an instrument sheet before placing the order. State the precise factor you expect to move: the level of government yields, the shape of the curve, inflation compensation, or corporate credit. Then record the vehicle's duration, benchmark, credit exposure, liquidity, expenses, margin treatment and expiry, plus your intended holding period and how you plan to exit.

The mismatch case is common and quiet. Suppose the thesis is government rates, expressed through a corporate bond fund. Treasury yields do exactly what you predicted, corporate spreads widen at the same time, and the rate gain gets eaten. A broad aggregate fund would have added mortgage prepayment risk on top of that. The position is not defective. It simply owns more variables than the thesis named, and the sheet is where you notice before the money goes in.

Pretrade questions:

1. What precise factor is expected to move?
1. Which unwanted factors ride along with this vehicle?
1. Can the position be exited under realistic liquidity, not screen liquidity?
1. Are margin, roll or premium-to-NAV risks present?
1. Does the prospectus or contract specification match the holding period?

None of these wrappers is inherently safer than the others. Owning bonds directly can leave you concentrated and unable to sell at any sensible price. A fund can lose money through rates, credit and prepayments at once. Futures can lose it faster than either, and the wrapper does not turn risky bonds into safe ones.

No instrument isolates a macro view perfectly. Cash bonds carry issuer and liquidity terms. Funds carry portfolio construction and market-price risk. Futures carry basis, margin and expiry. <mark>The right choice is the one whose residual risks you have identified, sized and agreed to monitor, not the one with the most convenient ticker.</mark>
