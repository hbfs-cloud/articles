*Part 1 of 6 in Bonds and Rates for Equity Traders.*

Lend $10,000 at 4% for ten years. You collect $400 a year, then your $10,000 comes back. That is a bond. The $10,000 is the principal, the 4% is the coupon, the ten years is the maturity, and if you paid exactly face value your yield is 4% too.

Most US bonds are issued in $1,000 pieces. A 5% coupon on $1,000 pays $50 a year, usually as two $25 payments. Buy that same bond in the market at $1,100 and the arithmetic shifts: $50 on $1,100 is 4.55%, and at maturity you get $1,000 back, not $1,100. Yield to maturity absorbs that loss, so it comes in lower still. Same bond, three different numbers depending on which one the screen decided to show you.

![The three shapes a yield curve takes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/yield_curve_shapes.png)

A bondholder is a creditor, not an owner. No vote, no share of profits, no residual claim. In a bankruptcy, creditors are paid before shareholders, and the queue itself has an order: secured debt, then senior unsecured, then subordinated, then preferred, then common. For an equity trader that ordering is the whole point of the exercise. Bondholders think about priority and recovery first; shareholders own whatever is left after the liabilities.

Treasury securities come in five shapes. Bills run four weeks to a year and pay no coupon at all: you pay $980, you receive $1,000, and the $20 gap is your interest. Notes run two to ten years and bonds twenty to thirty, both with fixed semiannual coupons. TIPS adjust the principal with the specified consumer-price index. Floating-rate notes reset from a stated index and spread. Identical credit, wildly different behaviour when rates move or inflation surprises.

Corporate debt adds contract terms on top. Secured or unsecured, senior or subordinated, callable or not. A call lets the issuer repay early, and issuers call bonds when it suits them, which tends to be when it suits the holder least.

So run a contract audit before you compare anything. Take the identifier, the CUSIP. Pull the prospectus. Write down maturity, coupon terms and the call schedule. Then name the yield you were quoted: current yield, yield to maturity, yield to call, or yield to worst. Two bonds showing 5.2% over eight years, one callable in two, are not the same instrument, and only yield to worst says so out loud.

Then look at trades, not quotes. Since 2002, FINRA's TRACE has required dealers to report eligible bond trades within 15 minutes, with price, yield, time and size. Before that, a retail buyer had no way to judge whether a dealer's price was reasonable.

Contract check:

- Confirm issuer, identifier, maturity and payment currency.
- Separate fixed, floating, inflation-linked and zero-coupon cash flows.
- Read seniority, collateral, call and conversion terms.
- Name the exact yield measure printed on the quote.
- Check trading activity, fees and accrued interest before dealing.

One limitation sits under all of it: yield to maturity cannot guarantee what you realize. It assumes every contractual payment arrives and embeds a reinvestment assumption for the coupons. Taxes, costs, a default, a call or an early sale can each change what you actually earn. And a bond that barely trades can display a price nobody will give you.
