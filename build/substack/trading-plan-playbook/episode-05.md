You cannot follow two thousand stocks. You can follow twenty or thirty properly. That gap is the entire argument for a watchlist, and it is why a name earns its slot the day before you need it, never in the minute the price starts moving.

A symbol goes on the list only when it passes your eligibility rules and carries four written fields: context, trigger, invalidation, expiry. A list of interesting tickers is not preparation. No decision attached, no slot.

![A fact is not a decision, and a decision is not an order](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/decision_flow.png)

Liquidity comes first, before any pattern. FINRA frames liquidity risk as the difficulty of cashing out when you need to. The usual measure is average daily volume in money terms — average price times average volume over the last 20 to 50 sessions. The rough test: does your position exceed about 1% of that daily flow? A $5,000 position inside a $50 million ADV is 0.01% of the tape; you get in and out without friction. The same $5,000 inside a $200,000 ADV is 2.5%, and now your own order is what moves the price. The risk you computed becomes fiction. Liquidity is also not a permanent label, so check it again right before you send the order.

Screening turns your edge into filters. Three to five, aligned with one setup. Fifteen criteria returns almost nothing, and each extra filter fits the past a little tighter. The screen never decides; it shortlists.

For individual companies, anchor event research in the filings themselves. Investor.gov treats Forms 10-K and 10-Q as the periodic reports and Form 8-K as the place current material news lands. Link the document you actually read, with the time it was available. A headline written afterwards is not contemporaneous evidence.

A candidate entered on Monday looks like this:

| Element | What it must specify |
|---|---|
| Context | consolidating after a filed company update; the update alone proves nothing. |
| Trigger | a trade above the recorded range boundary while the spread stays inside your stated tolerance. |
| Invalidation | a separate level that kills the chart thesis. |
| Expiry | gone after Thursday's close, or sooner if a new filing changes the premise. |
| Alert | set 0.3 to 0.5 of one daily ATR ahead of the trigger level, so the phone buzzes while you still have time to open the chart. |

An alert sitting exactly on the level arrives late — the move is already underway when you look.

Tuesday the stock gaps straight through the trigger before you can evaluate the order. The trigger was never permission to buy at any price. Apply the order and risk rules again; if the new entry breaks them, the correct action is no trade. Write that non-trade down, or your later review will only count the convenient signals.

Maintenance is a rhythm, not a project. Ten to fifteen minutes each evening on triggers hit and tomorrow's alerts. Thirty to forty-five minutes at the weekend to recompute levels and prune. Then:

- Remove expired, ineligible, and invalidated names.
- Update levels only by the method your plan states.
- Keep skipped triggers and the reason you skipped them.

Lists inflate. Everyone adds; nobody removes. Cap yours around 20 to 25 names and enforce one-in-one-out. Fifteen current names beat fifty half-stale ones, and stale annotations are worse than none — they feel like preparation while you act on a context that stopped existing two months ago.

**Limitation:** discipline does not make your candidates representative or predictive. Screening one theme concentrates you into one bet: ten semiconductor names triggering the same day is one sector position, not ten ideas. New disclosures, halts, or a sudden liquidity change can invalidate the work before you execute. Recheck the evidence; the list is not authorization.
