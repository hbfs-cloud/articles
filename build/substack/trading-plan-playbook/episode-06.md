The plan is written. Most people fail here anyway, not for want of rules but for want of a habit that
carries them out. Fifteen to twenty-five minutes in the evening, when the market is shut, is enough for a
swing trader. An hour-long intraday ritual is the version people abandon in three weeks.

Four states, always in this order: prepare, watch, execute, record.

![Every order ends reconciled](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/schematics/order_state_machine.png)

**Prepare**, away from the noise. Review the list. Mark the names sitting within 2% of their trigger, since
those are the only ones that can act tomorrow. Check the calendar. Most swing plans refuse a new entry when
results are due within five sessions, because an earnings gap walks straight over a stop. Then write the
order out in full: entry, stop, target, size, expiry.

**Watch** means watching for one named event, not for excitement. You update what the plan permits, such as
the current spread or whether the trigger actually happened. A price moving fast is not permission to
invent a new setup at 3 p.m.

**Execute** turns the note into a broker ticket, with the entry, the stop and the target sent together as a
linked order. <mark>A stop you intend to place later is a stop that does not exist.</mark> Investor.gov notes that a
market order generally favours execution over a guaranteed price, while a limit order controls price and
may not execute at all; routing and movement affect the fill. Pick the trade-off deliberately, not out of
habit.

Take a prepared ticket. Buy-stop entry at €152.40, invalidation at €148.90, first target at €159.80. Risk
per share is €3.50. On capital of €20,000 with 2.5% allowed per trade, the budget is €500, so €500 divided
by €3.50 gives 142 shares after rounding down, and the real exposure is 142 × €3.50, about €497. Reward
against risk is €7.40 over €3.50, roughly 2.1 to one. If the price has already run past €152.40 when you go
to place the order, recompute: when the ratio drops below the plan's floor, you skip it. Chasing a move
already underway is the fastest way to spend an edge.

**Record** immediately, at entry and at exit, while the detail is intact. Keep submission times, fills,
cancelled and rejected orders, fees, and the reason for every manual intervention. Investor.gov advises
understanding transaction and account fees; a commission-free label does not mean execution was free. Two
extra fields repay the effort: the worst unrealised loss and the best unrealised gain during the trade. On
the ticket above, price dipped to €150.10, then ran to €161.20 before you sold at €159.80. The dip never
came near €148.90. The run says the market offered €8.80 and you took €7.40. One trade proves nothing;
thirty of these will tune your stops and targets better than any opinion.

![What the trade offered, what you took](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hbfs-cloud/articles/main/substack-assets/examples/trading-plan-playbook_episode-06.png)

Run this compact check at the ticket:

1. Setup still valid, source information still current?
1. Trigger observed, not anticipated?
1. Order type, duration and session correct?
1. Size inside the trade, liquidity and portfolio caps?
1. Protective instruction accepted and visible on screen?
1. Actual fill and any exception saved?

**Limitation:** a routine cannot stop a platform failing, an order being rejected, data arriving late, a
halt, or a fill outside the expected range. Automation adds its own configuration and connectivity risks.
When what you see stops matching the plan, standing aside or getting out can be the right answer. Forcing
the original sequence to completion is not discipline.

The review question is narrow: did you follow the version of the plan that existed before the decision?
Profit does not excuse a breach, and a loss taken by the book does not condemn the method.
