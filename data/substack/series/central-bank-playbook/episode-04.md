*Part 4 of 6 in The Central Bank Playbook.*

In December 2021 the median FOMC participant expected the policy rate to finish 2022 at 0.75%. It finished at 4.50%. Nobody lied. The dot plot is a set of individual guesses made on one afternoon under one set of assumptions, and the assumptions changed. Treat it as a snapshot, never a promise, and half the mistakes people make on Fed day disappear.

An FOMC day arrives as five documents, spread over weeks, each with a different job.

The statement lands at 14:00 ET. It runs 300 to 400 words and every one of them was negotiated. The change from the previous statement is the signal, so read the two side by side rather than reading the new one alone. The implementation note comes with it and carries the actual operating settings — this is where the plumbing from part two shows up. Four of the eight annual meetings — March, June, September and December — also publish the Summary of Economic Projections: growth, unemployment, inflation, and the dots. Nineteen participants submit, so the median is the tenth dot. How tightly the dots cluster tells you about agreement; the shift in the median since the last round tells you about direction.

The press conference starts at 14:30 ET and is a separate event. Reporters ask questions the statement did not answer, and the answers routinely reverse the first move. The minutes arrive three weeks later, ten to fifteen pages, describing a meeting that has since been overtaken by new data. They are useful for understanding how the committee argues, not for guessing the next decision.

The minutes even have a counting vocabulary worth learning, because it is deliberate. "A couple" means two. "A few" is two or three. "Some" is three or four. "Several" is five to eight. "Many" is nine to twelve. "Most" is thirteen to sixteen. When "a few participants favoured a cut" becomes "several participants favoured a cut", the dovish camp grew, and no rate had to change for that to be news.

So work an FOMC day like this.

- Build the folder from the Fed's own meeting calendar, not a third-party one.
- Save the statement and the implementation note separately, at release.
- Diff the statement against its immediate predecessor before reading commentary.
- Log press-conference observations with their own timestamps.
- Read the minutes against what was known on the meeting date, not today.
- Label projections as individual conditional submissions every single time.

One more piece of timing. For the ten days before each meeting, officials are barred from public comment on policy, so the last speech before that blackout carries extra weight and the week itself tends to go quiet.

What actually happens to prices? In the hour after a statement the S&P 500 typically moves half a percent to one percent, and one to one and a half on projection days. Roughly 40% of the time the first hour partly unwinds the next day. Direction is close to unforecastable; elevated volatility is not.

**Limitation:** wording changes can reflect drafting rather than a real shift in how the committee reacts to data. Press-conference answers are shaped by the question asked. Reading in release order stops you from importing later information into an earlier document; it cannot assign a cause to any particular price move.
