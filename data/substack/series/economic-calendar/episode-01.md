*Part 1 of 6 in Read the Economic Calendar.*

Before each trading session, mark the official release time, the publishing agency and the exact data vintage for every scheduled event that could affect your positions. If any of those details is unclear, treat the event as unverified. The calendar's first job is exposure control, not prediction.

An economic release has several labels that aggregators often compress into one row. The reference period says when the measured activity occurred. The publication date says when the information becomes public. The estimate label tells you whether the figure is an initial estimate or a revision. The source identifies who produced it. Confusing any pair can turn old information into apparent news.

Use the agency calendar as the final check. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes schedules for labor and price releases. The Bureau of Economic Analysis maintains dates for national accounts and personal income data. The Federal Reserve lists FOMC meetings and links to meeting documents. A third-party calendar may be convenient for scanning, but its impact badge and consensus field are the provider's classifications, not official economic data.

Suppose an app shows “GDP” on Thursday. Do not stop at the label. Open the BEA schedule and identify the covered quarter and estimate round. If it is a later estimate, retrieve the preceding release and note what can be revised. Your premarket note should read “GDP revision” rather than “new quarter” if that is what the official page says. That small distinction changes the question from “How fast did activity grow?” to “What changed in the estimate?”

**Pre-session checklist:** A usable event note contains four lines:

- Official release name and agency.
- Publication time in your trading time zone.
- Reference period and estimate or revision status.
- Exposure decision: hold, reduce, hedge or remain flat.

Keep “prior,” “expected” and “actual” in separate fields. The prior may later be revised. An expected value is normally a private forecast or survey, not a number endorsed by the statistical agency. The actual is only known when the agency publishes it. Market prices can still move when the actual matches a displayed consensus because positioning, revisions and details inside the release also matter.

This method has a clear limit. Official schedules can change, publication can be delayed, and unscheduled policy or geopolitical news will not appear on an economic calendar. A verified event list reduces avoidable timing errors; it does not forecast direction or volatility.

Sources: [BLS release calendar](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/), [BEA release schedule](https://www.bea.gov/news/schedule), [Federal Reserve FOMC calendars](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).
