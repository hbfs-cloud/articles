*Part 1 of 6 in Read the Economic Calendar.*

Company results move one stock or one sector. A single inflation print moves shares, bonds, currencies, metals and crypto in the same minute. That is why the calendar comes before the chart.

It also explains why the calendar is a risk tool rather than a crystal ball. The market has already priced the number everyone expects. It reacts to the gap between what arrived and what was expected, and to nothing else.

Take a real entry. Nonfarm payrolls for March 2026: previous month +151,000, forecast +140,000, actual −92,000. The forecast was already in the price. The 232,000 miss was not, and that is where the movement came from. A reading of 3.5% inflation can push the market up or down depending only on whether the forecast said 3.2% or 3.8%.

Most calendars sort events into three tiers, usually with coloured icons. Low impact runs to eighty or a hundred events a month and typically moves the index less than 0.3%. Wholesale inventories, building permits, that sort of thing. Medium impact, fifteen to twenty-five a month, moves 0.3% to 1%: private payroll estimates, producer prices, retail sales, consumer sentiment. High impact, only four to eight a month, moves 1% to 3% or more. That short list is the jobs report, consumer prices, the Fed decision, and GDP.

For a swing trader that leaves roughly one event a day worth knowing about. Manageable.

Learn four clock times and you have covered nearly everything American. Most major releases land at 8:30 in New York, which is 14:30 in Paris: payrolls, consumer prices, producer prices, GDP, retail sales, weekly jobless claims. Purchasing-manager surveys and job-openings data come at 10:00. The Fed's rate decision is at 14:00, and the press conference half an hour later.

The month has a shape too. Week one is the jobs week, with the payroll report on the first Friday. Week two is the inflation week, with consumer prices around the 13th. Week three carries retail sales and, eight times a year, the Fed. Week four brings the Fed's preferred inflation measure. Two hot weeks, two quieter ones. Open new swing positions in the quiet ones.

An aggregator row compresses several different labels into one line, and that is where errors start. The reference period says when the activity happened. The publication date says when it became public. The estimate label says whether this is a first pass or a revision. Confuse any two and old information looks like news. If your app shows "GDP" on Thursday, open the agency page and find out which quarter and which estimate round. "GDP revision" and "new quarter" are different questions.

A usable event note has four lines:

- Official release name and the agency that publishes it.
- Publication time in your own time zone.
- Reference period, and whether it is a first estimate or a revision.
- Your exposure decision: hold, reduce, hedge, or stand aside.

Keep prior, expected and actual in separate fields. The prior can be revised later, and a revision can matter as much as the new number: payrolls cut from +200,000 to +120,000 changes the picture even if today's figure lands on the forecast. The expected value is a private survey, not something the statistical agency endorses. Track all three and record what actually happened.

Limitation: schedules change, releases get delayed, and the events that hurt most rarely appear on any calendar. A war, a bank failure, a resignation. Prices can also move when the number matches the forecast exactly, because positioning and the detail inside the release still matter. A verified event list removes avoidable timing mistakes. It does not tell you which way anything will go.
