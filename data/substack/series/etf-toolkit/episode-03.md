*Part 3 of 6 in The ETF Toolkit.*

Audit a sector or thematic ETF as a concentrated portfolio, not as automatic diversification. Before buying, inspect the index methodology, top holdings, issuer caps, rebalance rules and overlap with what you already own. If the thesis cannot explain why the basket is preferable to its largest constituents or a broad fund, the label is doing too much work.

Sector classifications are rule systems. The Global Industry Classification Standard, for example, organizes companies into sectors and increasingly granular industry groups. A thematic index may cut across those sectors using revenue screens, keywords, analyst classification or other proprietary rules. Two funds with similar names can therefore hold very different companies.

Start with weights. Imagine a hypothetical theme fund whose five largest positions weigh 14%, 12%, 10%, 9% and 7%. Those five total 52%. Owning fifty securities would not prevent more than half the portfolio from depending on five issuers. Equal-weighting can reduce single-name concentration, but it introduces different turnover and capacity tradeoffs.

Then measure portfolio overlap. Suppose Fund A and Fund B share seven of their ten largest holdings. Splitting money equally between them does not create two independent themes. It may duplicate the same companies while charging two expense ratios. Compare weights, not just names: a shared holding at 1% in one fund and 12% in another creates different exposure.

Index construction also changes the bet. Market-cap weighting lets winners become larger. Equal weighting requires periodic rebalancing. Modified or capped weighting imposes limits. A revenue-purity screen may exclude diversified firms that benefit from the theme, while a loose screen may include companies with little relevant business. None is universally superior; each needs to match the thesis.

Use this theme audit:

- Read the investment objective and index methodology.
- Add the top five and top ten weights.
- Compare holdings with broad funds already in the portfolio.
- Identify reconstitution frequency, caps and eligibility rules.
- Check spread, assets, closure provisions and expense ratio.
- Write one fundamental condition that would invalidate the theme.

Past performance is a weak substitute for this work. A strong return can result from one dominant constituent, valuation expansion or a factor exposure unrelated to the theme's marketing story. Correlation between fund performance and a narrative variable does not prove that the narrative caused the return.

The limitation is timing. Holdings files are snapshots, index providers can revise methodologies, and company classifications can change as businesses evolve. Re-run the audit after reconstitution and before increasing size. Diversification is a property of the combined exposures, not the number of tickers on the statement.
