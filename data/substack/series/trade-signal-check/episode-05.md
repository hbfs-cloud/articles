*Part 5 of 6 in The 30-Second Trade Signal Check.*

"Institutions bought 46% of today's volume" sounds precise. It is not evidence until 46% has a venue,
time window, denominator and defensible direction.

Precision without reproducible provenance is only decoration, not decision-grade evidence.

Do not use a directional claim as evidence when its source cannot support direction. Treat an opaque
model score as a score, not as observed buying.

FINRA, a private US broker-dealer self-regulatory organization supervised by the SEC, publishes daily
short-sale volume for certain off-exchange trades reported through its facilities. The files are not
consolidated with exchange data. They are not short interest either: short interest is a snapshot of
positions held on specified reporting dates.

FINRA gives a specific example. A firm facilitating a customer's long sale may first sell from its own
account, then purchase the customer's shares at the same price. The short-marked disseminated leg may
appear in the volume file while the offsetting purchase does not. No end-of-day short position has to
remain. The file does not identify institutional accumulation, a bullish imbalance or account ownership.

This is how a real venue statistic acquires a story it cannot prove. The same problem appears under labels
such as dark-pool buying, smart-money absorption and whale accumulation.

Test a precise claim with four questions:

1. What exactly is counted?
2. Which venues, sessions or instruments are missing?
3. Does the dataset identify direction and account ownership?
4. Can another person repeat the calculation from the same fields?

Then record three times: when the event occurred, when the source observed it and when you read it. A
dashboard refreshed today may still contain a trade file that ended two sessions ago.

For the next candidate, underline every percentage in the thesis. Write its source, denominator, coverage
and timestamp beside it. Cross out the directional conclusion when the source only measures activity.

## Opaque data is a different case

A private dataset may still be useful. Its owner should be able to reproduce a value from a versioned
method, stable definitions and known coverage, then test the model on data not used to build it. An
outside reader who cannot inspect the raw records should treat the published value as a model output,
not an independently verified fact. That output may rank candidates without proving who bought them.
