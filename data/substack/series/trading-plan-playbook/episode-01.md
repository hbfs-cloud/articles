*Part 1 of 6 in Build a Trading Plan You Can Execute.*

Write the plan on a day when you hold nothing and have nothing to prove. A plan settles five questions
ahead of time: what you are allowed to trade, what counts as a valid setup, how much money may be at risk,
what cancels the trade, and when you will look at the results. Leave one of them open and you will still
answer it, only later, mid-move, with real money on the screen. That document is commentary, not a control.

Start with the parts a chart cannot change. Account type. Which products you may hold. Whether you borrow
money to trade, which is what leverage means. The hours you can actually watch. How easily the thing can
be sold. And the loss you can carry without changing your behaviour. FINRA puts risk plainly: the chance
of a financial outcome that hurts you. So the number is yours, drawn from your own finances. No universal
percentage exists, and anybody who quotes one is guessing about your life.

Then the chain of decisions, in order:

- **Universe:** instruments you understand and your account permits.
- **Setup:** the conditions that make a candidate eligible, written so they can be checked.
- **Trigger:** the event that allows an order. Not a forecast that price will cooperate.
- **Invalidation:** the market condition that defeats the idea.
- **Size:** the smallest amount allowed by planned loss, liquidity and portfolio limits.
- **Exit:** instructions for invalidation, profit taking, time expiry and rare events.

Put numbers on it. Capital of €20,000, and the plan caps one trade's loss at 1%, which is €200. Entry at
€80, invalidation at €76, so €4 of risk per share. Divide: €200 by €4 gives 50 shares, and you round down,
never up. If the target sits at €88, you stand to make €8 against €4 risked, a payoff of two to one. A plan
that demands at least 1.5 to 1 lets this one through. That 50 is a ceiling, not an instruction. Fees,
slippage, what you already own, and the chance that price gaps straight past €76 can all push it lower, or
down to no trade at all.

Consistency has a second payoff people miss. A hundred trades taken under a hundred different rules teach
nothing, because there is no rule to judge. Count on a couple of hundred trades under the same written
rules before your record says anything about the method rather than about luck.

Run this check before an order:

- Confirm the instrument and the setup are permitted.
- Record the trigger, the invalidation and every exit path.
- Size from the risk budget, then apply the tighter portfolio and liquidity caps.
- Name any scheduled event that changes the decision.
- Fix the review date, and do not rewrite the plan while a trade is live.

**Limitation:** a written plan cannot make a losing strategy profitable, and it cannot force a fill at the
price you wanted. It makes decisions auditable, which is a smaller and more useful claim. A sound plan also
leaves room for judgement during trading halts, broker outages, corporate actions, or news that destroys
the original assumptions. When you depart from the rules, write down that you did. Pretending afterwards
that the rule was followed costs you the only record you have.
