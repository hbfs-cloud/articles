*Part 2 of 6 in Options Without the Hidden Risk.*

Treat every Greek as a local estimate with other inputs held constant. It is not a forecast and it is not stable. Before entering an option, run at least one price shock, one time shock and one implied-volatility shock. If the position only works while everything except your preferred input stays frozen, the risk case is incomplete.

Delta estimates the premium change for a $1 move in the underlying. Gamma estimates how Delta itself changes after that move. Theta estimates the effect of one less day to expiration. Vega estimates the premium change for a one-percentage-point change in implied volatility. Each is generated by a model, and several inputs can move together.

Take a hypothetical call priced at $3.20 with Delta 0.55, Gamma 0.04, Theta -0.06 and Vega 0.10. A $1 rise in the stock suggests an initial premium gain of about $0.55, all else equal. Gamma suggests Delta might then rise toward 0.59. One day passing suggests roughly $0.06 of decay. A two-point decline in implied volatility suggests another $0.20 loss.

Do not simply add those estimates and call the result a quote. Delta and Gamma change as the stock moves; Theta and Vega also vary with price, time and volatility. The calculation is a first-pass stress test. It reveals why a correct directional call can still lose money: a modest stock gain may be outweighed by time decay and a volatility decline.

Position-level Delta also needs the contract multiplier. Three calls with Delta 0.55 represent an initial directional exposure of roughly 165 share-equivalents: `3 x 100 x 0.55`. That is not the same as owning 165 shares. The option exposure is nonlinear, expires, and changes as Gamma moves Delta.

Run this short Greek audit:

- Record Delta, Gamma, Theta and Vega with the quote timestamp.
- Convert per-share Greeks into whole-position dollar exposure.
- Shock the underlying both up and down, not only toward the thesis.
- Test one day of decay and a plausible volatility rise and fall.
- Recalculate near the strike and near expiration, where sensitivities can change quickly.

One common shortcut deserves caution: some traders read call Delta as an approximate probability of expiring in the money. That interpretation depends on modeling assumptions and does not equal the probability of profit. Profit also depends on premium paid, transaction costs and the exit rule.

The limitation is broader than model error. Greeks usually describe small changes around the current state. Gaps, trading halts, wide spreads and abrupt volatility repricing can make the realized move very different from the estimate. Use the dashboard to expose sensitivity, not to manufacture certainty.
