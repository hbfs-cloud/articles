*Part 2 of 5 in Survive Gap Risk.*

Stress the instrument you own, not merely the chart you watch. A single stock, broad ETF, leveraged ETF, listed option, and futures contract can reference related markets while producing different losses, trading windows, and exit constraints. Before holding overnight, identify the instrument’s next tradable session, source of leverage, valuation mechanism, and failure mode.

For an individual stock, issuer-specific disclosures can move the next available market far beyond a resting stop. Earnings, financing, litigation, regulatory action, and corporate transactions require primary-source verification. Concentration makes that discontinuity a portfolio problem.

An ETF adds another layer. Its exchange price can trade above or below net asset value, and some underlying holdings may be closed while the ETF trades. Read the prospectus and issuer data for holdings, spread, premiums or discounts, and creation-redemption risks. Leveraged and inverse ETFs generally target a stated daily result; returns over longer periods can diverge substantially from that daily multiple, especially when markets move back and forth.

Listed options have limited trading hours for many contracts. A stock may reprice while its options market is closed, leaving the option holder unable to adjust. Strike, expiration, implied volatility, spread, exercise, and assignment rules all affect the result. The underlying stock’s percentage gap is not the option’s guaranteed percentage change.

Futures and retail foreign-exchange products introduce contract-specific sessions and leverage. Digital assets may trade continuously on a venue, but continuous timestamps do not guarantee continuous liquidity, venue access, or a transferable price across platforms. Use the regulated disclosure and the venue agreement that apply to the actual account.

**Worked procedure:** Suppose one thesis can be expressed through stock, a sector ETF, or a call option. For each candidate, write: the first session in which it can be exited; a stressed bid or option value; maximum capital loss; margin response; spread and depth; and any expiry or reset feature. Reject the expression whose mechanics you cannot model. The cheapest premium or smallest cash outlay need not carry the smallest risk.

**Instrument check**

- Read the prospectus, contract, or options disclosure.
- Map regular, extended, and closed trading periods.
- Identify embedded leverage, reset, expiry, and assignment.
- Stress the executable instrument price, fees, and margin.
- Check venue and custody access before the event.

Product structure does not make outcomes predictable. ETF arbitrage can be impaired, option markets can open wide, and a normally liquid futures contract can gap after a trading pause. Official disclosures describe mechanics and risks; they do not supply a reliable forecast of the next loss.
