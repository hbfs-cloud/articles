*Part 1 of 6 in The ETF Toolkit.*

Before buying an exchange-traded product, identify what you legally own and what the vehicle is designed to track. Do not stop at the ticker or the word “ETF.” Read the summary prospectus, investment objective, principal risks, holdings, expense table and issuer trading data. If the product is an ETN or commodity pool rather than a registered investment-company ETF, its protections and risks differ.

An ETF share represents a proportionate interest in a fund portfolio. Retail investors trade shares on an exchange at market prices, while authorized participants can transact with the fund in large creation units. That creation-redemption mechanism can help market price stay near portfolio value, but it does not guarantee equality.

Net asset value, or NAV, is calculated from fund assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. The exchange price moves during the session. A price above NAV is a premium; below NAV is a discount. The difference may widen when underlying markets are closed, securities are hard to price, or trading is stressed.

Trading cost deserves its own calculation. Suppose a hypothetical ETF is quoted at $49.95 bid and $50.05 ask. Buying 100 shares at the ask and immediately selling at the bid loses $10 before commission or any market movement: `$0.10 x 100`. The annual expense ratio is not the only cost. Bid-ask spread, brokerage charges, premiums or discounts, and tracking difference also affect the result.

Tracking difference is the fund return minus its benchmark return over the same period. Expenses contribute, but sampling, trading, taxes, cash balances and securities lending can also matter. Compare like with like: same index variant, currency treatment, dividend convention and date range.

Use this five-minute file check:

- Confirm whether the vehicle is an ETF, ETN, commodity pool or another ETP.
- Read the objective and benchmark methodology.
- Inspect holdings, concentration and replication method.
- Check expense ratio, median spread and historical premiums or discounts.
- Compare fund and benchmark returns on matching terms.
- Read creation, redemption, closure and liquidation provisions.

A high exchange volume is not a complete liquidity test. The liquidity of the underlying portfolio and the ability of market makers and authorized participants to hedge matter too. Conversely, a modest screen volume does not automatically make an ETF unusable. Spread, order-book depth, trade size and underlying liquidity need to be considered together.

The limitation is structural: public disclosures are snapshots. Holdings can change, spreads vary intraday, and NAV is generally an end-of-day measure. The prospectus explains design and risk; it cannot promise execution quality or future tracking.
