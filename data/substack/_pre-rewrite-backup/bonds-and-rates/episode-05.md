*Part 5 of 6 in Bonds and Rates for Equity Traders.*

Choose the instrument by the risk you intend to own. Use a cash bond when contractual cash flows and a maturity date are central. Use a bond fund when diversified portfolio exposure and exchange trading matter. Use futures only when standardized leveraged exposure, margin and contract expiry fit the plan. A rate view does not excuse an instrument mismatch.

An individual bond has defined terms, although default, calls and an early sale can change the realized result. A bond ETF owns a changing portfolio. Its shares trade intraday at a market price that may be above or below net asset value, and the fund itself normally does not mature on the investor's chosen date. Duration, credit quality, fees, holdings and index rules determine what the ticker represents.

Futures replace an upfront purchase with a margined contract. The CFTC defines margin as collateral or a performance bond, not partial payment for the underlying asset. Leverage means a relatively small amount of capital controls a larger exposure. Adverse moves can trigger margin calls, and losses may exceed the initial deposit. Contract expiry and settlement rules also require active management.

Write an instrument sheet before placing an order. State whether the thesis concerns the level of government yields, the curve, inflation compensation or corporate credit. Record the vehicle's duration, benchmark, credit exposure, liquidity, expenses, margin treatment and expiry. Add the intended holding period and exit method.

Consider a government-rate thesis expressed through a corporate-bond ETF. Even if Treasury yields move as expected, wider corporate spreads can offset the rate gain. A broad aggregate fund may add mortgage prepayment risk as well. The position is not defective; it simply owns more variables than the thesis named. The audit should expose that mismatch before capital is committed.

Pretrade questions:

- What precise factor is expected to move?
- Which unwanted factors come with the vehicle?
- Can the position be exited under realistic liquidity?
- Are margin, roll or premium-to-NAV risks present?
- Does the prospectus or contract specification match the holding period?

Direct ownership is not automatically safer than a fund, and an ETF wrapper does not make risky bonds low risk. Cash bonds can be illiquid and concentrated. Funds can lose money through rates, credit and prepayments. Futures can produce rapid leveraged losses.

No instrument isolates a macro view perfectly. Cash bonds add issuer and liquidity terms; funds add portfolio construction and market-price risk; futures add basis, margin and expiry. The right choice is the one whose residual risks are identified, sized and monitored, not the one with the most convenient ticker.
