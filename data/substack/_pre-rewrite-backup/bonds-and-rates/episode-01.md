*Part 1 of 6 in Bonds and Rates for Equity Traders.*

Before comparing yields, identify the issuer, principal amount, coupon type, maturity, seniority, security and every clause that can change the cash flows. If you cannot explain when the bond pays, when principal is due and who can alter that schedule, do not treat its yield as comparable with another bond's.

A bond is debt. The investor lends to an issuer, which promises specified interest and principal payments subject to the contract and the issuer's ability to pay. Ownership does not confer the residual claim or voting rights associated with common stock. For an equity trader, that difference matters: bondholders focus first on payment priority and downside recovery, while shareholders own the remaining economics after liabilities.

Treasury marketable securities include bills, notes, bonds, TIPS and floating-rate notes. Bills are sold at par or a discount and pay face value at maturity. Notes and bonds pay fixed interest on a schedule. TIPS adjust principal with the specified consumer-price index. Floating-rate notes reset their interest rate from a stated index and spread. These structures carry the full faith and credit of the United States, but they still differ in rate sensitivity, inflation exposure and cash-flow timing.

Corporate debt adds issuer credit and contractual complexity. A secured bond may have a claim on specified collateral; an unsecured bond relies on the issuer's general credit. Senior and subordinated claims occupy different positions in the capital structure. A call provision may let the issuer repay early, often when reinvestment is least attractive to the holder.

Use the CUSIP or other unique identifier to run a contract audit. Retrieve the prospectus or offering document, write the maturity and coupon terms, locate the call schedule, and identify the quoted yield as current yield, yield to maturity, yield to call or yield to worst. Then inspect recent transaction data where available. FINRA's TRACE data can show reported price, yield, execution time and size for eligible fixed-income trades.

Imagine two corporate bonds with similar yields and maturities. One is callable; the other is not. The callable bond's yield to maturity assumes it remains outstanding, but the issuer may redeem it under the contract. Comparing only the displayed yield hides a different cash-flow path. The useful comparison includes yield to worst and the call date.

Contract check:

- Confirm issuer, identifier, maturity and payment currency.
- Distinguish fixed, floating, inflation-linked and zero-coupon cash flows.
- Read seniority, collateral, call and conversion terms.
- Name the yield measure shown on the quote.
- Check trading activity, fees and accrued interest.

Yield to maturity is not a guaranteed realized return. It assumes contractual payments occur and embeds reinvestment assumptions; taxes, costs, default, calls and an early sale can change the outcome. Sparse secondary trading can also make the displayed price a poor guide to executable value.
