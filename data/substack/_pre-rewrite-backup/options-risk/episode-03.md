*Part 3 of 6 in Options Without the Hidden Risk.*

Choose the stock obligation before looking at the premium. Sell a covered call only if selling the shares at the strike is acceptable. Sell a cash-secured put only if buying the shares at the strike is acceptable even after a sharp decline. Buy protection only after defining what loss must be limited and for how long.

A covered call combines long stock with a short call. The premium provides only a small downside cushion; the stock can still lose most of its value. The call also caps the sale price if assigned. Suppose a hypothetical investor owns 100 shares bought at $50 and sells a $55 call for $1.50. The effective assigned sale price is $56.50 before fees. If the stock rises to $70, the position still gives up the shares at $55 when assigned. If the stock falls to zero, the $150 premium offsets only part of the $5,000 stock loss.

A cash-secured put creates a similar economic question from the other direction. Selling one $45 put for $1.20 requires enough cash to buy 100 shares at $45 if assigned. The effective acquisition price would be $43.80 before costs, but the market could be far below that level. The $120 premium is maximum option income, not maximum risk.

A protective put changes the downside shape. If stock bought at $50 is paired with a $45 put costing $1, the put gives the holder the right to sell at $45 through expiration. Ignoring fees, the combined expiration loss is limited to $6 per share: the $5 decline from purchase price to strike plus the $1 premium. That floor expires, and the premium is spent whether or not protection is needed.

A collar finances some or all of a protective put by selling a call. It exchanges part of the upside for a downside floor. The trade is not “free protection” merely because the initial premiums offset; assignment, spreads, taxes and the opportunity cost of capped gains remain.

Before using any stock-plus-option structure:

- Write the exact share obligation if the short option is assigned.
- Calculate effective purchase or sale price after premium.
- Show stock loss at zero, not only option profit.
- Check earnings, dividends and early-assignment exposure.
- Decide whether each leg will be closed, exercised or allowed to expire.

The limitation is that these expiration examples omit changing volatility and early exits. A protective put can be sold before expiration, while a covered call can be assigned early. Broker procedures and tax treatment also differ. The payoff shape is knowable; the path and final result are not.
