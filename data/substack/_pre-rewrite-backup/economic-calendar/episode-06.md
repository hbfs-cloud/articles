*Part 6 of 6 in Read the Economic Calendar.*

Decide before a scheduled release whether to hold, reduce, hedge or avoid exposure. Also decide which orders may remain active and what evidence would justify a later entry. If a gap or poor execution would breach your loss tolerance, position size is already too large for the event.

Build the week from official schedules. Record each release in your local time zone, note the affected positions, and distinguish initial estimates from revisions. Rank events by their relevance to your actual exposures rather than copying a provider's generic impact symbol. A bank stock, a long-duration bond fund and an energy producer do not carry the same event sensitivity.

For a concrete procedure, consider a rate-sensitive ETF held before a CPI release. Write two acceptable choices in advance: retain a deliberately limited position through publication, or close it and reassess after trading conditions settle. Record what part of the release matters to the thesis, such as monthly core components rather than the annual headline alone. Ban any unplanned market order at the release time. After publication, compare the official data with your stated condition before taking action.

Order mechanics belong in the plan. Investor.gov explains that a stop order becomes a market order when its stop price is reached, so the execution price can differ from the stop price in a fast market. A stop-limit order controls the eligible price but may not execute. Broker-dealers can also use different standards to determine whether a stop has been triggered. Read your broker's rules instead of treating a stop as a guaranteed loss cap.

The short event checklist:

- Verify agency, time, reference period and release vintage.
- List positions with direct or indirect sensitivity.
- Set maximum exposure before publication.
- Review open stops, limits and conditional orders.
- Define the evidence required for any post-release action.
- Log the decision and execution after the event.

Avoid grading the plan by whether the market happened to move in your favor. A good process can precede a losing outcome, and an improvised trade can make money. Review whether you followed the exposure limit, used the intended order and based the interpretation on the official release.

The plan cannot contain unscheduled news, exchange disruptions or every liquidity shock. It also cannot ensure execution at a chosen price. Its purpose is narrower: remove avoidable decisions from the most disorderly part of a known event and leave a record that can be reviewed without hindsight.
