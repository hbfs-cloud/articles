PART A: WHEN THINGS GO WRONG

The Three Emotional Phases of a Loss

When you lose money on an investment, your brain goes through a predictable sequence of emotions. This is not a theory — it is backed by psychology research and experienced by every single investor on earth. Understanding these phases will not make the pain disappear, but it will help you recognize what is happening inside you, so you do not make things worse.

The Injured Athlete Analogy

Imagine a basketball player who twists their ankle mid-game. Phase 1: they do not feel the pain immediately (shock and denial). Phase 2: the pain hits and they get frustrated, maybe blaming the floor or the referee (anger and blame). Phase 3: they accept the injury and start rehabilitation (acceptance and recovery). Investing works the same way. The only way to play again is to go through all three phases — you cannot skip any of them.

1
Phase 1: Shock & Denial
"This isn't really happening. It'll come back."

The moment you see a big red number on your screen, your brain literally shuts down the part that processes loss. You tell yourself it is temporary. You check the stock every 5 minutes hoping for a bounce. You avoid telling anyone about it. This is completely normal — your brain is trying to protect you from pain.

How long it lasts: A few hours to a few days, depending on the size of the loss.
What to do: Nothing. Seriously. Do not buy more. Do not sell in a panic. Just breathe. Acknowledge the feeling. The worst decisions are made in this phase.

2
Phase 2: Anger & Blame
"It's the market's fault. It's the CEO's fault. It's my broker's fault."

Once the reality sinks in, you get angry. You blame the market, the analysts who recommended the stock, the news, or the company's management. Some people blame themselves so harshly they spiral into guilt and shame. Others go the opposite direction and refuse to accept any responsibility at all.

How long it lasts: Days to weeks. Sometimes months if the loss is very large.
What to do: Write it down. Literally open a notebook and write: "I am angry because ___." Getting the emotions out of your head and onto paper is one of the most powerful things you can do. Do not trade during this phase.

3
Phase 3: Acceptance & Learning
"OK, that happened. What can I learn from it?"

This is where the magic happens. You stop looking for someone to blame and start asking yourself honest questions: "Did I do my research?" "Was my position too big?" "Did I have a stop-loss?" "Would I make the same decision again with the same information?" This is not about beating yourself up — it is about becoming a better investor.

How long it lasts: This phase should last the rest of your investing life. The best investors never stop learning.
What to do: Update your trading journal. Adjust your rules if needed. And when you are ready — slowly, carefully — get back in the game.

Key Takeaway

  • All three phases are normal. You are not weak for feeling shocked, angry, or sad about money.
  • The goal is not to skip phases — it is to move through them without making destructive decisions.
  • The most dangerous moment is during Phase 1 and Phase 2, because that is when people make revenge trades or panic sells.

Three Deadly Mistakes After a Loss

When you are in Phase 1 or Phase 2 of a loss, your brain will push you toward one of these three destructive behaviors. Knowing about them in advance is your best defense.

🃏
Revenge Trading
Like a poker player who just lost a big hand and starts betting wildly to "win it back." You take impulsive, oversized trades driven by emotion, not logic. Result? You often lose even more.

"I lost $2,000 on Tesla, so I'm going to YOLO $5,000 on some random penny stock to make it back." — Famous last words.

🎰
The Martingale Spiral
Doubling down after every loss, believing that the next trade "has to" be a winner. This is a casino strategy that has bankrupted millions. Markets are not coin flips — a losing trade can keep losing.

"I'll just buy more — it HAS to bounce." Narrator: it did not bounce. It went to zero.

🐻
Panic Selling
Selling everything at the absolute worst moment because the fear becomes unbearable. Like trying to outrun a bear — you cannot outrun a bear market. You lock in your losses and miss the recovery.

During COVID crash (March 2020), the S&P 500 dropped 34%. People who panic-sold missed the 68% recovery over the next 12 months.

Why Your Brain Tricks You

Your brain evolved to survive on the savannah, not to manage a stock portfolio. When you see red numbers, your amygdala (the fear center) hijacks your prefrontal cortex (the thinking center). This is called an "amygdala hijack." The same instinct that made your ancestors run from lions now makes you panic-sell stocks. The cure? Time. Wait at least 24 hours before making any decision after a loss.

The Sunk Cost Trap

Just because you already lost money on something does not mean you should keep holding it. "But I've already lost $3,000 on it" is not a reason to stay in a bad investment. The money is gone whether you sell or not. The only question is: "If I had cash right now, would I buy this stock today at this price?" If the answer is no, sell it and move on.

Your 3-Step Recovery Plan

You had a bad loss. Your portfolio is hurting. Here is your simple, step-by-step plan to get back on track. No complicated formulas. Just common sense.

1
Pause
Step away for at least 48 hours. Close your brokerage app. Do not check prices. Go for a walk, watch a movie, spend time with people you care about. Your portfolio will still be there when you come back. This is not quitting — it is resting.
2
Analyze
Open your trading journal and answer honestly: What went wrong? Was it the thesis, the timing, or the position size? Did you follow your own rules? Write it all down. This is your tuition — make sure you actually learn the lesson you paid for.
3
Gradual Return
Start small. Ridiculously small. If you normally trade $5,000 positions, start with $500. Rebuild your confidence with small wins. Like a runner recovering from an injury — you do not start with a marathon. You start with a walk around the block.

The Golden 1% Rule

Here is the single most powerful risk management rule for beginners: never risk more than 1% of your total portfolio on any single trade. If you have $10,000, your maximum loss on any single position should be $100. This means using stop-losses strategically.

Why 1%? Because even if you have 10 losing trades in a row (unlikely but possible), you have only lost 10% of your portfolio. You are still in the game. You can recover. At 10% per trade, three losses in a row and you have lost 30%. That is much harder to come back from.

The Math of Recovery

This is the single most important math concept in investing. If you lose 10%, you only need an 11% gain to get back to even. Manageable. But if you lose 50%, you need a 100% gain to recover — you need to double your money. And if you lose 90%? You need a 900% gain. This is why preventing large losses is infinitely more important than chasing large gains.

Even Legends Lose Money

If you think the world's best investors never lose money, think again. Every single one of them has stories of painful losses. The difference? They did not quit. They learned, adapted, and came back stronger.

Warren Buffett
The "Oracle of Omaha"
Lost $444 million on his investment in Dexter Shoe Company. He bought it with Berkshire stock instead of cash, making the actual loss closer to $3.5 billion when you account for the stock appreciation he missed out on. He calls it his worst investment ever.
Lesson: Even the greatest investor alive makes terrible calls. What matters is the next 100 decisions.
George Soros
The Man Who Broke the Bank of England
After his famous $1 billion profit betting against the British Pound, he lost $700 million in the 1998 Russian financial crisis. He also lost nearly $1 billion during the dot-com crash. His career return? Still spectacularly positive.
Lesson: You do not need to be right every time. You need your winners to be bigger than your losers.
Peter Lynch
Legendary Fidelity Fund Manager
Admitted that only about 6 out of every 10 stocks he picked were winners. That means 4 out of 10 were losers. But his winners made so much money that they more than covered all the losses and then some. His fund averaged 29% per year for 13 years.
Lesson: You do not need a high "batting average." A few home runs can make your entire career.

Key Takeaway

  • Losing is part of the game. Literally every successful investor has a list of terrible trades.
  • What separates winners from losers is not avoiding losses — it is managing them and learning from them.
  • Your next trade does not know about your last trade. Each one is a fresh start.
PART B: WHEN THINGS GO RIGHT

Why Winning Is Also Dangerous

This might be the most counterintuitive thing in this entire series: your gains can be just as dangerous as your losses. Most beginners worry about losing money. Very few worry about what happens when they start making money. But that is where some of the biggest traps hide.

The Lottery Winner Analogy

Did you know that about 70% of lottery winners go bankrupt within 5 years? Not because they are bad people, but because sudden wealth changes your behavior in predictable and dangerous ways. You start spending more freely, making riskier bets, and believing you have some special talent for making money. The same thing happens to investors after a big win. A sudden profit can make you feel invincible — and invincible people take reckless risks.

!
The Overconfidence Trap
Beginner's luck at the poker table

You made money on your first three trades. Congratulations! But here is the uncomfortable truth: you might have just gotten lucky. Beginner's luck is a real phenomenon — ask any poker player. The danger is that you start believing you have figured out the market. You increase your position sizes. You stop doing research. You stop using stop-losses because "you know what you're doing."

Signs you are overconfident:

  • You are increasing your position sizes after a winning streak
  • You are telling friends and family about your "brilliant" trades
  • You stopped using stop-losses because "you'll know when to sell"
  • You are spending less time researching before buying
  • You are considering quitting your job to trade full-time

Why Does This Matter?

Because overconfidence after gains is statistically the #1 reason retail investors eventually lose money. A study by Barber and Odean (two finance professors at UC Berkeley) found that overconfident investors trade 45% more than average and earn 6.5% less per year. The more confident you feel, the more careful you should actually be.

How to Protect Your Gains

Making money is great. Keeping it is better. Here are three simple strategies to make sure your gains do not evaporate.

1
Take Some Off the Table
The "house money" strategy

If a stock has gone up 50%, consider selling enough shares to recover your original investment. Now you are playing with "house money" — everything left is pure profit. Even if the stock goes to zero, you have not lost a single dollar of your own money. This is the simplest and most powerful gain protection strategy.

Example: You bought 100 shares of XYZ at $10 ($1,000 total). It goes to $15. Sell 67 shares ($1,005 = your money back). Keep 33 shares ($495 of pure profit) and let them ride forever. Worst case? You break even. Best case? Those 33 shares become worth much more.

2
Diversify Your Winners
Don't let one stock become your whole portfolio

If one stock has gone up so much that it now represents 30%, 40%, or 50% of your portfolio, you have a concentration risk problem. Even if you love the company, consider trimming it back to a reasonable size (usually 5-10% of your portfolio). Use the proceeds to buy other things you believe in.

Real example: Imagine you put 10% of your portfolio into Nvidia in early 2023. By early 2025, that position might have grown to represent 50% of your total portfolio. That is not because you bought more — it is because Nvidia went up so much. If Nvidia drops 30%, your entire portfolio drops 15%. Trimming is not pessimism; it is prudence.

3
Use Trailing Stop-Losses
An automatic safety net that follows your gains up

A trailing stop-loss is like a guard dog that follows your stock price upward but protects you if it reverses. If you set a 15% trailing stop, it will automatically sell your stock if it drops 15% from its highest point. As the stock goes up, the stop follows it up. It never goes down.

Example: You bought at $100. Stock rises to $150. Your 15% trailing stop is now at $127.50. Stock rises to $200. Stop moves to $170. Stock drops to $170. You sell at $170. You gave back some gains, but you still made 70%. Without the stop, you might have held all the way back down to $100 (or worse).

Key Takeaway

  • Unrealized gains are not real money until you sell. A $10,000 profit on paper can vanish in a week.
  • "Take some off the table" is the simplest and most important advice for winners.
  • Trailing stops let you ride winners while protecting against reversals.
  • Rebalancing is not being pessimistic — it is being smart.

The Tax Trap (Explained Simply)

Here is something that catches many beginners off guard: when you sell at a profit, you owe taxes on that profit. This is called "capital gains tax." The rate depends on how long you held the investment and what country you live in.

The Rookie Tax Mistake

Imagine you made $5,000 in trading profits during 2025. Tax season comes in April 2026. You owe, let's say, $1,500 in taxes on those profits. But you already spent or reinvested all $5,000. Now you owe $1,500 that you do not have. This happens to beginners every single year. The fix: every time you take a profit, set aside 25-30% in a savings account for taxes. Do not touch it. When tax season comes, you will be glad you did.

Why Does This Matter?

Because taxes can turn a winning trade into a barely-breaking-even trade. If you buy a stock, it goes up 10%, and you sell after 3 months, your actual profit after taxes (in the US, in a regular account) might be only 6-7%. Add in trading fees and you might be looking at 5%. Understanding this helps you make better decisions about when to sell and which account to use.

PART C: MANAGING YOUR EMOTIONS

Your Trading Journal: The Most Underrated Tool

Ask any professional trader what their most important tool is, and they will not say a stock screener or a charting platform. They will say: my trading journal. It is the single best way to turn expensive mistakes into free lessons. And it does not need to be complicated.

The Simple 3-Column Journal

You do not need fancy software. A spreadsheet, a notebook, or even your phone's notes app will work. Every time you make a trade, write down three things: what you did, why you did it, and how you felt. That is it. After a month, go back and read your entries. You will be shocked by how much you learn about yourself.

What I Did Why I Did It How I Felt
Bought 50 shares of AAPL at $185 Strong earnings, new AI features, RSI near oversold. Planned to hold 3-6 months. Calm and confident. Did my research over the weekend. Position is 3% of portfolio.
Sold all my TSLA at $160 (loss of $1,200) Hit my stop-loss at -15%. Original thesis (FSD launch) did not play out. Frustrated but relieved. Kept telling myself I'd feel worse losing more. Took a walk after.
Bought 200 shares of DOGE-USD at $0.08 My friend said it was going to $1. No real research. Excited, FOMO. Saw it going up on Twitter. Bought at 11pm on a Friday night.
Red flag pattern: Notice how the DOGE trade has no real thesis, was driven by FOMO, and was executed late at night. The journal makes this obvious in hindsight.

Key Takeaway

  • The journal reveals your patterns. After 20 entries, you will see things like "I always buy on Fridays when I'm bored" or "I never use stop-losses on stocks I emotionally like."
  • Writing forces clarity. If you cannot explain in one sentence why you are buying something, you probably should not buy it.
  • Your emotions are data. "I felt excited and impulsive" is a red flag. "I felt calm and methodical" is a green flag.

The "3 Losing Trades = Pause" Rule

This is a rule used by many professional traders, and it is beautifully simple: if you have 3 losing trades in a row, you stop trading for at least one full week. No exceptions. No "just one more." You step away completely.

Why 3?

One losing trade is normal — it happens to everyone. Two in a row is unlucky but not unusual. But three in a row? That is a signal. Either the market environment has changed and your strategy is not working, or your emotions are affecting your decisions. Either way, the smartest move is to stop, review your journal, and figure out what is going on before you put more money at risk.

  • After 3 losses: Close your trading app. Set a calendar reminder for one week from today.
  • During the break: Review all 3 trades in your journal. Look for patterns. Were they the same kind of trade? The same sector? The same time of day?
  • Before coming back: Write down what you will do differently. Be specific. "I will only trade stocks with volume above 1 million" is good. "I'll be more careful" is not.
  • When you return: Start with half your normal position size. If you win 2 out of 3, you can go back to normal size.

Why Does This Matter?

Because most people's worst losing streaks happen not because of bad luck, but because of compounding emotional decisions. Loss #1 makes you anxious. Loss #2 makes you desperate. Loss #3 makes you reckless. Loss #4 through #10 happen because you were trading on tilt, not on logic. The pause rule breaks this cycle before it starts.

Take Care of Yourself (Seriously)

This might seem like strange advice in a stock market guide, but hear us out: your physical and mental health directly affects your investment performance. This is not motivational-poster nonsense — it is backed by research.

Sleep
Sleep-deprived people take 50% more risk than rested people. If you are making trading decisions at 2am, you are already at a disadvantage. Get 7-8 hours. Never trade when exhausted.
Exercise
Physical activity reduces cortisol (the stress hormone) and increases endorphins. A 30-minute walk before market open can literally make you a better decision-maker. Many top traders swear by their morning workout routine.
Talk to Someone
Do not bottle up your trading emotions. Talk to a friend, a partner, or join an online community of investors. Sharing your wins and losses with someone who understands reduces the emotional weight by half.

The Screen Time Trap

Studies show that investors who check their portfolio more than once a day earn lower returns than those who check once a week. Why? Because every time you look, you are exposed to short-term noise that triggers emotional reactions. Checking less often is not laziness — it is discipline. Set one time per day (or per week) to review your portfolio, and stick to it.

When It Becomes Too Much

We need to talk about something serious for a moment. For some people, trading can become an addiction. Just like gambling, the dopamine rush of a winning trade can create compulsive behavior. This is not a character flaw — it is a medical condition that affects real people, including very intelligent people.

Warning Signs of Trading Addiction

If you recognize 3 or more of these signs in yourself, please take them seriously:

  • You think about trades constantly, even during meals, conversations, or sleep
  • You need to trade larger and larger amounts to feel the same excitement
  • You have tried to stop or reduce trading and could not
  • You become irritable or anxious when you cannot trade (weekends, holidays)
  • You are hiding your trading activity or losses from your partner/family
  • You are trading with money you cannot afford to lose (rent, food, emergency fund)
  • You are borrowing money or using credit cards to fund your trading account
  • You feel unable to close your trading app even when you know you should

Why Does This Matter?

Because no amount of money is worth your mental health, your relationships, or your well-being. Investing should make your life better, not worse. If it is making you anxious, depressed, or secretive, that is a clear sign that something needs to change. And changing does not mean quitting forever — it might mean taking a long break, working with a therapist, or switching to a simple index fund strategy that requires zero daily decisions.

BONUS: THE LAZY INVESTOR STRATEGY

Bonus: The Lazy Investor Strategy

10% per year in 5 minutes per month

Throughout this series, we have talked about stock picking, portfolio construction, strategies, emotions... But there is one method so simple it almost seems too good to be true. And yet, it beats the majority of finance professionals over the long term.

The method in 3 steps

1

Open a brokerage account

Pick a low-cost broker (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, Interactive Brokers...). It is free and takes about 15 minutes.

2

Set up automatic transfers

Schedule a monthly transfer from your bank account to your brokerage. Even $50 a month is a great start.

3

Buy ONE single ETF

Every month, buy shares of the same world or US index ETF. That is it. No analysis. No timing.

Which ETF to pick?

Here are three excellent options. Any of them will do the job:

The 3 Rules of the Lazy Investor

1. Automate

Set up the automatic transfer. If it is automatic, you cannot forget or hesitate. The money leaves before you have a chance to spend it.

2. Never sell

The market drops 30%? Good — you are buying at a discount. The key is time in the market, not timing the market.

3. Ignore the noise

TV pundits, predicted crashes, "golden opportunities"... Ignore all of it. Your ETF does the work for you, quietly, in the background.

Warren Buffett's Million-Dollar Bet

In 2007, Warren Buffett (one of the richest investors in the world) bet $1 million that a simple S&P 500 index fund would beat the best hedge funds over 10 years. The result in 2017? The index fund returned 125%. The hedge funds? Only 36% on average. The professionals with their teams of analysts, algorithms, and billions of dollars lost to a strategy that anyone can execute in 5 minutes a month.

The 5 Golden Rules of Investing

If you forget everything else from this series, remember these five rules. Print them out. Stick them on your monitor. Tattoo them on your forearm (just kidding... maybe). These are the rules that separate people who build wealth from people who blow up their accounts.

Rule #1: Never Risk What You Cannot Afford to Lose
Emergency fund first. Investing money second. If losing it all would ruin your life, you are playing with fire.
Rule #2: Do Your Own Research
Nobody cares about your money as much as you do. Never buy something just because someone on the internet said so.
Rule #3: Diversify
Do not put all your eggs in one basket. Spread across stocks, sectors, geographies, and asset classes.
Rule #4: Be Patient
Wealth is built over years and decades, not days and weeks. Time in the market beats timing the market.
Rule #5: Manage Your Emotions
Fear and greed are your two biggest enemies. When you feel either one strongly, that is your signal to slow down.

A Letter to You, the Graduate

Dear Future Investor,

If you are reading this, you have done something remarkable. You sat through six chapters of financial education — not because someone forced you to, but because you chose to invest in yourself before investing in the market. That alone puts you ahead of 95% of people who open a brokerage account.

Here is what I want you to know: you will make mistakes. Not "might" — will. You will buy something that drops 30% the next day. You will sell something too early and watch it double. QUIZ: TEST YOUR KNOWLEDGE

Final Exam: 8 Questions

This is your graduation exam. No grades, no pressure — just a chance to see how much you have absorbed from this final chapter. Click each question to reveal the answer.

Q1: What are the three emotional phases investors go through after a big loss?
Answer: (1) Shock & Denial — "this isn't really happening." (2) Anger & Blame — looking for someone or something to blame. (3) Acceptance & Learning — honestly analyzing what went wrong and using it to improve. The goal is not to skip these phases but to move through them without making destructive trading decisions.
Q2: What is "revenge trading" and why is it dangerous?
Answer: Revenge trading is when you take impulsive, oversized trades immediately after a loss, trying to "win your money back" quickly. It is like a poker player going on tilt. It is dangerous because the decisions are driven by emotion (anger, frustration) rather than logic, and the position sizes are usually too large. This almost always leads to even bigger losses.
Q3: If you lose 50% of your portfolio, what percentage gain do you need to get back to even?
Answer: You need a 100% gain to recover from a 50% loss. If you start with $10,000 and lose 50%, you have $5,000. To get from $5,000 back to $10,000, you need to double your money (100% gain). This is why preventing large losses is far more important than chasing large gains. A 10% loss only requires an 11% gain to recover, which is much more manageable.
Q4: Why can winning trades be dangerous for a beginner?
Answer: Because winning breeds overconfidence. After a streak of profitable trades, beginners often believe they have "figured out the market." They increase position sizes, stop doing research, remove stop-losses, and take on more risk. This is called "beginner's luck" and it is one of the most common paths to eventually losing large amounts of money. Studies show overconfident investors trade 45% more and earn 6.5% less per year.
Q5: What is the "house money" strategy for protecting gains?
Answer: When a stock has risen significantly (e.g., 50%), you sell enough shares to recover your original investment. The remaining shares represent pure profit — "house money." Even if the stock goes to zero, you have not lost any of your own capital. Example: You buy 100 shares at $10 ($1,000). Stock rises to $15. You sell 67 shares to get back ~$1,000 (your money). The remaining 33 shares ($495) are pure profit that you let ride.
Q6: What should you write in your trading journal for every trade?
Answer: Three things: (1) What you did — the action (buy/sell, ticker, amount, price). (2) Why you did it — your thesis or reason (technical signal, fundamental analysis, news, etc.). (3) How you felt — your emotional state (calm, excited, anxious, impulsive). After reviewing your journal over time, patterns emerge that reveal your strengths and weaknesses. The "how I felt" column is especially powerful for spotting emotional trading.
Q7: What is the "3 losing trades = pause" rule?
Answer: If you have 3 consecutive losing trades, you stop trading for at least one full week. During that week, you review all 3 trades in your journal, identify patterns, and write down what you will do differently. When you return, you start with half your normal position size. This rule exists to prevent emotional spiral trading, where each loss leads to increasingly desperate and larger bets.
Q8: What are the 5 Golden Rules of investing from this series?
Answer: (1) Never risk what you cannot afford to lose — emergency fund comes first. (2) Do your own research (DYOR) — never buy just because someone recommended it. (3) Diversify — across stocks, sectors, geographies, and asset classes. (4) Be patient — wealth is built over years, not days. (5) Manage your emotions — when you feel strong fear or greed, slow down. These five rules, consistently followed, will put you ahead of most investors over time.
GLOSSARY

Glossary: All the Terms in Plain English

Every term from this chapter explained in the simplest possible language. Bookmark this page — you will come back to it.

TermSimple Explanation
Amygdala Hijack When the fear center of your brain overrides your rational thinking. This is why you panic-sell or revenge-trade. The cure is to wait 24 hours before any decision after a strong emotion.
Capital Gains Tax Tax you pay on investment profits when you sell. Short-term (held less than 1 year) is taxed more than long-term (held more than 1 year). Always set money aside for it.
DCA (Dollar-Cost Averaging) Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals (e.g., $200 every month) regardless of the price. This smooths out your average purchase price over time and removes the stress of timing.
Diversification Spreading your money across different investments so that one bad pick does not ruin your whole portfolio. Like not putting all your eggs in one basket.
Drawdown How much an investment drops from its highest point to its lowest point before recovering. A 30% drawdown means you temporarily lost 30% of your peak value.
Emergency Fund 3-6 months of living expenses kept in a safe, liquid account (savings account). This must be fully funded BEFORE you invest in the stock market.
House Money A strategy where you sell enough of a winning position to recover your original investment, then let the remaining shares (pure profit) ride risk-free.
Martingale A dangerous strategy of doubling your bet after every loss, believing the next one "has to" be a winner. It comes from gambling and has bankrupted countless traders.
Overconfidence Bias A psychological tendency to overestimate your own ability, especially after a winning streak. Studies show it leads to excessive trading and lower returns.
Panic Selling Selling everything in fear during a market crash, locking in your losses and missing the eventual recovery. The worst time to sell is when everyone is selling.
Position Sizing Deciding how much money to put into each trade. The golden rule for beginners: risk no more than 1% of your total portfolio on any single trade.
Rebalancing Periodically adjusting your portfolio back to your target allocation. If one stock grew too large, you sell some and redistribute. Usually done quarterly or annually.
Revenge Trading Making impulsive, oversized trades after a loss in an attempt to quickly recover the lost money. Like a poker player on tilt. Almost always makes things worse.
Stop-Loss An automatic order that sells your stock if it drops below a certain price. Like a safety net. A trailing stop-loss follows the price up but protects you on the way down.
Sunk Cost Fallacy Holding a bad investment just because you already lost money on it. "I can't sell now, I'm already down 40%." The money is gone whether you sell or not. The question is: would you buy it today?
Trading Journal A record of every trade you make, including what you did, why, and how you felt. The most powerful self-improvement tool for investors. Reveals patterns you cannot see in real time.
Trailing Stop-Loss A stop-loss that moves up with the stock price but never moves down. If you set a 15% trailing stop, it will sell the stock if it drops 15% from its highest point since you bought it.
Unrealized Gains/Losses Profits or losses that exist on paper but have not been "locked in" by selling. An unrealized gain of $5,000 means your investment is currently worth $5,000 more than you paid, but you have not sold yet.
