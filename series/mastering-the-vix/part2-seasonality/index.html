The September-October Effect

September and October are consistently the highest-VIX months of the year. This is not superstition — it is driven by three structural forces: (1) mutual fund fiscal year-ends in October force portfolio rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting, (2) Q3 earnings approach with maximum uncertainty, and (3) hedge funds return from summer and aggressively reposition. The "October crash" reputation (1929, 1987, 2008) further amplifies the fear premium, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where traders buy more protection precisely because they expect October to be volatile.

The practical takeaway: protection bought in July is structurally cheap. Protection bought in September is structurally expensive. Smart institutional desks roll hedges forward during summer doldrums, capturing 3-5 VIX points of cheapness before the fall vol spike materializes.

Weekly VIX Patterns

The Day-of-Week Effect

VIX seasonality is not just a monthly phenomenon — it operates on a weekly cycle as well. The day of the week you enter or exit a volatility position matters, and the data shows a consistent pattern that has persisted across market regimes.

Monday: The Weekend Gap Premium
VIX tends to be elevated on Mondays due to weekend risk premium

Markets are closed for ~63 hours over the weekend. During that time, geopolitical events, natural disasters, corporate scandals, and policy announcements can occur without the market being able to reprice. This weekend gap risk systematically inflates implied volatility on Monday mornings. Traders buying Friday afternoon protection pay less than those buying Monday morning protection for the same calendar coverage — the weekend risk premium averages 0.3-0.5 VIX points.

  • Average Monday VIX change: +0.35 points from Friday close
  • 60% of Mondays show higher VIX than Friday close
  • Selling vol Monday AM captures weekend premium decay
Tuesday-Wednesday: The Midweek Compression
VIX typically declines as weekend risk evaporates and no immediate catalysts loom

Once Monday's gap risk has been priced and no immediate event looms, implied volatility tends to compress. Tuesday and Wednesday are statistically the lowest-anxiety days of the trading week. Systematic vol sellers have historically found these the best entry days for short premium positions, as the weekend risk premium has decayed but Friday's OpEx gamma effects have not yet begun.

  • Average Tuesday VIX change: -0.22 points
  • Average Wednesday VIX change: -0.18 points
  • Best days for entering short volatility positions
Thursday-Friday: OpEx Positioning
Gamma effects dominate as weekly/monthly options approach expiration

Thursday sees positioning activity for Friday's weekly options expiration. Market makers begin hedging their gamma exposure, which can create directional moves that either amplify or suppress realized volatility. Friday is dominated by OpEx mechanics — with 0DTE options now accounting for over 40% of SPX options volume, the Friday close has become increasingly influenced by gamma unwinding and dealer hedging flows.

  • Average Thursday VIX change: +0.08 points (neutral)
  • Average Friday VIX change: -0.15 points (OpEx compression)
  • OpEx weeks vs non-OpEx weeks: Friday VIX drop is 2x larger on monthly OpEx

The 0DTE Revolution

Since 2022, the explosion of 0DTE (zero days to expiration) SPX options has fundamentally altered intraday VIX patterns. With daily expirations available Monday through Friday, every day is now a mini-OpEx. The traditional weekly cycle is becoming less pronounced as gamma effects occur daily rather than concentrating on Fridays. This does not eliminate the weekly pattern, but it compresses it. The Monday gap premium and midweek compression persist, but the Friday OpEx effect is spreading across the entire week.

The FOMC Cycle

The Most Predictable VIX Pattern

If there is one VIX seasonal pattern that has been most extensively studied and most reliably exploited by institutional traders, it is the FOMC cycle. The Federal Reserve meets 8 times per year on predetermined dates, and the VIX behavior around these meetings follows a remarkably consistent script.

The FOMC VIX Script

T-5 to T+5: A predictable arc of fear and relief

T-5 to T-1: The Fear Build
VIX rises 2-3 points as uncertainty premium builds before the announcement

In the 5 trading days before an FOMC meeting, the VIX systematically rises. This is not because traders expect bad news — it is because they are pricing in uncertainty. The Fed could raise, cut, or hold rates. The dot plot could shift. Powell's press conference could introduce new language. This uncertainty is real and measurable, and it gets priced into options as an "FOMC premium."

The rise is not linear. It typically accelerates in the final 2 days (T-2 and T-1) as traders who procrastinated on hedging rush to buy protection. This creates a vol squeeze effect where put demand spikes, driving VIX higher even if the S&P 500 is flat.

T+0 (2:00 PM ET): The Crush
VIX drops 1.5-2 points in minutes as uncertainty resolves

At precisely 2:00 PM Eastern on FOMC Wednesday, the Fed releases its statement. In the first 30 minutes after the announcement, the VIX drops an average of 1.5 points regardless of what the Fed actually says. This is the "uncertainty premium removal" — the volatility that was priced for the unknown has been resolved, and the options market rapidly reprices.

Even on hawkish surprises, the VIX often drops initially because the resolution of uncertainty is inherently vol-compressing. The VIX may later rise if the market decides the outcome is genuinely bearish, but the initial reaction is almost always a crush.

T+1 to T+5: The FOMC Drift
Stocks tend to rally and VIX tends to remain compressed for several days after FOMC

One of the most studied anomalies in market microstructure is the "FOMC drift" — the tendency for stocks to rally in the days following an FOMC meeting. Research by Lucca and Moench (2015) at the New York Fed found that a significant portion of the equity risk premium over the past 30 years has accrued in the 24 hours around FOMC announcements. The VIX counterpart of this drift is a sustained compression that lasts 3-5 trading days post-FOMC.

Why the FOMC Pattern Is So Reliable

The FOMC pattern persists because it is driven by structural market mechanics, not behavioral bias. Before FOMC, hedgers must buy protection because of risk management mandates — fund prospectuses, bank capital requirements, and fiduciary obligations force protective put buying. After FOMC, those same hedgers can roll off protection because the event has passed. This is not a choice — it is a compliance requirement. As long as institutional risk management rules exist, the FOMC VIX cycle will persist. This is what makes it arguably the single most reliable seasonal pattern in all of volatility trading.

When the Pattern Breaks

The FOMC VIX pattern works most of the time, but it fails spectacularly during regime shifts. When the Fed signals a major policy pivot (taper tantrum 2013, emergency rate cuts 2020, aggressive hiking cycle 2022), the post-FOMC VIX crush can reverse into a sustained VIX spike. The pattern also weakens during inter-meeting actions — emergency meetings or unscheduled interventions break the calendar regularity. Always check whether the upcoming FOMC is expected to be a "pivot meeting" before deploying FOMC-based vol trades.

Options Expiration Effects

The OpEx Gamma Machine

Options expiration is the mechanical heartbeat of the volatility market. Every month, trillions of dollars in options contracts expire, forcing dealers to unwind hedges, gamma to reset, and implied volatility to reprice. Understanding the OpEx cycle is essential for any volatility trader.

Monthly OpEx (3rd Friday)
The single most important date on the volatility calendar

Standard monthly options expire on the third Friday of each month. In the days leading up to OpEx, several mechanical forces converge:

  • Gamma compression: As expiration approaches, near-the-money options develop extreme gamma. Dealers who are long gamma (typical in a positive GEX environment) dampen market moves by buying dips and selling rips, compressing realized volatility and dragging the VIX lower.
  • Theta decay acceleration: Time decay goes parabolic in the final 48 hours before expiration. Short premium positions profit rapidly, reducing demand for new protection and further compressing VIX.
  • Pin risk: Large open interest at round strikes can "pin" the market to those levels on expiration day, creating artificially low realized vol.

The net result: VIX typically drops into OpEx. On average, VIX declines 0.5-1.0 points in the 3 days leading to monthly expiration. But watch the Monday after OpEx — with the gamma overhang removed, the market is "unanchored" and can make larger directional moves, often spiking VIX.

Quad Witching (Quarterly OpEx)
Four types of contracts expire simultaneously — March, June, September, December

Quarterly "quad witching" is monthly OpEx on steroids. On these days, stock options, stock index options, stock index futures, and single stock futures all expire simultaneously. The notional value expiring can exceed $5 trillion, creating massive rebalancing flows.

  • Volume spikes 40-60% above normal monthly OpEx
  • Last-hour volatility can be 2-3x normal as portfolio managers roll positions
  • VIX term structure distortion: near-term VIX can spike while VIX3M remains stable
  • Post-quad-witching week historically shows elevated VIX (+0.8 pts avg) as new gamma rebuilds

The 0DTE Factor

The introduction of daily SPX expirations has created a new paradigm. With 0DTE options now accounting for over 40% of total SPX options volume, the traditional OpEx cycle is being supplemented by a daily micro-cycle. Every day, billions in 0DTE options expire at 4:00 PM ET, creating intraday gamma effects that used to only occur on monthly OpEx Fridays. For VIX traders, this means: (1) intraday VIX moves are increasingly driven by 0DTE gamma flips, (2) the traditional monthly OpEx VIX compression pattern is slightly diminished but still present, and (3) new opportunities exist in trading intraday VIX patterns tied to 0DTE flows.

Earnings Season Volatility

The Earnings Uncertainty Premium

Four times a year, corporate America opens its books. Earnings season is the single largest source of idiosyncratic uncertainty in the equity market, and it has a measurable, predictable impact on the VIX. The pattern is simple: VIX rises in the 2-3 weeks before earnings season begins, then declines as individual companies report and uncertainty resolves.

The Mega-Cap Effect
AAPL, MSFT, AMZN, GOOG, META collectively move the VIX

The five largest US companies by market cap represent over 25% of the S&P 500's weight. When these companies report earnings (typically in the last week of January, April, July, and October), the VIX is directly affected by their individual outcomes. A single company — Apple or Microsoft — can move the SPX by 1-2% on earnings, which translates to a 1-2 point VIX move.

Critically, the aggregate earnings risk premium for mega-cap tech week is 1-3 VIX points above what would be expected from macro conditions alone. This premium builds during the 5 days before the first mega-cap report and dissipates over 2-3 days after the last one. Smart volatility traders specifically target this window.

Why Earnings Season Moves VIX Even If You Do Not Trade Individual Stocks

You might think: "I only trade SPX options, not individual stocks — why should I care about earnings season?" The answer is correlation. During earnings season, implied correlation (measured by the CBOE Implied Correlation Index) typically drops because individual stocks are moving on company-specific news rather than macro themes. Lower implied correlation means index implied volatility (VIX) drops relative to individual stock implied volatility — even if the VIX level stays elevated in absolute terms. This creates opportunities in dispersion trades (long single-stock vol, short index vol) that are only available during earnings season.

The Earnings-FOMC Collision

Some of the highest VIX readings in non-crisis markets occur when mega-cap earnings week coincides with an FOMC meeting. This dual uncertainty creates a compounding premium — both the FOMC fear build and the earnings uncertainty premium stack on top of each other. When this happens (roughly 2-3 times per year), the VIX premium can be 3-5 points above equilibrium, making it one of the most attractive environments for short premium strategies after both events resolve.

Notable Anomalies

When the Calendar Breaks

Seasonal patterns are statistical tendencies, not laws of physics. Understanding when and why they break is just as important as understanding the patterns themselves. Here are the most instructive VIX anomalies that every volatility trader should study.

Volmageddon — February 5, 2018
The day a volatility product broke the market

The VIX had spent most of 2017 below 12, lulling an entire generation of traders into believing volatility was permanently dead. Inverse volatility products — most notably the XIV (VelocityShares Daily Inverse VIX Short-Term ETN) — had attracted billions from retail traders running the "short vol" strategy as a passive income trade.

On February 5, 2018, the VIX spiked from 17 to 50 in a single day — a +116% move. The XIV lost 96% of its value in after-hours trading and was liquidated the next day. Credit Suisse terminated the product entirely. Over $2 billion in investor wealth was destroyed in hours. The cause? A feedback loop: as the VIX rose, inverse vol products had to buy VIX futures to rebalance, pushing VIX higher, forcing more buying, in a death spiral.

Lesson: Seasonality told you February 2018 was a "rising VIX" month (February is structurally higher than January). The pattern was right. But the magnitude was 10x what seasonality predicted because of a structural vulnerability in the product ecosystem. Seasonal patterns give you direction and timing; they do not cap magnitude.

COVID — March 16, 2020
VIX 82.69: The highest closing reading in history

The COVID crash was the fastest bear market in history. The S&P 500 fell 34% in 23 trading days. The VIX reached 82.69 on March 16, 2020, surpassing the 2008 financial crisis peak of 80.86. Seasonality would have told you March is a moderately elevated VIX month (average 18.5). The actual reading was 4.5x the seasonal average.

Lesson: Seasonal patterns operate within a "normal" regime. Exogenous shocks (pandemics, wars, financial system collapses) create regime breaks where historical averages become meaningless. The key is to size positions based on the assumption that seasonal patterns work 80% of the time, not 100%.

2017: The Ultra-Low VIX Anomaly
52 days below VIX 10 — more than the previous 27 years combined

2017 was the mirror image of a VIX spike — a prolonged vol suppression event. The VIX closed below 10 on 52 trading days, compared to just 9 days total in the prior 27 years. The standard seasonal cycle — summer low, fall high — was almost completely flattened. September 2017 VIX averaged just 11.2 vs the historical September average of 20.8.

Lesson: Low-volatility regimes can persist far longer than models predict. The seasonal pattern was directionally correct (September was the highest VIX month of 2017) but the absolute level was compressed by macro conditions (strong earnings, low rates, synchronized global growth, no geopolitical shocks). Seasonality gives you relative positioning, not absolute levels.

Election Year Volatility
Presidential elections create a unique VIX pattern with a pre-election build and post-election crush

Every four years, the US presidential election injects a unique volatility pattern. The VIX typically rises 3-5 points in October of election years (above the already-elevated seasonal October average), as options traders price the binary outcome risk of Election Day. After the election is called, VIX typically drops 4-6 points in a massive uncertainty removal event — regardless of which party wins.

The 2020 election was a notable exception: the VIX remained elevated post-election due to contested results and delayed vote counting. The uncertainty removal only happened when the electoral outcome became clear, not on Election Night.

Key Dates Every VIX Trader Should Know

  • Oct 19, 1987 — Black Monday. DJIA -22.6% in one day. VIX equivalent estimated at 150+.
  • Oct 15, 2008 — Financial crisis peak. VIX closed at 69.95 (intraday 81.65).
  • Aug 24, 2015 — "Flash Crash 2.0." VIX opened at 53, closed at 40. China devaluation fears.
  • Feb 5, 2018 — Volmageddon. VIX +116% intraday. XIV liquidated.
  • Mar 16, 2020 — COVID peak. VIX 82.69, highest closing reading ever.
  • Jan 27, 2021 — GameStop squeeze. VIX spiked to 37 on retail-driven single-stock vol.
  • Aug 5, 2024 — Japan carry-trade unwind. VIX spiked to 65 intraday, reversed to 27 same day.

Black Swans vs Grey Swans

Nassim Taleb's distinction matters for seasonality traders. A Black Swan is an event that is both unpredictable and unprecedented — COVID, 9/11. Seasonal patterns cannot help with these. A Grey Swan is an event that is known to be possible but whose timing is uncertain — a banking crisis, a credit event, a geopolitical escalation. Grey Swans tend to amplify existing seasonal patterns rather than override them. The 2008 financial crisis peaked in October — the historically worst month. The 2018 Volmageddon happened in February — a seasonally rising-VIX month. Grey Swans often ride the seasonal current. This means seasonal high-VIX periods are not just "expensive" for protection buyers — they are also the periods when catastrophic VIX spikes are most likely to occur.

The Seasonality Playbook

Putting It All Together

Now that you understand the monthly cycle, the weekly cycle, the FOMC cycle, OpEx mechanics, and earnings season effects, how do you actually use this knowledge? Here is a month-by-month playbook for volatility traders.

Q1: January-March
Transition from low-vol year-end to rising spring volatility
  • January: VIX starts low (16.8 avg). Good month to buy cheap protection for the year. Roll year-end hedges into Q1.
  • February: VIX rising (18.2 avg). Q4 earnings resolving. Watch for Volmageddon-type tail risks. Reduce short vol exposure.
  • March: Quarter-end rebalancing drives vol (18.5 avg). Quad witching in March. Good month for calendar spreads (short March, long April).
Q2: April-June
Earnings resolution into summer compression
  • April: Tax selling creates vol pockets (17.4 avg). Earnings season begins. Play the FOMC cycle if meeting falls in April/May.
  • May: "Sell in May" is partially a vol-buying phenomenon (17.8 avg). Institutions buy summer hedges. Good time to sell straddles with May expiration.
  • June: Summer begins, VIX drops (16.5 avg). Quad witching creates a brief June vol spike then compression. Start accumulating September/October hedges while they are cheap.
Q3: July-September
Summer doldrums into the most dangerous quarter
  • July: Peak cheapness for volatility (15.2 avg). Best month to buy protection for fall. VIX puts are cheap, VIX calls are cheap. Load up on September/October hedges.
  • August: Still low VIX (16.1 avg) but beware August surprises. Thinly-traded markets amplify moves. Good month for risk-defined short vol (iron condors) but keep hedges in place.
  • September: Danger zone (20.8 avg). Do not sell naked vol in September. If you accumulated July/August hedges, they start paying off. Play defensive.
Q4: October-December
Peak volatility into year-end compression
  • October: Historically highest VIX (21.4 avg). Protection is expensive but necessary. Consider selling premium into the November crush — but only risk-defined (spreads, not naked).
  • November: Post-election (every 4 years) or post-October normalization (17.6 avg). VIX drops as year-end rally positioning begins. Good month for short vol structures with December expiration.
  • December: Year-end rally compresses VIX (16.2 avg). Santa rally effect is real. Start buying January protection while December VIX is low. Quad witching creates a brief December spike then final compression.

Monthly Strategy Summary

  • Buy Protection When Cheap: July, August, December, January. VIX is structurally depressed. Hedge costs are minimized.
  • Sell Vol When Expensive: September, October (post-spike), FOMC days. VIX premium is structurally elevated. Short premium expected value is highest.
  • Play the FOMC Cycle: Buy VIX T-5, sell T+0. 78% win rate over 15 years. The most reliable single trade in volatility.
  • Respect Earnings Season: Do not sell vol into mega-cap earnings week. Wait for post-earnings VIX crush.
  • OpEx Compression: VIX drops into monthly OpEx (3rd Friday). VIX spikes after quarterly OpEx (quad witching). Trade accordingly.
  • Size for Tail Risk: Seasonal patterns work 80% of the time. The other 20% includes Volmageddon and COVID. Never risk more than you can afford to lose on a "seasonality says it should be fine" thesis.

The Meta-Rule

Seasonality is a second-order signal, not a primary signal. It tells you when vol is cheap or expensive relative to its own calendar cycle, not whether the overall regime is bullish or bearish for volatility. A VIX of 18 in July (above seasonal average) is more concerning than a VIX of 18 in October (below seasonal average). Always combine seasonality with regime analysis (Part 3), term structure analysis (Part 4), and positioning data (Part 5) before putting on a trade.
