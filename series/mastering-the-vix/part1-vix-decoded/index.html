What Is the VIX?

Not a Fear Gauge — An Expectations Meter

Every financial news anchor calls the VIX the "fear index." It makes for a good headline, but it is misleading. The VIX does not measure fear. It does not measure panic. It does not even measure current volatility. The VIX measures the market's expectation of how volatile the S&P 500 will be over the next 30 calendar days.

More precisely, the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX) is derived from the prices of S&P 500 index options (SPX). When option traders collectively pay more for options — because they expect bigger moves ahead — the VIX rises. When they pay less, the VIX falls. It is a forward-looking measure of implied volatility, not a backward-looking measure of realized volatility.

The Key Distinction

Implied Volatility (IV) is what the market expects to happen. It is derived from option prices. Realized Volatility (RV) is what actually happened. It is calculated from historical price movements. The VIX measures IV. The two often diverge — and that divergence is tradeable. We will explore this in depth in the "Implied vs Realized" section below.

The VIX was created by the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) in 1993, based on research by Professor Robert Whaley of Vanderbilt University. The original VIX (now called VXO) was based on S&P 100 (OEX) options and used only eight at-the-money strikes. In 2003, the CBOE redesigned the index entirely:

  • Switched to S&P 500 (SPX) options — much deeper and more liquid market
  • Uses a wide strip of out-of-the-money strikes — captures the entire implied volatility surface, not just ATM
  • Model-free calculation — no Black-Scholes assumptions required
  • Based on variance swap pricing — theoretically more robust

The media branded it the "fear index" because VIX spikes tend to coincide with market selloffs. But correlation is not causation. The VIX also rises before anticipated events like FOMC meetings, elections, and earnings seasons — situations where uncertainty is high but fear may be absent. A better name would be the "uncertainty index" or the "expected move index."

Understanding this distinction — that the VIX is an expectation, not a measurement — is the foundation for everything in this series. When you see "VIX at 25," you are not seeing fear. You are seeing what options traders collectively believe the S&P 500 will do over the next month. And as we will learn, those traders are systematically wrong in a very specific, very profitable way.

How the VIX Is Calculated

The Math Behind the Number

You do not need a PhD to understand VIX calculation. The core idea is elegant: aggregate the prices of many SPX options across different strike prices to extract the market's expected variance, then take the square root to get volatility.

Here is the process in four steps:

Step 1: Select the Options
CBOE uses two SPX option expirations that bracket 30 days

The VIX always looks at two sets of SPX options: one expiring just before 30 calendar days from now (near-term) and one expiring just after (next-term). It interpolates between them to target exactly 30 days. Only out-of-the-money (OTM) options are used — puts below the forward price, calls above it. This avoids double-counting intrinsic value.

Step 2: Weight by Strike
Each option contributes proportionally to the strip

Each OTM option's price is weighted by the distance between its strike and the adjacent strikes (delta-K). Options that are deep OTM contribute less because they are cheaper, but they still contribute. This is critical: deep OTM puts — crash protection — have a disproportionate effect on the VIX when their prices spike. This is why the VIX rises more on selloffs than it falls on rallies.

Step 3: Calculate Variance
Sum the weighted option prices to get expected variance

The weighted strip of option prices is summed and adjusted by the risk-free rate and forward price. The result is the expected variance of the S&P 500 over 30 days. This is essentially the fair price of a variance swap — a derivative that pays the difference between realized and implied variance.

Step 4: Annualize and Square Root
Convert 30-day variance to annualized volatility

Take the square root of the expected variance to get standard deviation (volatility). Then annualize it by multiplying by the square root of 365/30. The result is the VIX — an annualized percentage representing expected volatility.

What Does a VIX Number Actually Mean?

The VIX is quoted in annualized percentage points. To convert it to expected daily moves, you divide by the square root of the number of trading days in a year (252):

These Are 1-Standard-Deviation Moves

A VIX of 20 means the market expects SPX to move ±1.26% on a given day with ~68% probability (one sigma). The actual move will exceed this roughly one-third of the time. For risk management, multiply by 2 for a ~95% confidence interval, or by 3 for ~99.7%. This is why a VIX of 80 during COVID did not mean "the market will move 5% daily" — it meant there was a 68% chance of a 5% move, and a 32% chance of something even larger.

One of the most important properties of the VIX calculation is its asymmetry. Because it uses OTM options, and because OTM puts are typically more expensive than equidistant OTM calls (the "volatility skew"), the VIX is naturally biased upward when downside risk increases. The market charges a premium for crash protection, and the VIX reflects that premium. This is why the VIX moves more on 3% down days than 3% up days.

VIX Levels Decoded

What Each VIX Level Tells You

Not all VIX readings are created equal. A VIX of 18 in January 2020 meant something very different from a VIX of 18 in April 2020 (coming down from 82). Context matters. But as a general framework, here is how to interpret the major VIX regimes:

The VIX Mean Reversion Property

The single most important statistical property of the VIX is its mean-reverting nature. Unlike stock prices, which can trend indefinitely, the VIX always returns to its long-term average (historically around 19-20). High VIX readings will come down. Low VIX readings will spike. The question is always when and how fast. This mean-reverting property is the foundation of most volatility trading strategies, which we will explore in Parts 4 and 5.

The chart above shows the VIX from 2020 to 2026. Notice how every spike is followed by a decline back toward the 15-22 range. The COVID spike to 82 collapsed to 25 within 6 weeks. The 2022 bear market kept the VIX elevated (20-35) for months, but it eventually normalized. The 2025 tariff-driven spike receded as markets priced in the new reality. Mean reversion is reliable; its timing is not.

The VIX Term Structure

Beyond the Headline Number

The single VIX number you see on CNBC is just the tip of the iceberg. Beneath it lies the VIX term structure — a curve that shows expected volatility at different time horizons. Understanding this curve is what separates tourists from professionals in the volatility space.

The CBOE publishes several volatility indices with different time horizons:

Index Horizon Description Primary Use
VIX9D 9 days Near-term expected vol. Extremely sensitive to imminent events (FOMC, CPI, earnings). Event-driven trading, gamma scalping
VIX 30 days The standard VIX. Benchmark for volatility expectations. Headline risk gauge, VIX futures, options strategies
VIX3M 3 months Medium-term expected vol. More stable, less event-driven. Term structure analysis, VIX/VIX3M ratio
VIX6M 6 months Long-term expected vol. Reflects structural uncertainty. Macro positioning, long-dated hedging
VIX1Y (VXV) 12 months One-year expected vol. The most stable point on the curve. Strategic allocation, long-term risk budgeting

The relationship between these indices creates two fundamental regimes:

Contango vs Backwardation

The two states of the VIX term structure that define your volatility strategy

Contango (Normal State)
Futures prices ABOVE spot VIX — market expects calm now, uncertainty later

In a normal market, the term structure slopes upward: VIX9D < VIX < VIX3M < VIX6M. This makes intuitive sense — the further out you look, the more uncertainty there is. You know roughly what will happen this week, but six months from now? Anything could happen.

Contango is the dominant state. The VIX term structure is in contango approximately 80-85% of the time. This is the "normal" state of the world. VIX futures roll down the curve as they approach expiration, losing value. This is why products like VXX (which hold rolling front-month VIX futures) bleed value over time — they are constantly buying expensive futures and selling them cheaper as they approach expiry.

  • Market is calm — no immediate crisis or major event
  • Short vol strategies work well — selling VIX futures or credit spreads profits from roll-down
  • VXX/UVXY decay — long volatility ETPs lose money from negative roll yield
  • Risk: complacency — extended contango often precedes violent VIX spikes
Backwardation (Crisis State)
Spot VIX ABOVE futures — panic NOW, expectation it will subside

When the term structure inverts — VIX9D > VIX > VIX3M > VIX6M — you have backwardation. The market is saying: "Things are terrible right now, but we expect them to calm down eventually." This is the signature of acute fear.

Backwardation is rare and short-lived. It occurs only about 15-20% of the time, almost exclusively during market selloffs. It is the single best objective signal that the market is in genuine distress (as opposed to just being nervous). Historical studies show that buying SPX when the VIX term structure is in steep backwardation has produced strong positive returns on 3-6 month horizons.

  • Active crisis — selling, margin calls, forced liquidation
  • Short vol strategies get destroyed — negative gamma, exploding losses
  • VXX/UVXY surge — positive roll yield amplifies long vol positions
  • Contrarian signal — backwardation > 5pts has historically signaled bottoms

The VIX/VIX3M Ratio: A Simple but Powerful Signal

One of the most useful term structure metrics is the VIX/VIX3M ratio. When VIX < VIX3M (ratio below 1.0), the market is calm and contango prevails. When VIX > VIX3M (ratio above 1.0), backwardation is setting in and stress is elevated. A ratio above 1.10 has historically coincided with major market bottoms approximately 70% of the time within 1-3 months. This ratio is freely available and updated in real time — no expensive data feed required.

Contango is Not Safety

The most dangerous VIX environment is deep, extended contango — when the curve is steep and has been stable for months. This creates two problems: (1) vol-selling strategies attract more capital, crowding the trade, and (2) the market becomes complacent, building leverage and reducing hedges. The Volmageddon event of February 5, 2018 happened after 14 months of uninterrupted contango. The VIX went from 14 to 50 in a single day, destroying several volatility ETPs and billions of dollars in short-vol capital.

Implied vs Realized Volatility

The Volatility Risk Premium

Here is the single most profitable fact in all of volatility trading: the VIX consistently overestimates actual realized volatility. This is not a bug — it is a structural feature of the market, and it has persisted for decades.

On average, the VIX trades 2-4 points above subsequent 30-day realized volatility. If the VIX is at 20, the S&P 500 typically realizes about 16-18% annualized volatility over the following month. This gap is called the Volatility Risk Premium (VRP).

Why Does the VRP Exist?

The VRP exists for the same reason insurance premiums exceed expected losses: risk aversion. Option buyers (hedgers) are willing to pay a premium for protection, just as homeowners pay more for fire insurance than the expected cost of fires. Option sellers (dealers, hedge funds) demand a premium for bearing the risk of sudden, large moves. This creates a structural overpayment for options, which the VIX reflects.

The VRP is the reason why selling options is profitable over the long term. You are harvesting the insurance premium. Of course, this works until it does not — the premium is compensation for the risk of catastrophic loss during tail events. This is the classic "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller" trade, and managing its risks is the subject of Part 5.

The key insight is that the VRP is positive most of the time but deeply negative during crises. When the VIX underestimates realized volatility — when actual moves exceed expectations — is precisely when selling vol blows up. This is why position sizing and tail risk hedging are critical for any volatility strategy.

The chart above illustrates the VRP over time. The blue area (VIX implied) almost always sits above the orange area (realized volatility). The gap between them is your premium — or your loss during extreme events. Notice how the premium collapses and inverts during the 2020 crash: the VIX at 60-80 was actually underpricing the realized moves, which exceeded 10% daily.

Do Not Confuse VRP With Free Money

The VRP is real, persistent, and well-documented in academic literature (Carr & Wu 2009, Bollerslev et al. 2009). But harvesting it requires:
(1) Sufficient capital to survive drawdowns (the VRP can be -10 or worse during crises).
(2) Position sizing discipline — no single trade should risk more than 2-3% of capital.
(3) Tail hedging — owning cheap OTM puts to survive Black Swan events.
We will cover practical VRP harvesting strategies in detail in Part 5.

5 VIX Myths Debunked

What the VIX Does NOT Do

The VIX is one of the most misunderstood indicators in finance. Here are five myths that even experienced traders believe, and why they are wrong.

1
"The VIX Predicts Crashes"
False

The VIX does not predict anything. It reacts to option prices, which in turn react to market moves and positioning. Before every major crash in history — 2008, 2020, the August 2015 flash crash — the VIX was at normal or low levels. It did not warn you. It spiked after the selling began.

A better predictive signal is the shape of the term structure or the VVIX (the volatility of VIX itself). But even these are anticipatory signals, not crystal balls. The VIX is a thermometer, not a weather forecast: it tells you the temperature right now, not what it will be tomorrow.

2
"High VIX = Always Buy the Dip"
Oversimplified

The idea that you should "buy when the VIX spikes" has some statistical merit on longer time horizons (6-12 months), but it is dangerous as a short-term trading rule. During the 2008 GFC, the VIX first hit 40 in September. If you bought then, SPX fell another 40% over the next six months. The VIX can stay elevated for weeks or months.

A better approach: Wait for the VIX to start declining from elevated levels (i.e., the rate of change turns negative). Historically, buying SPX 5-10 days after a VIX spike peak — not at the peak — has produced significantly better risk-adjusted returns.

3
"The VIX Can't Go Below 10"
False

The VIX hit 9.14 on November 3, 2017, its all-time closing low. Throughout late 2017, the VIX spent weeks below 10. This was driven by historically low hedging demand, massive short-vol strategies, and extremely tight realized volatility in SPX.

The lesson: there is no theoretical floor for the VIX. If option prices are sufficiently cheap, the VIX can go as low as the math allows. But ultra-low VIX readings are inherently unstable — they attract short-vol capital until the crowding triggers a violent unwind. The February 2018 Volmageddon was the direct consequence of the 2017 VIX compression.

4
"You Can Buy the VIX"
False

You cannot buy or sell "the VIX." The VIX is a calculated index — there are no shares, no ETF, no fund that tracks the VIX spot price. What you can trade:

  • VIX Futures — the closest proxy, but they track expected future VIX, not spot VIX
  • VIX Options — options on VIX futures (not on spot VIX), European-style, cash-settled
  • VIX ETPs — VXX, UVXY, SVXY — track rolling VIX futures, with significant tracking error and roll costs
  • SPX Options — the underlying instruments from which VIX is derived

Each of these instruments has its own dynamics, costs, and risks. A VIX future can expire at a very different level than where spot VIX is on expiration day. We will deep-dive into trading these instruments in Part 4.

5
"VIX and SPX Always Move in Opposite Directions"
Mostly True, But Not Always

The correlation between daily VIX changes and SPX returns is approximately -0.80. That is strongly negative, but it is not -1.0. There are days when both VIX and SPX rise simultaneously — typically before a major event (FOMC, elections) when the market rallies but option demand increases. And there are days when both fall — after an anticipated event passes and vol crush occurs alongside a flat or slightly negative SPX.

The asymmetry is also notable: VIX rises more on SPX down days than it falls on equivalent SPX up days. A 2% SPX decline might push VIX up 4-5 points, but a 2% SPX rally might only pull VIX down 2-3 points. This is because the volatility skew — OTM puts being more expensive than equidistant OTM calls — amplifies downside VIX moves.

Your Daily VIX Checklist

Before the market opens, check these four things in under 60 seconds:

  1. VIX level — which regime are we in? (Complacency, Normal, Elevated, Crisis)
  2. VIX/VIX3M ratio — is the term structure in contango or backwardation?
  3. VIX direction — is VIX rising or falling? (Rising VIX = tighten stops, reduce size. Falling VIX = expand stops, look for opportunities.)
  4. VVIX — is the "vol of vol" elevated? (High VVIX = expect VIX itself to make a large move today, in either direction.)

This 60-second check gives you a complete read on the volatility landscape. It should be as automatic as checking the weather before leaving the house.
