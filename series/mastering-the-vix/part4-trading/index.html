VIX Futures

The Foundation of VIX Trading

VIX futures are the bedrock of the entire VIX trading ecosystem. Every VIX ETP, every VIX strategy, every institutional vol trade ultimately flows through the VIX futures market. If you understand futures, the rest falls into place. If you do not, everything else is a black box.

A VIX future is a bet on where VIX will be at expiration, not where it is right now. This is the single most important concept: VIX futures converge to VIX spot at expiration, but before expiration, they trade at a premium or discount to spot.

Front Month vs. Back Months
Understanding the term structure is the key to everything

At any given time, multiple VIX futures contracts are trading simultaneously, each expiring in a different month. The front month (nearest expiration) is the most liquid and tracks VIX spot most closely. The back months reflect the market's expectation of future volatility and are typically priced higher than the front month.

This price relationship across expirations is called the term structure. It has two possible shapes:

  • Contango (normal, ~80% of the time) — Back months priced HIGHER than front month. The market expects higher volatility in the future. Long VIX futures positions lose money from roll.
  • Backwardation (crisis, ~20% of the time) — Back months priced LOWER than front month. The market is in panic — current vol is extreme, and the market expects it to decrease. Long VIX futures positions gain from roll.

Roll Yield

The silent killer — or silent earner — of every VIX position

VIX futures expire. To maintain a continuous position, you must roll: sell the expiring contract and buy the next month. This rolling process has a cost or benefit called roll yield.

Roll Yield Mathematics

Suppose VIX spot is at 14. The front-month future (M1) trades at 15.50 and the second-month future (M2) at 17.00.

Contango roll cost = (M2 - M1) / M1 = (17.00 - 15.50) / 15.50 = 9.7% per month

If you are holding a long position and must roll from M1 to M2, you sell at 15.50 and buy at 17.00, paying a 1.50-point premium ($1,500 per contract). Over a year of continuous rolling, this compounding negative carry typically erodes 50-70% of the position’s value.

The opposite occurs in backwardation: rolling from a higher-priced M1 to a cheaper M2 generates positive carry. This is why short vol strategies are profitable in calm markets and long vol strategies are expensive to maintain.

Convergence is the mechanism: as expiration approaches, the futures price is pulled toward VIX spot. In contango, this means the futures price declines toward spot even if VIX spot doesn't move. For a long futures holder, this convergence is pure loss. For a short futures holder, it is pure profit — until the next VIX spike turns it into catastrophic loss.

Why VIX Futures Are Not a "Buy and Hold"
The math is brutal for directional long-vol positions

Consider a trader who was "right" about VIX direction: VIX spot moved from 14 to 16 (+14%) over two months. But their front-month futures position rolled twice, paying ~10% each time in contango costs. Net result: 14% gain - 20% roll cost = -6% loss. Being right on direction and wrong on timing is the defining tragedy of VIX futures trading.

The only scenario where long VIX futures work consistently is during a sudden, violent spike where the 30-50% single-day move overwhelms the roll cost. Think March 2020 (+400%) or August 2015 (+135%). These events are, by definition, rare and unpredictable.

VIX ETPs Explained

Exchange-Traded Products for Volatility

VIX ETPs are the gateway through which most retail traders access the volatility market. They package VIX futures exposure into a ticker symbol you can buy and sell like a stock. But this convenience comes with a devastating cost: structural decay that makes almost every long VIX ETP a guaranteed loser over time.

How VIX ETPs Work

Daily-rebalanced portfolios of VIX futures — and why that matters

A VIX ETP like VXX holds a rolling portfolio of front-month and second-month VIX futures. Each day, it sells a fraction of M1 futures and buys a corresponding fraction of M2 futures to maintain a constant weighted-average maturity of approximately 30 days. This daily rebalancing is where the decay originates.

The Contango Decay Problem
Why holding VXX for months is financial suicide

Because VIX futures are in contango approximately 80% of the time, VXX is perpetually rolling from cheaper front-month contracts into more expensive back-month contracts. Every single day, a tiny slice of value is destroyed. Over a month, this adds up to roughly 5%. Over a year, 60-70%. Over five years, 99%+.

If you had bought $10,000 of VXX (or its predecessor) in January 2019 and held through March 2020 — through the biggest VIX spike in a decade — your position would have peaked at roughly $25,000 during the March COVID crash but would have been worth approximately $1,200 before the spike even started. The contango erosion from Jan 2019 to Feb 2020 destroyed 88% of the position before the hedge was needed.

UVXY is worse: the 1.5x leverage compounds the daily decay. Negative compounding means UVXY has historically lost 75-90% of its value annually. It undergoes regular reverse splits (8:1 in 2024, for example) to keep the share price above $1.

The Graveyard of VIX ETPs

The history of VIX ETPs is littered with terminated products, blown-up funds, and regulatory investigations. Here are the casualties:

  • XIV (VelocityShares Daily Inverse VIX Short-Term ETN) — Lost 96% of its value on February 5, 2018 ("Volmageddon"). Credit Suisse terminated the product the next day. Approximately $1.9 billion in investor capital was destroyed in hours.
  • TVIX (VelocityShares Daily 2x VIX Short-Term ETN) — 2x leveraged long VIX. Underwent multiple reverse splits as it decayed to near zero. Delisted in 2020. From its 2012 reverse-split-adjusted peak, it declined over 99.99%.
  • SVXY (original) — Originally offered -1x inverse VIX exposure. After Volmageddon, ProShares cut leverage to -0.5x to reduce blowup risk. The original -1x version would have been terminated.
  • Dozens more — ZIV, VXZ (old versions), EXIV, VMIN, and many others have been terminated, restructured, or made nearly untradable due to decay.
SVXY and the Short Vol Trade
It works — until it spectacularly doesn’t

SVXY (-0.5x inverse VIX) benefits from contango: each daily roll adds value because you are effectively selling expensive far-month futures and buying cheaper near-month futures. In calm, trending markets, SVXY can return 20-40% annually with relatively smooth equity curves.

The problem is tail risk. On Volmageddon (Feb 5, 2018), the original -1x SVXY lost 93% of its value in a single session. The current -0.5x version would lose approximately 46.5% in an equivalent event. Short vol is a strategy of collecting pennies in front of a steamroller: the wins are small and frequent, the losses are rare and existential.

If you run a short vol strategy, position sizing and stop-loss discipline are not optional — they are survival requirements. We cover this in the Position Sizing section below.

VIX Options

The Most Misunderstood Derivative in the Market

VIX options are the instrument of choice for institutional hedgers and sophisticated vol traders. They are also the instrument most frequently mispriced by retail traders, because VIX options break almost every rule you learned about equity options.

The #1 Mistake Beginners Make

Retail traders look at VIX spot at 14, see a VIX 20 call priced at $2.50, and think: "VIX just needs to go up 6 points for this to be in the money. That's free money during the next selloff!" Wrong.

VIX options are priced off VIX futures, not VIX spot. If VIX spot is at 14 but the front-month VIX future is at 16.50, your 20-strike call only needs VIX futures to move 3.50 points — but it also needs VIX futures to move, not VIX spot. VIX spot can spike to 22 while the future you’re pricing against only moves to 19 (because futures dampen spot moves). Your "guaranteed winner" expires worthless.

Key Characteristics

How VIX options differ from every other option you have traded

  • European-style exercise — Can only be exercised at expiration, not before. No early assignment risk.
  • Cash-settled — No delivery of shares or futures. Settlement is the difference between the strike and the SOQ (Special Opening Quotation) on expiration morning.
  • AM settlement — The SOQ is calculated from SPX option opening prices on expiration Wednesday morning. This means your VIX option position settles based on a calculation, not a tradable price.
  • Priced off VIX futures — The underlying for VIX option pricing is the VIX future expiring closest to the option expiration, NOT VIX spot. This creates persistent discrepancies between what traders expect and what they get.
  • Inverse skew — VIX calls are more expensive (higher implied vol) than VIX puts. This is the opposite of equity options, where puts carry a fear premium. In VIX, the fear is of upside (a vol explosion), not downside.

VIX Calls as Portfolio Insurance
The classic institutional hedge — and how to do it cheaply

Buying OTM VIX calls (e.g., 25-strike when VIX is at 15) is a popular hedge for equity portfolios. When markets crash, VIX spikes, and your calls explode in value, offsetting equity losses. The cost of this insurance is the premium paid for the calls, which typically decays to zero if no spike occurs.

To reduce cost, institutions use call spreads (buy VIX 25 call, sell VIX 40 call) or ratio spreads (buy 1x VIX 25 call, sell 2x VIX 45 call). These structures cap the payout but reduce the net premium to near zero. The risk in a ratio spread is that VIX spikes beyond your short strike, creating a naked short call position at the worst possible time.

The VVIX connection: When VVIX (vol of VIX) is low, VIX options are cheap. This is the best time to buy protective VIX calls. When VVIX is high, VIX options are expensive, and you should wait or use spreads.

VIX Put Selling for Premium
Harvesting mean reversion — with controlled risk

Because VIX is strongly mean-reverting, selling VIX puts during a spike (e.g., selling VIX 25 puts when VIX is at 35) is a high-probability trade. VIX will almost certainly decline back below 25, and the put expires worthless. The European exercise feature means no early assignment risk.

The edge comes from mean reversion: VIX has never stayed above 30 for more than a few months in history. Selling puts at spikes is selling fear at its most expensive. But timing is critical — selling too early in a crisis (when VIX is at 25 and heading to 80) is catastrophic. Wait for the first sign of VIX deceleration (lower highs on intraday VIX, VIX/VIX3M ratio starting to decline, VVIX rolling over).

Entry & Exit Strategies

When to Pull the Trigger

Knowing VIX instruments is necessary but not sufficient. The difference between profitable vol traders and the rest is timing: when to enter, how to size, and when to exit — before the trade turns against you. Volatility trades are inherently short-duration. Holding too long is the most common way to lose money.

When to Go Long VIX

Buying volatility before the storm — the three setups that work

Signal 1: VIX < 13 with Steep Term Structure
Complacency + carry cost = setup for a spike

When VIX drops below 13 and the term structure is very steep (M2/M1 ratio > 1.15), the market is pricing extreme complacency. Historically, VIX below 12 has preceded some of the largest spikes (Feb 2018 Volmageddon started at VIX 9, COVID crash started at VIX 14). Low vol begets high vol — this is the volatility clustering property.

The trade: buy VIX calls (not futures — the contango cost of holding futures at these levels is 8-10% per month). Use 1-2 month expiration, 20-25 strike. Budget 0.5-1% of portfolio for this tail hedge. Accept that 7 out of 10 times, the premium decays to zero. The 3 out of 10 times it hits, it pays 5-10x.

Signal 2: VVIX Divergence
When the vol of vol warns before VIX does

VVIX (the volatility of VIX options) sometimes spikes while VIX itself remains low. This divergence signals that options market makers are hedging against a potential VIX explosion. The smart money is buying VIX calls aggressively while VIX spot remains suppressed — a divergence that often resolves with a sharp VIX spike 1-5 days later.

The setup: VVIX > 110 while VIX < 16. Buy VIX call spreads (e.g., 20/30 call spread) for reduced cost. Timeframe: 2-4 weeks to expiration.

Signal 3: Pre-Event Volatility
FOMC, CPI, earnings — known catalysts for vol expansion

Certain events reliably increase VIX: FOMC rate decisions, CPI prints, major earnings (AAPL, NVDA, etc.), and geopolitical events. The pattern is consistent: VIX rises 2-5 days before the event and declines sharply after (the "vol crush"). Buying VIX exposure 5-7 days before a major event and selling the day before captures the anticipatory vol premium.

This is not a directional bet on the event outcome. You are trading the uncertainty premium itself. Once the event occurs, uncertainty resolves, and VIX collapses regardless of the outcome. Sell before the event, not after.

When to Go Short VIX

Selling the spike — the three setups with the best risk/reward

Signal 1: VIX Spike > 35 (Post-Capitulation)
The mean reversion trade — selling fear at peak prices

VIX above 35 is historically unsustainable. In the past 30 years, VIX has spent less than 5% of trading days above 35. When it gets there, it typically reverts below 25 within 10-20 trading days. Shorting VIX (via VIX put selling, SVXY, or short VIX futures) after the initial spike has exhausted is a high-probability trade.

The key is waiting for exhaustion signals: VIX making lower intraday highs, SPX finding a floor, credit spreads starting to tighten. Never short VIX on the first day of a spike — VIX above 35 can go to 80 (March 2020).

Signal 2: VIX/VIX3M Ratio > 1.15 Normalizing
Term structure inversion is mean-reverting

When VIX/VIX3M (1-month vs. 3-month VIX) exceeds 1.15, the term structure is deeply inverted (backwardation). This state is unstable and almost always normalizes within 5-15 trading days. As the ratio declines toward 1.00, long VIX positions suffer and short VIX positions profit.

The entry trigger: VIX/VIX3M ratio drops below 1.10 after peaking above 1.15. This confirms the panic is subsiding. Target: ratio at 0.90-0.95 (normal contango). Stop: ratio makes a new high above 1.20 (panic worsening).

Signal 3: Post-Event Vol Crush
Selling VIX after the known catalyst removes all uncertainty

After FOMC, CPI, or major earnings, VIX typically drops 10-20% in a single session regardless of outcome. Selling VIX (or buying SVXY) immediately after the event resolution captures the vol crush. This is the mirror image of the pre-event long trade.

Best after events where VIX was elevated beforehand (VIX > 18 pre-event). If VIX is already low (< 14), the vol crush is minimal and not worth the risk.

The Cardinal Rule of VIX Trading

Volatility trades are not investments. They are tactical, short-duration positions. The maximum holding period for any VIX trade should be 1-20 days. Holding long VIX for months means you are paying contango rent on a house that may never appreciate. Holding short VIX indefinitely means you are collecting rent on a house built on a fault line. Set your exit before you enter. No exceptions.

Position Sizing for Volatility

The Difference Between a Drawdown and a Blowup

VIX moves are violent. A 20-50% move in a single day is common during spikes. UVXY can double in 48 hours — or lose 30% in a week during calm markets. The instruments are leveraged, path-dependent, and mean-reverting. Standard position sizing rules from equity trading are dangerously inadequate for volatility.

Every VIX blowup story — from Volmageddon retail casualties to the implosion of Malachite Capital’s vol fund — shares a common root cause: the position was too large for the magnitude of the adverse move. Being directionally right means nothing if a 3-day drawdown triggers a margin call before the trade works.

10 Position Sizing Rules for Volatility

  1. Never allocate more than 5% of portfolio to long VIX — These are lottery tickets, not core positions. Budget 1-2% for call hedges, up to 5% for a high-conviction spike trade.
  2. Never allocate more than 10% to short VIX — Short vol can produce 50-90% drawdowns in a single week. If your position is 10% of NAV, the worst-case portfolio impact is -5% to -9%. Survivable.
  3. Always define maximum loss before entry — Before placing the trade, write down: "The most I will lose on this trade is $X." If that number makes you uncomfortable, cut the size.
  4. Use options to define risk — Buying VIX calls defines max loss as the premium paid. Selling VIX put spreads defines max loss as the spread width minus premium received. These structures cap your exposure.
  5. Account for leverage — UVXY at 1.5x leverage means a $10,000 position behaves like $15,000 of VIX futures exposure. VIX futures at $1,000/point means one contract = $15,000-$25,000 of notional exposure. Size accordingly.
  6. Scale into positions — Do not put the full allocation on at once. Enter 1/3 at the first signal, add 1/3 on confirmation, and add the final 1/3 if the trade works in your favor. Average into winners, not losers.
  7. Take profits in thirds — Sell 1/3 at the first target (e.g., +30%), 1/3 at the second target (+60%), and let the final 1/3 ride with a trailing stop. This locks in gains while maintaining upside exposure.
  8. The Kelly Criterion for vol trades — Kelly % = (Win% × Avg Win / Avg Loss - Loss%) / (Avg Win / Avg Loss). For a typical long VIX hedge: 30% win rate, 5x avg win, 1x avg loss → Kelly = (0.30 × 5 - 0.70) / 5 = 16%. Use quarter-Kelly (4%) for safety.
  9. Correlation matters — If you are long VIX calls AND short SPY puts, your net vol exposure is larger than either position alone. Calculate aggregate delta and vega across all positions.
  10. Maintain a cash buffer — Keep at least 20% of your trading capital in cash when running vol positions. VIX spikes trigger margin increases across your entire portfolio. Cash prevents forced liquidations at the worst possible time.

Kelly Criterion: Worked Example

You want to trade long VIX calls as portfolio hedges. Your historical track record:

  • Win rate: 25% (1 out of 4 trades is profitable)
  • Average winner: +400% (VIX spike captured)
  • Average loser: -100% (premium expires worthless)

Kelly % = (0.25 × 4.0 - 0.75) / 4.0 = (1.0 - 0.75) / 4.0 = 6.25%

Full Kelly says allocate 6.25% per trade. But Kelly assumes perfect knowledge of true odds — in practice, use quarter-Kelly = 1.56%. On a $100,000 portfolio, that is $1,560 per VIX call hedge. Enough to buy meaningful protection, small enough that 3-4 consecutive losses ($4,680-$6,240) do not impair the portfolio.

The Asymmetry Problem
Why standard risk management fails for vol trades

In equity trading, a 5% stop loss on a $10,000 position means you lose $500. Simple. In VIX trading, the price can gap through your stop. VIX can open 40% higher than the previous close (it did exactly this on Feb 5, 2018). Your "5% stop" becomes a 40% loss. VIX futures can be limit-up, meaning you cannot exit until the limit is expanded.

This is why options are superior to futures for long VIX positions: your max loss is the premium, regardless of how far VIX gaps. And it is why short VIX positions must be sized assuming a 50-80% adverse move, not a 5-10% move.

The 7 Deadly Sins of VIX Trading

How Every Vol Trader Has Lost Money

Every experienced VIX trader has committed at least three of these sins. Most have committed all seven. The difference between those who survive and those who blow up is whether they learned from the first offense or repeated it until it became fatal. These are not theoretical risks — they are the actual causes of the vast majority of VIX trading losses.

1
Holding VXX/UVXY as a "Long-Term Hedge"

Why This Kills You

You buy VXX as "portfolio insurance" and hold it for three months. During that period, VIX stays between 13 and 18 — nothing happens. Your VXX position has lost 15-25% from contango decay. You think: "I’ll keep holding — the crash is coming." After six months, you are down 35-45%. After a year, 60-70%. The crash you were hedging against would need VIX to go above 40 just for you to break even.

The fix: Use VIX call options for hedges, not ETPs. A 2-month VIX call costs the premium and nothing more. If VIX does not spike, you lose the premium (1-2% of portfolio). If it does, the call pays 3-10x. The asymmetry is in your favor without the continuous bleed of roll decay.

2
Shorting VIX Into a Crash

Why This Kills You

VIX is at 25. You think: "This is elevated, it always comes down." You short VIX futures or buy SVXY. VIX goes to 35. You add to your short — "it HAS to mean-revert." VIX goes to 50. Now you are facing a margin call. You cover at 48, locking in catastrophic losses. Two weeks later, VIX is back at 22. You were right about the direction but wrong about the timing, and the drawdown destroyed you.

The fix: Never short VIX during an active crisis. Wait for exhaustion signals: 3+ consecutive days of lower VIX highs, SPX forming a base, VIX/VIX3M ratio declining. The money you miss by waiting is insurance against a drawdown that ends your trading career.

3
Using VIX Options Without Understanding Futures Pricing

Why This Kills You

You buy a VIX 20 call when VIX spot is at 13, thinking you need a 7-point move. But the relevant VIX future is at 17.50. Your call is only 2.50 points OTM from the future’s perspective. If VIX spikes and the future goes to 22, you expect your call to be worth at least $2.00. But settlement is based on the SOQ, which could differ from the closing VIX price by 1-3 points. Your $2.00 expected profit becomes $0.50 or even $0.00.

The fix: Always know which VIX future your option is priced off. Calculate your option’s moneyness relative to the future, not spot. Check the SOQ risk: if your option is near-the-money, the AM settlement lottery could go either way.

4
Oversizing Volatility Positions

Why This Kills You

You allocate 20% of your portfolio to a short VIX trade because the carry is "easy money" — SVXY has returned 30% annually for three years. Then Volmageddon happens. Your 20% allocation drops 93% in one session, a -18.6% portfolio hit. Combined with the equity losses from the concurrent SPX selloff, your portfolio is down 25%+ in a single day. Recovery takes years.

The fix: Follow the sizing rules in the Position Sizing section. Max 5% long VIX, max 10% short VIX. These numbers are not conservative — they are survival thresholds.

5
No Stop Loss on Short Volatility

Why This Kills You

"VIX always comes down" is the most dangerous phrase in vol trading. It is technically true — VIX has always reverted — but it can stay elevated long enough to generate margin calls, forced liquidations, and permanent capital loss. During COVID (Feb-Mar 2020), VIX stayed above 30 for 50 consecutive trading days. If your short VIX position did not survive those 50 days, the eventual reversion to 20 is irrelevant.

The fix: Set a hard stop. For short VIX futures: close if VIX moves 30% against you. For SVXY: close if position drops 25%. For sold VIX puts: close at 2x premium received. No exceptions, no "waiting for the revert."

6
Buying Deep OTM VIX Calls as Lottery Tickets

Why This Kills You

VIX is at 15. You buy VIX 50 calls for $0.10, dreaming of a 2020-style spike where they’d be worth $30. The problem: VIX futures (the underlying for pricing) rarely exceed 40 even during major crises. During COVID, VIX hit 82 intraday but the front-month VIX future peaked around 55. Your 50-strike call needs an event more extreme than a global pandemic to have meaningful value. These are not lottery tickets — they are donations to market makers.

The fix: Buy ATM or slightly OTM calls relative to the VIX future, not spot. If the future is at 17, a 22-strike call has realistic odds of paying off during a moderate spike. A 50-strike call does not.

7
Ignoring the Term Structure

Why This Kills You

You look at VIX spot and make trading decisions based solely on its level. VIX is at 18 — "slightly elevated, time to short." But you did not check the term structure. M2/M1 ratio is 0.95 (backwardation), meaning the market is pricing near-term risk higher than long-term risk. This is a warning sign, not a sell signal. VIX at 18 in backwardation is far more dangerous than VIX at 22 in steep contango.

The fix: Check the full term structure before every VIX trade. Use vixcentral.com or the CBOE website. The shape of the curve tells you more about future VIX behavior than the spot level alone. Contango = calm (short vol favored). Backwardation = stress (long vol favored or stay flat).

A VIX future is a bet on where VIX will be at expiration, not where it is right now. This is the single most important concept: VIX futures converge to VIX spot at expiration, but before expiration, they trade at a premium or discount to spot.

Front Month vs. Back Months
Understanding the term structure is the key to everything

At any given time, multiple VIX futures contracts are trading simultaneously, each expiring in a different month. The front month (nearest expiration) is the most liquid and tracks VIX spot most closely. The back months reflect the market's expectation of future volatility and are typically priced higher than the front month.

This price relationship across expirations is called the term structure. It has two possible shapes:

  • Contango (normal, ~80% of the time) — Back months priced HIGHER than front month. The market expects higher volatility in the future. Long VIX futures positions lose money from roll.
  • Backwardation (crisis, ~20% of the time) — Back months priced LOWER than front month. The market is in panic — current vol is extreme, and the market expects it to decrease. Long VIX futures positions gain from roll.

Roll Yield

The silent killer — or silent earner — of every VIX position

VIX futures expire. To maintain a continuous position, you must roll: sell the expiring contract and buy the next month. This rolling process has a cost or benefit called roll yield.

Roll Yield Mathematics

Suppose VIX spot is at 14. The front-month future (M1) trades at 15.50 and the second-month future (M2) at 17.00.

Contango roll cost = (M2 - M1) / M1 = (17.00 - 15.50) / 15.50 = 9.7% per month

If you are holding a long position and must roll from M1 to M2, you sell at 15.50 and buy at 17.00, paying a 1.50-point premium ($1,500 per contract). Over a year of continuous rolling, this compounding negative carry typically erodes 50-70% of the position’s value.

The opposite occurs in backwardation: rolling from a higher-priced M1 to a cheaper M2 generates positive carry. This is why short vol strategies are profitable in calm markets and long vol strategies are expensive to maintain.

Convergence is the mechanism: as expiration approaches, the futures price is pulled toward VIX spot. In contango, this means the futures price declines toward spot even if VIX spot doesn't move. For a long futures holder, this convergence is pure loss. For a short futures holder, it is pure profit — until the next VIX spike turns it into catastrophic loss.

Why VIX Futures Are Not a "Buy and Hold"
The math is brutal for directional long-vol positions

Consider a trader who was "right" about VIX direction: VIX spot moved from 14 to 16 (+14%) over two months. But their front-month futures position rolled twice, paying ~10% each time in contango costs. Net result: 14% gain - 20% roll cost = -6% loss. Being right on direction and wrong on timing is the defining tragedy of VIX futures trading.

The only scenario where long VIX futures work consistently is during a sudden, violent spike where the 30-50% single-day move overwhelms the roll cost. Think March 2020 (+400%) or August 2015 (+135%). These events are, by definition, rare and unpredictable.

VIX ETPs Explained

Exchange-Traded Products for Volatility

VIX ETPs are the gateway through which most retail traders access the volatility market. They package VIX futures exposure into a ticker symbol you can buy and sell like a stock. But this convenience comes with a devastating cost: structural decay that makes almost every long VIX ETP a guaranteed loser over time.

How VIX ETPs Work

Daily-rebalanced portfolios of VIX futures — and why that matters

A VIX ETP like VXX holds a rolling portfolio of front-month and second-month VIX futures. Each day, it sells a fraction of M1 futures and buys a corresponding fraction of M2 futures to maintain a constant weighted-average maturity of approximately 30 days. This daily rebalancing is where the decay originates.

The Contango Decay Problem
Why holding VXX for months is financial suicide

Because VIX futures are in contango approximately 80% of the time, VXX is perpetually rolling from cheaper front-month contracts into more expensive back-month contracts. Every single day, a tiny slice of value is destroyed. Over a month, this adds up to roughly 5%. Over a year, 60-70%. Over five years, 99%+.

If you had bought $10,000 of VXX (or its predecessor) in January 2019 and held through March 2020 — through the biggest VIX spike in a decade — your position would have peaked at roughly $25,000 during the March COVID crash but would have been worth approximately $1,200 before the spike even started. The contango erosion from Jan 2019 to Feb 2020 destroyed 88% of the position before the hedge was needed.

UVXY is worse: the 1.5x leverage compounds the daily decay. Negative compounding means UVXY has historically lost 75-90% of its value annually. It undergoes regular reverse splits (8:1 in 2024, for example) to keep the share price above $1.

The Graveyard of VIX ETPs

The history of VIX ETPs is littered with terminated products, blown-up funds, and regulatory investigations. Here are the casualties:

  • XIV (VelocityShares Daily Inverse VIX Short-Term ETN) — Lost 96% of its value on February 5, 2018 ("Volmageddon"). Credit Suisse terminated the product the next day. Approximately $1.9 billion in investor capital was destroyed in hours.
  • TVIX (VelocityShares Daily 2x VIX Short-Term ETN) — 2x leveraged long VIX. Underwent multiple reverse splits as it decayed to near zero. Delisted in 2020. From its 2012 reverse-split-adjusted peak, it declined over 99.99%.
  • SVXY (original) — Originally offered -1x inverse VIX exposure. After Volmageddon, ProShares cut leverage to -0.5x to reduce blowup risk. The original -1x version would have been terminated.
  • Dozens more — ZIV, VXZ (old versions), EXIV, VMIN, and many others have been terminated, restructured, or made nearly untradable due to decay.
SVXY and the Short Vol Trade
It works — until it spectacularly doesn’t

SVXY (-0.5x inverse VIX) benefits from contango: each daily roll adds value because you are effectively selling expensive far-month futures and buying cheaper near-month futures. In calm, trending markets, SVXY can return 20-40% annually with relatively smooth equity curves.

The problem is tail risk. On Volmageddon (Feb 5, 2018), the original -1x SVXY lost 93% of its value in a single session. The current -0.5x version would lose approximately 46.5% in an equivalent event. Short vol is a strategy of collecting pennies in front of a steamroller: the wins are small and frequent, the losses are rare and existential.

If you run a short vol strategy, position sizing and stop-loss discipline are not optional — they are survival requirements. We cover this in the Position Sizing section below.

VIX Options

The Most Misunderstood Derivative in the Market

VIX options are the instrument of choice for institutional hedgers and sophisticated vol traders. They are also the instrument most frequently mispriced by retail traders, because VIX options break almost every rule you learned about equity options.

The #1 Mistake Beginners Make

Retail traders look at VIX spot at 14, see a VIX 20 call priced at $2.50, and think: "VIX just needs to go up 6 points for this to be in the money. That's free money during the next selloff!" Wrong.

VIX options are priced off VIX futures, not VIX spot. If VIX spot is at 14 but the front-month VIX future is at 16.50, your 20-strike call only needs VIX futures to move 3.50 points — but it also needs VIX futures to move, not VIX spot. VIX spot can spike to 22 while the future you’re pricing against only moves to 19 (because futures dampen spot moves). Your "guaranteed winner" expires worthless.

Key Characteristics

How VIX options differ from every other option you have traded

  • European-style exercise — Can only be exercised at expiration, not before. No early assignment risk.
  • Cash-settled — No delivery of shares or futures. Settlement is the difference between the strike and the SOQ (Special Opening Quotation) on expiration morning.
  • AM settlement — The SOQ is calculated from SPX option opening prices on expiration Wednesday morning. This means your VIX option position settles based on a calculation, not a tradable price.
  • Priced off VIX futures — The underlying for VIX option pricing is the VIX future expiring closest to the option expiration, NOT VIX spot. This creates persistent discrepancies between what traders expect and what they get.
  • Inverse skew — VIX calls are more expensive (higher implied vol) than VIX puts. This is the opposite of equity options, where puts carry a fear premium. In VIX, the fear is of upside (a vol explosion), not downside.

VIX Calls as Portfolio Insurance
The classic institutional hedge — and how to do it cheaply

Buying OTM VIX calls (e.g., 25-strike when VIX is at 15) is a popular hedge for equity portfolios. When markets crash, VIX spikes, and your calls explode in value, offsetting equity losses. The cost of this insurance is the premium paid for the calls, which typically decays to zero if no spike occurs.

To reduce cost, institutions use call spreads (buy VIX 25 call, sell VIX 40 call) or ratio spreads (buy 1x VIX 25 call, sell 2x VIX 45 call). These structures cap the payout but reduce the net premium to near zero. The risk in a ratio spread is that VIX spikes beyond your short strike, creating a naked short call position at the worst possible time.

The VVIX connection: When VVIX (vol of VIX) is low, VIX options are cheap. This is the best time to buy protective VIX calls. When VVIX is high, VIX options are expensive, and you should wait or use spreads.

VIX Put Selling for Premium
Harvesting mean reversion — with controlled risk

Because VIX is strongly mean-reverting, selling VIX puts during a spike (e.g., selling VIX 25 puts when VIX is at 35) is a high-probability trade. VIX will almost certainly decline back below 25, and the put expires worthless. The European exercise feature means no early assignment risk.

The edge comes from mean reversion: VIX has never stayed above 30 for more than a few months in history. Selling puts at spikes is selling fear at its most expensive. But timing is critical — selling too early in a crisis (when VIX is at 25 and heading to 80) is catastrophic. Wait for the first sign of VIX deceleration (lower highs on intraday VIX, VIX/VIX3M ratio starting to decline, VVIX rolling over).

Entry & Exit Strategies

When to Pull the Trigger

Knowing VIX instruments is necessary but not sufficient. The difference between profitable vol traders and the rest is timing: when to enter, how to size, and when to exit — before the trade turns against you. Volatility trades are inherently short-duration. Holding too long is the most common way to lose money.

When to Go Long VIX

Buying volatility before the storm — the three setups that work

Signal 1: VIX < 13 with Steep Term Structure
Complacency + carry cost = setup for a spike

When VIX drops below 13 and the term structure is very steep (M2/M1 ratio > 1.15), the market is pricing extreme complacency. Historically, VIX below 12 has preceded some of the largest spikes (Feb 2018 Volmageddon started at VIX 9, COVID crash started at VIX 14). Low vol begets high vol — this is the volatility clustering property.

The trade: buy VIX calls (not futures — the contango cost of holding futures at these levels is 8-10% per month). Use 1-2 month expiration, 20-25 strike. Budget 0.5-1% of portfolio for this tail hedge. Accept that 7 out of 10 times, the premium decays to zero. The 3 out of 10 times it hits, it pays 5-10x.

Signal 2: VVIX Divergence
When the vol of vol warns before VIX does

VVIX (the volatility of VIX options) sometimes spikes while VIX itself remains low. This divergence signals that options market makers are hedging against a potential VIX explosion. The smart money is buying VIX calls aggressively while VIX spot remains suppressed — a divergence that often resolves with a sharp VIX spike 1-5 days later.

The setup: VVIX > 110 while VIX < 16. Buy VIX call spreads (e.g., 20/30 call spread) for reduced cost. Timeframe: 2-4 weeks to expiration.

Signal 3: Pre-Event Volatility
FOMC, CPI, earnings — known catalysts for vol expansion

Certain events reliably increase VIX: FOMC rate decisions, CPI prints, major earnings (AAPL, NVDA, etc.), and geopolitical events. The pattern is consistent: VIX rises 2-5 days before the event and declines sharply after (the "vol crush"). Buying VIX exposure 5-7 days before a major event and selling the day before captures the anticipatory vol premium.

This is not a directional bet on the event outcome. You are trading the uncertainty premium itself. Once the event occurs, uncertainty resolves, and VIX collapses regardless of the outcome. Sell before the event, not after.

When to Go Short VIX

Selling the spike — the three setups with the best risk/reward

Signal 1: VIX Spike > 35 (Post-Capitulation)
The mean reversion trade — selling fear at peak prices

VIX above 35 is historically unsustainable. In the past 30 years, VIX has spent less than 5% of trading days above 35. When it gets there, it typically reverts below 25 within 10-20 trading days. Shorting VIX (via VIX put selling, SVXY, or short VIX futures) after the initial spike has exhausted is a high-probability trade.

The key is waiting for exhaustion signals: VIX making lower intraday highs, SPX finding a floor, credit spreads starting to tighten. Never short VIX on the first day of a spike — VIX above 35 can go to 80 (March 2020).

Signal 2: VIX/VIX3M Ratio > 1.15 Normalizing
Term structure inversion is mean-reverting

When VIX/VIX3M (1-month vs. 3-month VIX) exceeds 1.15, the term structure is deeply inverted (backwardation). This state is unstable and almost always normalizes within 5-15 trading days. As the ratio declines toward 1.00, long VIX positions suffer and short VIX positions profit.

The entry trigger: VIX/VIX3M ratio drops below 1.10 after peaking above 1.15. This confirms the panic is subsiding. Target: ratio at 0.90-0.95 (normal contango). Stop: ratio makes a new high above 1.20 (panic worsening).

Signal 3: Post-Event Vol Crush
Selling VIX after the known catalyst removes all uncertainty

After FOMC, CPI, or major earnings, VIX typically drops 10-20% in a single session regardless of outcome. Selling VIX (or buying SVXY) immediately after the event resolution captures the vol crush. This is the mirror image of the pre-event long trade.

Best after events where VIX was elevated beforehand (VIX > 18 pre-event). If VIX is already low (< 14), the vol crush is minimal and not worth the risk.

The Cardinal Rule of VIX Trading

Volatility trades are not investments. They are tactical, short-duration positions. The maximum holding period for any VIX trade should be 1-20 days. Holding long VIX for months means you are paying contango rent on a house that may never appreciate. Holding short VIX indefinitely means you are collecting rent on a house built on a fault line. Set your exit before you enter. No exceptions.

Position Sizing for Volatility

The Difference Between a Drawdown and a Blowup

VIX moves are violent. A 20-50% move in a single day is common during spikes. UVXY can double in 48 hours — or lose 30% in a week during calm markets. The instruments are leveraged, path-dependent, and mean-reverting. Standard position sizing rules from equity trading are dangerously inadequate for volatility.

Every VIX blowup story — from Volmageddon retail casualties to the implosion of Malachite Capital’s vol fund — shares a common root cause: the position was too large for the magnitude of the adverse move. Being directionally right means nothing if a 3-day drawdown triggers a margin call before the trade works.

10 Position Sizing Rules for Volatility

  1. Never allocate more than 5% of portfolio to long VIX — These are lottery tickets, not core positions. Budget 1-2% for call hedges, up to 5% for a high-conviction spike trade.
  2. Never allocate more than 10% to short VIX — Short vol can produce 50-90% drawdowns in a single week. If your position is 10% of NAV, the worst-case portfolio impact is -5% to -9%. Survivable.
  3. Always define maximum loss before entry — Before placing the trade, write down: "The most I will lose on this trade is $X." If that number makes you uncomfortable, cut the size.
  4. Use options to define risk — Buying VIX calls defines max loss as the premium paid. Selling VIX put spreads defines max loss as the spread width minus premium received. These structures cap your exposure.
  5. Account for leverage — UVXY at 1.5x leverage means a $10,000 position behaves like $15,000 of VIX futures exposure. VIX futures at $1,000/point means one contract = $15,000-$25,000 of notional exposure. Size accordingly.
  6. Scale into positions — Do not put the full allocation on at once. Enter 1/3 at the first signal, add 1/3 on confirmation, and add the final 1/3 if the trade works in your favor. Average into winners, not losers.
  7. Take profits in thirds — Sell 1/3 at the first target (e.g., +30%), 1/3 at the second target (+60%), and let the final 1/3 ride with a trailing stop. This locks in gains while maintaining upside exposure.
  8. The Kelly Criterion for vol trades — Kelly % = (Win% × Avg Win / Avg Loss - Loss%) / (Avg Win / Avg Loss). For a typical long VIX hedge: 30% win rate, 5x avg win, 1x avg loss → Kelly = (0.30 × 5 - 0.70) / 5 = 16%. Use quarter-Kelly (4%) for safety.
  9. Correlation matters — If you are long VIX calls AND short SPY puts, your net vol exposure is larger than either position alone. Calculate aggregate delta and vega across all positions.
  10. Maintain a cash buffer — Keep at least 20% of your trading capital in cash when running vol positions. VIX spikes trigger margin increases across your entire portfolio. Cash prevents forced liquidations at the worst possible time.

Kelly Criterion: Worked Example

You want to trade long VIX calls as portfolio hedges. Your historical track record:

  • Win rate: 25% (1 out of 4 trades is profitable)
  • Average winner: +400% (VIX spike captured)
  • Average loser: -100% (premium expires worthless)

Kelly % = (0.25 × 4.0 - 0.75) / 4.0 = (1.0 - 0.75) / 4.0 = 6.25%

Full Kelly says allocate 6.25% per trade. But Kelly assumes perfect knowledge of true odds — in practice, use quarter-Kelly = 1.56%. On a $100,000 portfolio, that is $1,560 per VIX call hedge. Enough to buy meaningful protection, small enough that 3-4 consecutive losses ($4,680-$6,240) do not impair the portfolio.

The Asymmetry Problem
Why standard risk management fails for vol trades

In equity trading, a 5% stop loss on a $10,000 position means you lose $500. Simple. In VIX trading, the price can gap through your stop. VIX can open 40% higher than the previous close (it did exactly this on Feb 5, 2018). Your "5% stop" becomes a 40% loss. VIX futures can be limit-up, meaning you cannot exit until the limit is expanded.

This is why options are superior to futures for long VIX positions: your max loss is the premium, regardless of how far VIX gaps. And it is why short VIX positions must be sized assuming a 50-80% adverse move, not a 5-10% move.

The 7 Deadly Sins of VIX Trading

How Every Vol Trader Has Lost Money

Every experienced VIX trader has committed at least three of these sins. Most have committed all seven. The difference between those who survive and those who blow up is whether they learned from the first offense or repeated it until it became fatal. These are not theoretical risks — they are the actual causes of the vast majority of VIX trading losses.

1
Holding VXX/UVXY as a "Long-Term Hedge"

Why This Kills You

You buy VXX as "portfolio insurance" and hold it for three months. During that period, VIX stays between 13 and 18 — nothing happens. Your VXX position has lost 15-25% from contango decay. You think: "I’ll keep holding — the crash is coming." After six months, you are down 35-45%. After a year, 60-70%. The crash you were hedging against would need VIX to go above 40 just for you to break even.

The fix: Use VIX call options for hedges, not ETPs. A 2-month VIX call costs the premium and nothing more. If VIX does not spike, you lose the premium (1-2% of portfolio). If it does, the call pays 3-10x. The asymmetry is in your favor without the continuous bleed of roll decay.

2
Shorting VIX Into a Crash

Why This Kills You

VIX is at 25. You think: "This is elevated, it always comes down." You short VIX futures or buy SVXY. VIX goes to 35. You add to your short — "it HAS to mean-revert." VIX goes to 50. Now you are facing a margin call. You cover at 48, locking in catastrophic losses. Two weeks later, VIX is back at 22. You were right about the direction but wrong about the timing, and the drawdown destroyed you.

The fix: Never short VIX during an active crisis. Wait for exhaustion signals: 3+ consecutive days of lower VIX highs, SPX forming a base, VIX/VIX3M ratio declining. The money you miss by waiting is insurance against a drawdown that ends your trading career.

3
Using VIX Options Without Understanding Futures Pricing

Why This Kills You

You buy a VIX 20 call when VIX spot is at 13, thinking you need a 7-point move. But the relevant VIX future is at 17.50. Your call is only 2.50 points OTM from the future’s perspective. If VIX spikes and the future goes to 22, you expect your call to be worth at least $2.00. But settlement is based on the SOQ, which could differ from the closing VIX price by 1-3 points. Your $2.00 expected profit becomes $0.50 or even $0.00.

The fix: Always know which VIX future your option is priced off. Calculate your option’s moneyness relative to the future, not spot. Check the SOQ risk: if your option is near-the-money, the AM settlement lottery could go either way.

4
Oversizing Volatility Positions

Why This Kills You

You allocate 20% of your portfolio to a short VIX trade because the carry is "easy money" — SVXY has returned 30% annually for three years. Then Volmageddon happens. Your 20% allocation drops 93% in one session, a -18.6% portfolio hit. Combined with the equity losses from the concurrent SPX selloff, your portfolio is down 25%+ in a single day. Recovery takes years.

The fix: Follow the sizing rules in the Position Sizing section. Max 5% long VIX, max 10% short VIX. These numbers are not conservative — they are survival thresholds.

5
No Stop Loss on Short Volatility

Why This Kills You

"VIX always comes down" is the most dangerous phrase in vol trading. It is technically true — VIX has always reverted — but it can stay elevated long enough to generate margin calls, forced liquidations, and permanent capital loss. During COVID (Feb-Mar 2020), VIX stayed above 30 for 50 consecutive trading days. If your short VIX position did not survive those 50 days, the eventual reversion to 20 is irrelevant.

The fix: Set a hard stop. For short VIX futures: close if VIX moves 30% against you. For SVXY: close if position drops 25%. For sold VIX puts: close at 2x premium received. No exceptions, no "waiting for the revert."

6
Buying Deep OTM VIX Calls as Lottery Tickets

Why This Kills You

VIX is at 15. You buy VIX 50 calls for $0.10, dreaming of a 2020-style spike where they’d be worth $30. The problem: VIX futures (the underlying for pricing) rarely exceed 40 even during major crises. During COVID, VIX hit 82 intraday but the front-month VIX future peaked around 55. Your 50-strike call needs an event more extreme than a global pandemic to have meaningful value. These are not lottery tickets — they are donations to market makers.

The fix: Buy ATM or slightly OTM calls relative to the VIX future, not spot. If the future is at 17, a 22-strike call has realistic odds of paying off during a moderate spike. A 50-strike call does not.

7
Ignoring the Term Structure

Why This Kills You

You look at VIX spot and make trading decisions based solely on its level. VIX is at 18 — "slightly elevated, time to short." But you did not check the term structure. M2/M1 ratio is 0.95 (backwardation), meaning the market is pricing near-term risk higher than long-term risk. This is a warning sign, not a sell signal. VIX at 18 in backwardation is far more dangerous than VIX at 22 in steep contango.

The fix: Check the full term structure before every VIX trade. Use vixcentral.com or the CBOE website. The shape of the curve tells you more about future VIX behavior than the spot level alone. Contango = calm (short vol favored). Backwardation = stress (long vol favored or stay flat).
