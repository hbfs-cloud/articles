What Are Commodities

The Raw Materials of Civilization

A commodity is any raw material or primary agricultural product that can be bought and sold. Unlike stocks or bonds, commodities are tangible things you can touch, weigh, or burn. Every barrel of West Texas Intermediate crude oil is essentially interchangeable with another barrel of the same grade. This property — called fungibility — is what makes commodities tradeable on exchanges.

Commodities are the oldest traded assets in human history. Sumerian clay tablets from 4,500 years ago record futures-like agreements on barley. The rice futures market in Osaka, Japan, dates back to 1697. The Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) was established in 1848 and standardized grain contracts that are still traded today. When you trade wheat futures in 2026, you are participating in a tradition that predates the Roman Empire.

Why Commodities Matter

Every product you use starts as a commodity. Your morning coffee is a softs commodity traded on ICE. Your car runs on crude oil refined into gasoline. The copper in your phone's wiring is an industrial metal traded on COMEX. The wheat in your bread is a grain traded on CBOT. Understanding commodities means understanding the physical foundation of the global economy.

For traders, commodities offer something stocks do not: fundamental supply and demand that is directly observable. You can measure crude oil inventories, count grain harvests, and track mine output. This makes commodity analysis uniquely anchored to physical reality rather than earnings estimates and sentiment.

Hard Commodities vs. Soft Commodities

The commodity world splits into two broad categories: hard commodities (mined or extracted from the earth) and soft commodities (grown or raised). This distinction matters because the supply dynamics are fundamentally different.

Understanding Fungibility

Fungibility means that one unit of a commodity is interchangeable with another unit of the same grade. A bushel of #2 Yellow Corn from Iowa is identical to a bushel of #2 Yellow Corn from Nebraska. This is fundamentally different from stocks — one share of Apple is not interchangeable with one share of Microsoft.

Fungibility is what allows commodity exchanges to exist. Buyers and sellers agree on standardized contract specifications (grade, quantity, delivery location), and the exchange guarantees that what you buy matches that specification. Without fungibility, you would need to inspect every barrel of oil personally before buying it.

The Commodity Supercycle

Supercycles — The Macro Pulse of Commodities

Commodity prices do not move randomly. They follow long-term patterns called supercycles — multi-decade periods of rising or falling prices driven by structural shifts in supply and demand. Understanding where we are in the current supercycle is the single most important macro context for commodity traders.

A commodity supercycle typically lasts 15-25 years from trough to peak, followed by another 15-25 years of decline. Since 1800, we have observed four completed supercycles and may be in the early stages of a fifth.

The Four Historical Supercycles

Cycle 1 (1890s-1930s): Driven by rapid industrialization in the US and Europe. Steel, copper, and coal demand surged. Ended with the Great Depression destroying demand.

Cycle 2 (1930s-1950s): World War II and post-war reconstruction created unprecedented demand for metals, oil, and food. The Marshall Plan rebuilt Europe with American raw materials.

Cycle 3 (1950s-1980s): Japan and Germany's economic miracles, the 1973 oil embargo, and global population growth. Oil went from $3 to $40/barrel. Gold went from $35 to $850.

Cycle 4 (1990s-2010s): China's entry into the global economy. A billion people industrializing simultaneously. Iron ore up 10x, copper up 5x, oil from $10 to $147. The "China Bid" was the defining force.

Cycle 5 (2020s-?): Energy transition (copper, lithium, rare earths), deglobalization, supply chain reshoring, climate disruption to agriculture, and central bank gold buying. We may be in the early bull phase.

What Drives Supercycles?

Every supercycle is driven by a mismatch between supply and demand that takes years — sometimes decades — to resolve. This happens because commodity supply responds slowly to price signals:

  • Mining lead times: A new copper mine takes 7-15 years from discovery to first production. Even at record prices, supply cannot ramp quickly.
  • Oil field depletion: Existing oil fields deplete at 4-8% per year. Just to maintain current production requires massive ongoing investment. Cut capex, and production falls within 18 months.
  • Agricultural cycles: Farmers can only plant once per year (in most climates). Even if wheat prices double, you cannot grow more wheat until next planting season.
  • Demand inertia: When a country industrializes, it needs steel, copper, and energy for decades. China's demand did not stop when commodity prices rose — it was structural, not speculative.
The Supercycle Equation
Rising Demand + Slow Supply Response = Sustained Price Increase

The Contrarian View

Not everyone agrees we are in a supercycle. Bears argue that: (1) AI and automation will reduce demand for physical goods, (2) recycling and substitution will ease supply constraints, (3) high prices are self-correcting as they incentivize new supply. The truth is probably somewhere in between — some commodities (copper, uranium) have clearer structural deficits than others (oil, aluminum).

Quiz: Supercycle Drivers

Question: Why does the energy transition increase demand for copper specifically?

Answer: An electric vehicle requires approximately 83 kg of copper compared to 23 kg for a conventional car — nearly 4x more. EVs need copper for the motor, battery wiring, charging infrastructure, and power electronics. Additionally, renewable energy generation (solar and wind) uses 4-5x more copper per megawatt than fossil fuel plants. If the world transitions to EVs and renewables, copper demand could increase by 50% by 2035 — and there are not enough mines being built to supply it.

Futures & Spot Markets

How Commodity Markets Work

There are two ways to price a commodity: the spot price (what you pay for immediate delivery) and the futures price (what you agree to pay for delivery at a specific future date). Understanding the relationship between these two prices is fundamental to commodity trading.

The Spot Market

The spot price is the current cash price for a commodity for immediate delivery. When you see "oil is $80/barrel" on the news, that is typically the front-month futures price (the closest to spot), not the true physical spot price. Physical spot markets are often over-the-counter (OTC) and less transparent than futures markets.

Spot vs. Futures — The Coffee Example

Imagine you own a coffee shop. Today, coffee beans cost $2.50/lb on the spot market. You can buy them right now at that price. But you are worried about a drought in Brazil that might push prices to $4.00/lb next quarter.

You could buy coffee futures — a contract that locks in a purchase price of $2.65/lb for delivery in three months. You pay a small premium over today's spot price, but you are hedged against the risk of a price spike. If the drought hits and spot goes to $4.00, you still buy at $2.65. If prices drop to $2.00, you overpaid — but you bought certainty.

This is exactly why futures markets exist: to allow producers and consumers to manage price risk.

Futures Contracts — The Basics

A futures contract is a standardized agreement to buy or sell a specific quantity of a commodity at a predetermined price on a specific future date. Key specifications:

Leverage Warning

Futures are heavily leveraged. A WTI crude oil contract controls $80,000 worth of oil but only requires ~$6,500 in margin. That is 12:1 leverage. A 5% move in oil = $4,000 gain or loss on a $6,500 deposit. This is why futures are both powerful and dangerous. Most retail traders should start with commodity ETFs before touching futures.

Rolling Contracts

Futures contracts have expiration dates. If you hold a June contract past expiration and have not offset your position, you may be obligated to take physical delivery of 1,000 barrels of crude oil. Obviously, that is not what most traders want.

To maintain a continuous position, traders roll their contracts: they sell the expiring month and buy the next month. This rolling process has costs that are directly related to the relationship between spot and futures prices — which brings us to contango and backwardation.

Futures Pricing Formula
Futures Price = Spot Price + Storage Costs + Financing Costs - Convenience Yield
Quiz: Futures Mechanics

Question: You buy 1 WTI crude oil futures contract at $80/barrel. The price rises to $83/barrel. What is your profit?

Answer: Each contract is 1,000 barrels. The price moved $3/barrel in your favor. Profit = 1,000 x $3 = $3,000. On initial margin of ~$6,500, that represents a return of approximately 46%. This illustrates the power (and danger) of futures leverage — a 3.75% move in the underlying commodity produced a 46% return on your margin deposit.

Contango & Backwardation

The Shape of the Futures Curve

The futures curve is the set of all futures prices for a commodity across different delivery months. Its shape tells you something critical about the market's expectations and the supply/demand balance. There are two shapes: contango and backwardation.

Contango — Normal Market

Contango means futures prices are higher than the spot price. The further out you go in time, the higher the price. This is the "normal" state for most commodities because futures prices incorporate storage costs, insurance, and financing. If it costs $2/barrel/month to store oil, the 6-month future should be approximately $12 above spot.

Contango in Practice

When oil is in contango, the cash-and-carry trade works: buy physical oil at spot, store it, and sell a futures contract at the higher futures price. If storage costs are less than the contango spread, you lock in a risk-free profit. This is what tank farms and oil supertankers do. In April 2020, contango was so extreme that traders filled every storage tank and even rented supertankers to park oil at sea — crude oil went negative (-$37/barrel) because there was literally nowhere to put it.

Backwardation — Tight Market

Backwardation means the spot price is higher than futures prices. The further out you go, the lower the price. This is a sign of a tight market — demand for the physical commodity right now exceeds supply, and buyers are willing to pay a premium for immediate delivery.

Backwardation in Practice

When oil is in backwardation, it means: "I need oil NOW and I will pay more for it than for a future promise." This typically happens during supply disruptions (wars, hurricanes, OPEC cuts) or demand surges. Backwardation is bullish for the commodity — it means the physical market is tighter than the financial market expects.

For commodity ETF holders, backwardation is actually positive because when the ETF rolls its expiring contracts, it sells high (expiring near-month) and buys low (next month) — earning a roll yield. In contango, the opposite happens: the ETF sells low and buys high, creating a drag called negative roll yield.

The USO Trap

The United States Oil Fund (USO) lost over 90% of its value between 2007 and 2020, even though oil prices were roughly the same. The culprit: chronic contango destroying value through negative roll yield. Every month, USO sold expiring contracts cheap and bought next-month contracts at a premium. Over 13 years, this "roll cost" ate nearly all the returns. This is the single most important lesson for commodity ETF investors: check the curve shape before going long.

Quiz: Contango vs. Backwardation

Question: Crude oil spot price is $82. The 3-month future is $79. The 6-month future is $77. Is this contango or backwardation? Is this bullish or bearish? What does it mean for USO?

Answer: This is backwardation — spot is higher than futures, and the curve slopes downward. This is bullish — the physical market is tight, buyers need oil now. For USO, this is positive — when USO rolls its contracts, it sells the expiring contract at a higher price and buys the next month at a lower price, earning a positive roll yield. This is the ideal environment for long commodity ETF positions.

Key Market Players

Who Trades Commodities and Why

Commodity markets have three distinct types of participants, and understanding their motivations is critical to interpreting price action. The CFTC (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) publishes the Commitments of Traders (COT) report every Friday, which breaks down positioning by category.

Commercials (Hedgers)

Producers and consumers of the physical commodity. They use futures to lock in prices and reduce business risk. Airlines hedge jet fuel. Farmers hedge grain. Oil companies hedge production. They are the informed participants.

Large Speculators

Hedge funds, CTAs (commodity trading advisors), and managed futures funds. They seek profit from price movements. They follow trends, trade spreads, and use leverage. They are typically trend followers and amplify price moves.

Small Speculators

Retail traders — you and me. The smallest category by volume. Typically the least informed and the most reactive. Historically, the COT data shows retail is most bullish at tops and most bearish at bottoms.

Reading the COT Report

The COT report is a powerful tool for commodity traders. Here is what to watch:

  • Commercial net position: When commercials are unusually net long (buying), it often signals that the commodity is undervalued. They know their product best.
  • Large speculator net position: When speculators are at extreme long positions, the trade is crowded. A reversal may be near.
  • Net change week-over-week: Are commercials adding or reducing hedges? Are speculators building or unwinding positions?

The classic contrarian signal: commercials are net long while speculators are net short. This means the "smart money" (producers who know their market intimately) is buying while the "trend followers" are selling. Historically, commercials win this battle.

Physical vs. Financial Market

An important distinction: the physical commodity market and the financial commodity market operate in parallel but can diverge. The physical market is where actual barrels of oil change hands. The financial market is where paper contracts (futures, options, ETFs) trade. On any given day, the volume of paper oil traded can be 50-100x the actual physical oil production.

Quiz: Market Players

Question: An airline buys jet fuel futures for delivery in 6 months. Are they a hedger or speculator? Why?

Answer: The airline is a hedger (commercial). They are buying jet fuel that they will actually consume in their business. They are not trying to profit from price movements — they are trying to lock in a known cost so they can plan their operations and ticket prices with certainty. If fuel prices spike, their futures gain offsets the higher physical cost. If fuel prices drop, they overpaid on futures but got certainty. This is the original purpose of futures markets: risk transfer from those who cannot afford price volatility to those willing to bear it.

Ways to Trade Commodities

Five Ways to Get Commodity Exposure

Not all commodity exposure is equal. Each method has different risk profiles, costs, and levels of complexity. Here is the complete menu, from simplest to most sophisticated:

1

Commodity ETFs

Buy and sell like stocks. No margin accounts needed. Examples: GLD (gold), USO (oil), DBA (agriculture). Watch for roll yield drag in contango markets.

2

Commodity Stocks

Buy shares of companies that produce commodities. Exxon (oil), Newmont (gold), Mosaic (fertilizer). Adds company-specific risk but also dividends.

3

Futures Contracts

Direct exposure with leverage. Requires margin account and understanding of contract specs. Most capital-efficient but highest risk.

4

Options on Futures

Buy calls or puts on futures contracts. Defined risk (premium paid). Complex but allows asymmetric bets with limited downside.

5

CFDs

Contracts for Difference. Popular outside the US. Synthetic exposure with leverage. No expiration dates but overnight financing costs.

The Beginner's Path

If you are new to commodities, start here:

  1. Month 1-3: Paper trade commodity ETFs (GLD, USO, DBA). Learn how contango affects returns. Track the futures curve weekly.
  2. Month 4-6: Buy commodity stocks (XLE for energy, GDX for gold miners). Understand leverage to commodity prices.
  3. Month 7-12: Open a futures account with a micro contract. Trade Micro WTI (/MCL = 100 barrels, 10x smaller than standard). Risk $500 max.
  4. Year 2+: Standard futures, spreads, options strategies.

The key is to master one commodity first before trying to trade them all. Most successful commodity traders specialize in 1-3 markets.

Quiz: Choosing Your Vehicle

Question: You want to bet that gold prices will rise over the next 6 months. You have $5,000 and are a beginner. Which vehicle should you use and why?

Answer: GLD (SPDR Gold Trust) is the best choice. With $5,000 you can buy approximately 20 shares. GLD holds physical gold in vaults, so there is no roll yield drag (unlike futures-based ETFs). The expense ratio is 0.40% annually — minimal. You can set a stop-loss like any stock. There is no margin call risk, no contract expiration to worry about, and no K-1 tax form. A gold futures contract (100 oz) would control ~$500,000 of gold — far too much leverage for a $5,000 account. Start simple, scale up as you learn.

Indices & Supply/Demand Fundamentals

Commodity Indices

Commodity indices track baskets of commodities and serve as benchmarks for the asset class. They differ significantly in composition, which means they can diverge widely. Knowing which index to follow depends on your view of the commodity mix.

Why Weights Matter

If you track the S&P GSCI, you are essentially tracking oil with some metals and agriculture sprinkled in. A 10% move in oil will move GSCI by ~5.5%, but a 10% move in corn barely registers. If you want balanced commodity exposure, BCOM is better. If you want to minimize roll costs, DBIQ Optimum Yield is superior because it selects the futures contract on each curve that minimizes contango.

Supply and Demand Fundamentals

Unlike stocks, commodity prices are ultimately governed by physical supply and physical demand. No amount of speculation can permanently decouple a commodity from its fundamental balance sheet. Here is the framework for analyzing any commodity:

The Supply Side

  • Production: How many barrels/tonnes/bushels are being produced right now? Is production growing or shrinking?
  • Inventories: How much is in storage? Are inventories above or below the 5-year average? Rising inventories = bearish. Falling inventories = bullish.
  • Spare Capacity: Can producers ramp up quickly? OPEC has ~5M bbl/day spare capacity. Most mining operations have zero.
  • Disruption Risk: Geopolitical events (wars, sanctions), natural disasters (hurricanes, earthquakes), labor strikes.
  • Investment Cycle: Current capex on new projects. Low capex today = supply deficit in 3-7 years.

The Demand Side

  • GDP Growth: Commodity demand is highly correlated with global economic growth. A global recession hits all commodities.
  • China: The marginal buyer for most commodities. China consumes 50% of global copper, 50% of steel, 15% of oil. China's PMI is a leading indicator.
  • Seasonality: Natural gas demand peaks in winter (heating) and summer (cooling). Gasoline demand peaks in summer driving season. Coffee peaks before winter in the Northern Hemisphere.
  • Substitution: High prices drive substitution — natural gas for coal, palladium for platinum, aluminum for copper. This creates natural price ceilings.
  • Structural Shifts: EV adoption reducing oil demand. Energy transition increasing copper/lithium demand. Vertical farming reducing water use.
Fundamental Price Signal
If Demand Growth > Supply Growth and Inventories Below 5Y Average = Bullish

Quiz: Supply and Demand

Question: Crude oil inventories have been falling for 8 consecutive weeks and are now 10% below the 5-year average. OPEC has cut production by 2M bbl/day. China's PMI just came in at 52 (expansion). What is the fundamental outlook?

Answer: This is a strongly bullish setup. Falling inventories indicate demand exceeds supply. Below-average inventories mean the buffer is thin. OPEC production cuts restrict supply. China's expanding PMI signals growing demand. All four factors align: declining supply, growing demand, low inventories, and restricted spare capacity. The futures curve should be in backwardation, and prices should be trending higher. This is the kind of fundamental backdrop that supports sustained commodity rallies.

  • Commodities are tangible, fungible raw materials traded on exchanges. They split into hard (mined/extracted) and soft (grown/raised).
