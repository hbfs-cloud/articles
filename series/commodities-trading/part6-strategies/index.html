Seasonal Trading Patterns

The Most Reliable Seasonal Trades

Commodity seasonality is more reliable than equity seasonality because it is grounded in physical reality: crops grow and are harvested at specific times, heating demand peaks in winter, driving demand peaks in summer. These patterns repeat year after year with measurable consistency.

Trade Entry Exit Win Rate (20Y) Avg Return Logic

Long Gold Jul-Aug Feb-Mar 70% +8% Indian wedding season + Chinese New Year buying

How to Use Seasonal Data

Seasonal patterns are not standalone trading signals — they are context that increases your edge when combined with other analysis. The workflow:

  1. Check the seasonal pattern: is this a historically favorable time to be long or short this commodity?
  2. Confirm with fundamentals: do supply/demand conditions support the seasonal trade this year?
  3. Confirm with technicals: is the price action setting up in the direction of the seasonal pattern?
  4. Size the trade: seasonal trades have ~60-70% win rates, not 100%. Size for the possibility of being in the 30-40% that fails.

A seasonal trade with fundamental confirmation has a significantly higher win rate than either alone. The combination is where the edge lives.

Quiz: Seasonal Trading

Question: It is February. The gasoline seasonal trade (long Feb, sell May) has a 75% win rate and averages +10%. Crude oil inventories are below average, and refinery maintenance is about to end. Should you take the trade?

Answer: Yes, with high conviction. The seasonal pattern is strong (75% win rate). The fundamental confirmation is excellent: below-average inventories mean less supply buffer, and refineries coming back online from maintenance will increase gasoline production and crude demand. The trade has seasonal + fundamental alignment. Enter long gasoline futures or RBOB gasoline ETF. Set stop at 8% below entry (expected return 10%, stop 8% = R:R ~1.25:1). This is a classic seasonal trade with fundamental tailwinds.

Calendar Spreads

Spread Trading — The Professional's Edge

A calendar spread (or time spread) involves simultaneously buying one delivery month and selling another delivery month of the same commodity. This is how professional commodity traders make money — not by guessing the direction of outright prices, but by trading the relative value between delivery months.

Why Spreads Are Superior

Spread trading has several advantages over outright positions:

  • Lower margin: Exchanges recognize that spreads are less risky than outright positions and require only 20-30% of outright margin. This means more capital efficiency.
  • Lower volatility: The spread between two delivery months of the same commodity moves much less than the outright price. This reduces the impact of unexpected events.
  • Fundamental purity: A spread trade eliminates macro noise (dollar moves, equity correlation, risk sentiment) and isolates the supply/demand signal specific to that commodity's term structure.
  • Seasonal reliability: Spread seasonal patterns are more reliable than outright seasonal patterns because they are driven by physical delivery economics (storage costs, transportation).

Common Spread Trades

Spread Structure When to Trade Expected Move
Bull Spread (Oil) Long near-month, Short far-month When expecting tighter supply (backwardation) Spread widens (near-month rises vs far)
Bear Spread (Oil) Short near-month, Long far-month When expecting oversupply (contango) Spread narrows or flips to contango
Corn Jul/Dec Long July (old crop), Short Dec (new crop) When spring planting is delayed or drought risk rises Old crop premium expands
Soybean Crush Long Meal + Long Oil, Short Soybeans When crush margins are compressed Products outperform beans

Quiz: Spread Trading

Question: WTI crude is in backwardation: the front month (CL1) is at $82, and the 6-month contract (CL6) is at $76. You believe OPEC will extend cuts, keeping the market tight. How do you trade this?

Answer: Execute a bull spread: Long CL1 (front month), Short CL6 (6-month). The current spread is $6 (backwardation). If OPEC extends cuts, the market stays tight, front-month demand increases, and backwardation deepens. The spread could widen to $8-10. Your profit would be $2,000-4,000 per spread (spread change x 1,000 barrels). Margin requirement is only ~$1,500 (vs $6,500 for outright). The trade is insulated from dollar moves, equity correlation, and even moderate demand changes — it is a pure supply tightness play. This is how institutional commodity traders express views.

Trend Following on Commodities

The Trend Is Your Friend (Especially in Commodities)

Commodities trend better than any other asset class. This is because supply/demand imbalances take time to resolve — a drought does not fix itself overnight, OPEC cuts last months, and mine development takes years. These structural forces create persistent price trends that systematic traders can exploit.

A Simple Trend-Following System

The classic commodity trend-following system uses moving average crossovers:

  • Entry (Long): When the 20-day EMA crosses above the 50-day EMA and price is above the 200-day SMA.
  • Entry (Short): When the 20-day EMA crosses below the 50-day EMA and price is below the 200-day SMA.
  • Stop Loss: 2x ATR (14-period) from entry. Wide enough to avoid noise, tight enough to limit losses.
  • Trailing Stop: Move stop to breakeven when trade reaches 1.5x ATR profit. Then trail at 2x ATR below highest close.
  • Position Size: Risk 1% of account per trade. Number of contracts = (1% of account) / (2 x ATR x contract size).

This system applied across 20-30 commodities simultaneously has generated positive returns in every decade since the 1970s, including during the 2008 financial crisis when most asset classes collapsed. The key: diversification across many commodities captures different trends at different times.

Quiz: Trend Following

Question: Your trend system signals long gold. Account is $100,000. Gold's 14-day ATR is $45/oz. Gold futures contract is 100 oz. How many contracts do you trade?

Answer: Risk per trade = 1% x $100,000 = $1,000. Dollar risk per contract = 2 x ATR x contract size = 2 x $45 x 100 = $9,000. Contracts = $1,000 / $9,000 = 0.11. You cannot trade even one standard contract. You need micro gold futures (/MGC = 10 oz). Dollar risk per micro = 2 x $45 x 10 = $900. Contracts = $1,000 / $900 = 1.1 = 1 micro contract. This demonstrates why professional trend-following requires significant capital. To diversify across 20 commodities at 1% risk each, you need $500K-1M minimum for standard contracts, or $50-100K for micros.

Mean-Reversion Strategies

When Commodities Snap Back

While commodities trend well over months, they also exhibit mean-reversion over shorter time frames (1-5 days). Extreme moves tend to partially reverse as the market digests information, repositions, and finds equilibrium. This creates opportunities for contrarian traders.

Mean-Reversion Indicators

Indicator Oversold (Buy) Overbought (Sell) Best Commodities
RSI (14) Below 25 Above 75 Gold, crude oil
Bollinger Band %B Below 0 (outside lower band) Above 1 (outside upper band) Natural gas, coffee
Z-Score (20-day) Below -2.0 Above +2.0 All — universal signal
% from 20-day MA More than -5% below More than +5% above Wheat, corn, soybeans

When Mean Reversion Fails

Mean-reversion is the opposite of trend following and can be devastatingly wrong during genuine regime changes. If you bought "oversold" crude oil at $100 in 2014, you rode it to $26 — a 74% loss. Mean-reversion works best in range-bound environments. During strong trends, mean-reversion signals become traps. Always check whether the commodity is trending (use 200-day MA or ADX > 25 filter) before applying mean-reversion strategies.

Quiz: Mean Reversion

Question: Natural gas drops 12% in two days on a mild weather forecast revision. RSI hits 18. Bollinger %B is at -0.3. The 200-day MA is $3.40 and current price is $3.10. Is this a mean-reversion buy?

Answer: Cautious yes, with a tight stop. The 12% drop in 2 days is extreme and likely an overreaction to a single weather model update. RSI at 18 and %B below 0 are both deeply oversold. However, price is below the 200-day MA ($3.10 < $3.40), suggesting the broader trend is bearish. The trade: buy for a 1-3 day bounce targeting a return to $3.25-3.30 (4-6% upside). Set a tight stop at $3.00 (3% risk). R:R is approximately 1.5:1. This is a tactical mean-reversion trade, not a position trade. Exit quickly if the next weather model confirms the mild forecast.

Correlation Pairs

Trading Commodity Correlations

Commodities do not move in isolation. They exist in a web of relationships driven by shared economic drivers, substitution effects, and physical linkages. Trading these correlations — especially when they diverge from historical norms — is one of the most profitable strategies in commodity markets.

The Soy/Corn Ratio — Nature's Built-In Reversion

The soybean/corn price ratio is one of the most reliable mean-reverting relationships in commodities. It typically ranges from 2.0 to 3.0 (soybeans are usually 2-3x the price of corn per bushel). The mechanism is biological:

  • When the ratio exceeds 2.8, soybeans are relatively expensive. Farmers plant more soybeans next season, increasing supply and pushing the ratio down.
  • When the ratio drops below 2.2, corn is relatively expensive. Farmers plant more corn, reducing soybean acreage, and the ratio rises.

The beauty is that reversion is guaranteed by farmer behavior — it just takes one planting season (6-12 months). This gives you a natural edge with a known timeline for mean reversion.

Quiz: Correlation Pairs

Question: The oil/natural gas ratio (oil price / gas price) is at 28x (normally 20-25x). What does this mean and how do you trade it?

Answer: A ratio of 28x means oil is expensive relative to gas (or gas is cheap relative to oil). The trade: long natural gas, short crude oil in a dollar-neutral spread. The expectation is that the ratio reverts toward 22-25x over the next 1-3 months. This can happen via gas rising (weather event, storage draw), oil falling (OPEC increase, demand weakness), or both. The spread trade is insulated from macro moves (if both energy prices move together, the spread is unaffected). The risk: oil/gas correlation has weakened over the past decade due to US shale (domestic gas abundant, oil globally priced), so the ratio can stay elevated longer than expected. Use a stop at 32x (above the trade entry at 28x).

Risk Management

Surviving Commodity Markets

Commodity markets have destroyed more trading accounts than any other asset class. Leverage, volatility, and gap risk combine to create an environment where a single bad trade can wipe out months of profits. Risk management is not optional — it is the primary skill of successful commodity traders.

1

Position Sizing

Never risk more than 1-2% of account on any single trade. Use ATR-based sizing. Reduce size in high-volatility environments.

2

Stop Losses

Always use stops. Set them at 2x ATR for trend trades, 1x ATR for mean-reversion. Never move stops further from entry.

3

Correlation Limits

Do not hold 5 long energy positions simultaneously. Limit correlated exposure. One bad OPEC meeting hits them all.

4

Max Drawdown Rule

If account drops 10% from peak, reduce all positions by 50%. If 15%, go flat and reassess. Preservation of capital is rule #1.

The Commodity-Specific Risks

Risk Description Mitigation
Limit Moves Exchanges halt trading when prices move too far in one day. You cannot exit. Natgas, grains are common. Use options instead of futures for event trades. Never hold full position into known binary events.
Gap Risk Commodities gap on weekend weather changes, Sunday OPEC decisions, Monday market opens. Reduce position size before weekends. Use options for overnight/weekend hedges.
Roll Risk Futures expire. You must roll or close. Roll can be costly in contango or during low liquidity. Roll 5-7 days before expiry. Never hold into delivery month unless you want physical delivery.
Liquidity Risk Back-month contracts have thin liquidity. Wide bid-ask spreads. Slippage on entries/exits. Trade only front 2-3 months. Check open interest before entering. Avoid thinly traded commodities.
Negative Prices WTI went to -$37 in April 2020. Storage full, no buyers. Losses can exceed 100% of position value. Never hold physical-delivery contracts into the delivery window. Use ETFs for long-term holds.
The Risk Management Hierarchy
1. Position Size → 2. Stop Loss → 3. Correlation Limits → 4. Max Drawdown → 5. Hedge
Quiz: Risk Management

Question: You hold 3 long positions: crude oil (2 contracts), heating oil (1 contract), and gasoline (1 contract). All three are energy. What is wrong with this portfolio?

Answer: Massive correlation risk. All three commodities are highly correlated (0.85+). If OPEC surprises with a production increase or a global demand shock hits, all three positions will lose simultaneously. You effectively have a single concentrated bet on energy prices with 4x the notional exposure you think you have. Fix: reduce to 1 crude oil contract and diversify into uncorrelated commodities (gold, wheat, or copper). A properly diversified commodity portfolio should have no more than 30-40% of risk in any single sector.

Building a Commodity Portfolio

The Commodity Portfolio Framework

A well-constructed commodity portfolio should be diversified across sectors, strategies, and time horizons. Here is a framework for building one:

Model Commodity Portfolio (ETF-Based)

Allocation ETF Weight Role
Gold GLD or IAU 25% Safe haven, inflation hedge, portfolio stabilizer
Silver SLV 10% Industrial + monetary, leveraged gold exposure
Energy Stocks XLE 20% Oil/gas exposure without contango. Dividends
Agribusiness MOO 15% Agricultural commodity exposure via equities
Broad Commodities PDBC 15% Diversified basket, optimized roll, tax-efficient
Gold Miners GDX 10% Leveraged gold exposure, dividends
Cash / T-Bills SHV 5% Dry powder for opportunistic trades

Portfolio Construction Principles

  1. Diversify by sector: Energy, precious metals, agriculture, industrial metals. No single sector should exceed 35% of the portfolio.
  2. Prefer equities over futures ETFs: For buy-and-hold allocations, commodity stocks (XLE, MOO, GDX) outperform futures-based ETFs due to no contango, dividends, and operational leverage.
  3. Physical metals for core: GLD and SLV hold physical metal — no roll cost, no contango. These are the best long-term commodity positions.
  4. Use futures for tactical: Reserve futures trading for short-term opportunistic trades (seasonal patterns, event trades, spreads). Do not hold futures long-term.
  5. Rebalance quarterly: Commodities can diverge significantly over 3 months. Rebalancing captures mean reversion across sectors.
  6. Size the allocation: Commodities should represent 5-15% of a total investment portfolio. More than 15% adds volatility without proportional return benefit.
Quiz: Portfolio Construction

Question: You manage a $500,000 investment portfolio and want to add commodity exposure. How much should you allocate and how would you structure it?

Answer: Allocate 10% ($50,000) to commodities. Structure: $12,500 in GLD (25% — gold core), $5,000 in SLV (10% — silver), $10,000 in XLE (20% — energy equities), $7,500 in MOO (15% — agribusiness), $7,500 in PDBC (15% — broad commodities), $5,000 in GDX (10% — gold miners), $2,500 in cash (5% — dry powder). This gives you diversified exposure across all major commodity sectors with a preference for equity vehicles that pay dividends and avoid contango. Rebalance quarterly. Total expected dividend yield: ~2%. Expected correlation with S&P 500: ~0.3 (meaningful diversification). Expected volatility: ~15% (vs 20% for SPY).

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways — Part 6

  • Seasonal patterns in commodities are grounded in physical reality (crop cycles, weather, driving seasons) and have 55-75% win rates over 20+ years.
  • Calendar spreads are how professionals trade — lower margin, lower volatility, purer fundamental signal. Learn bull/bear spreads and crush/crack spreads.
  • Commodities trend better than any other asset class because supply/demand imbalances take time to resolve. Trend-following has been profitable every decade since the 1970s.
  • Mean-reversion works for short-term trades (1-5 days) when RSI/Bollinger signals are extreme. Fails during genuine regime changes — always check the trend first.
  • Trade correlation pairs (gold/silver, soy/corn, oil/gas ratio) for lower risk and natural mean-reversion built into the physical markets.
  • Risk management is the primary skill: 1-2% position sizing, ATR-based stops, correlation limits (max 35% in one sector), and max drawdown rules.
  • Build a commodity portfolio with 5-15% of total assets. Prefer physical-backed ETFs and commodity stocks over futures ETFs. Diversify across sectors and rebalance quarterly.
