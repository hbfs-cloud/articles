Natural Gas — Volatility King of Energy Markets

Henry Hub — The Benchmark

Understanding the Natural Gas Market

Natural gas is the second most important energy commodity after crude oil. It heats homes, generates electricity (40% of US power), and is increasingly used as a feedstock for chemicals and fertilizers. Yet it trades completely differently from oil — and is far more volatile.

The US benchmark is Henry Hub, named after a pipeline hub in Erath, Louisiana, where 13 interstate and intrastate pipelines converge. Henry Hub natural gas futures (ticker: NG) are the most liquid gas contract in the world, traded on NYMEX.

Why Gas Is More Volatile Than Oil

Natural gas is inherently more volatile for three reasons:

  1. Storage is limited and seasonal: Gas must be injected into underground storage during summer and withdrawn during winter. If winter is colder than expected, storage depletes faster, and prices spike. There is no OPEC for gas — no swing producer to add supply quickly.
  2. Demand swings with weather: A cold snap in Chicago can increase US gas demand by 10 Bcf/day overnight. Oil demand does not swing like this. Temperature is the single most important variable.
  3. Pipeline constraints: Gas is delivered via pipeline (unlike oil which can be shipped globally). If a pipeline goes down or a region faces a cold snap, local prices can spike to $100+/MMBtu while the rest of the country trades at $3.

The 2022 European Gas Crisis

In August 2022, European TTF gas hit EUR 340/MWh — equivalent to ~$100/MMBtu, while US Henry Hub traded at $9/MMBtu. That is an 11x price differential between two markets for the same molecule. The cause: Russia cut pipeline gas deliveries to Europe, and there was not enough LNG capacity to fill the gap. This event triggered Europe's energy crisis, industrial shutdowns, and a massive buildout of LNG import terminals. It also demonstrated that gas markets are still largely regional, not global — unlike oil.

Quiz: Henry Hub Basics

Question: You buy 1 natural gas futures contract at $3.50/MMBtu. The price rises to $4.00. What is your profit?

Answer: Contract size is 10,000 MMBtu. Price change = $0.50/MMBtu. Profit = 10,000 x $0.50 = $5,000. On ~$2,500 initial margin, that is a 200% return on margin from a 14% move in the underlying. This illustrates why natural gas futures are among the most profitable — and most dangerous — instruments for retail traders. A $0.50 adverse move would produce a $5,000 loss and a margin call.

Seasonality — The Annual Cycle

The Natural Gas Seasonal Pattern

Natural gas is the most seasonal commodity in the world. The annual cycle is driven by two competing forces: heating demand (winter) and cooling demand (summer air conditioning), overlaid with a mandatory storage injection/withdrawal cycle.

Withdrawal Season (Nov-Mar)

Peak demand. Gas is withdrawn from underground storage to heat homes and generate power. Cold snaps can spike demand and deplete storage faster than expected. Prices tend to peak in January-February.

Shoulder Season (Apr-May)

Transition period. Heating demand fades, cooling demand has not started. Lowest demand of the year. Prices often bottom in April. This is when storage injections begin.

Injection Season (Apr-Oct)

Gas is injected into underground storage to prepare for next winter. The market watches weekly injections vs the 5-year average. Below-average injections = winter shortage risk = bullish.

Cooling Season (Jun-Aug)

Summer heat drives air conditioning demand, which increasingly relies on gas-fired power plants. Extreme heat waves can rival winter demand peaks. Summer 2023 saw record cooling demand.

Trading the Seasonal Pattern

The classic seasonal natgas trade:

  • Go long in April-May when prices typically bottom during the shoulder season.
  • Hold through summer injection season as the market prices in winter risk.
  • Exit in October-November before the first winter data either confirms or denies the cold winter thesis.

This trade works about 60% of the time over the past 20 years. The key risk: a mild winter forecast in October can collapse the trade. The key boost: a La Nina pattern (associated with colder winters in the US) can amplify gains significantly.

Natgas Seasonal Signal
Storage Below 5Y Avg + Cold Winter Forecast + La Nina = Maximum Bullish Setup
Quiz: Seasonal Trading

Question: It is late October. Storage is 5% below the 5-year average. NOAA just issued a La Nina advisory (associated with colder US winters). Henry Hub is at $3.20. What is your view?

Answer: Bullish. Below-average storage entering winter means less buffer. La Nina winters tend to be colder in the eastern US (the primary heating demand region). $3.20 is a reasonable entry for a winter trade targeting $4.50-5.00 if the cold materializes. Set a stop at $2.80 (below the October support). Risk/reward is approximately 1:3. This is the textbook seasonal setup that gas traders wait for all year.

EIA Storage Reports

The Weekly Storage Report — Gas Trader's Bible

The EIA Natural Gas Storage Report, released every Thursday at 10:30 AM ET, is the most important weekly data point for natural gas traders. It reports the change in underground storage levels, measured in Bcf (billion cubic feet).

How Storage Works

The US has approximately 4,700 Bcf of total storage capacity spread across three regions: East, Midwest, and South Central (which includes salt cavern and non-salt storage). The annual cycle targets ending storage of approximately 3,500-3,800 Bcf before winter begins (November 1) and a minimum of approximately 1,200-1,500 Bcf at the end of winter (March 31).

Trading the Storage Report

The storage report is a high-frequency trading event. Here is the workflow:

  1. Before 10:30 AM: Check the consensus estimate (Bloomberg survey, Twitter polls, ICE settlement). Know the expected number.
  2. 10:30 AM sharp: The number drops. Compare actual vs. expected. If the difference is >10 Bcf, there will be a move.
  3. First 2 minutes: Algo-driven spike or drop. Gas can move $0.10-0.20 ($1,000-2,000 per contract) in seconds.
  4. Next 30 minutes: The market digests regional breakdowns, context, and repositions. Often reverses the initial spike if the headline number was misleading.
  5. Rest of day: If the number was truly surprising (miss >20 Bcf), the trend continues. If it was a modest surprise, prices revert.

Professional gas traders do not trade the headline number — they trade the deviation from consensus and the implied supply/demand balance change. A -95 Bcf withdrawal when -80 was expected is far more bullish than a -150 Bcf withdrawal when -148 was expected.

Quiz: Storage Analysis

Question: The EIA reports a storage injection of +82 Bcf for the week. Consensus was +95 Bcf. The 5-year average injection for this week is +90 Bcf. Total storage is 2,450 Bcf vs. the 5-year average of 2,600 Bcf. Interpret this data.

Answer: Moderately bullish. The injection was 13 Bcf less than expected and 8 Bcf less than the 5-year average, meaning demand is running above normal. The storage deficit vs. the 5-year average widened from the previous week. At 2,450 Bcf, storage is 150 Bcf (5.8%) below average. If this injection shortfall persists for the remaining injection weeks, the market will enter winter with storage well below average — a bullish setup. Expect gas to rally $0.05-0.10 on this report.

Weather — The Key Driver

Weather Trading in Natural Gas

No other commodity is as weather-dependent as natural gas. Temperature is the single most important short-term price driver. Professional gas traders are as much meteorologists as they are financial analysts.

Heating Degree Days and Cooling Degree Days

HDD and CDD Explained

The market measures weather impact using Heating Degree Days (HDD) and Cooling Degree Days (CDD):

  • HDD = max(0, 65 - average daily temperature). If the average temp is 35F, HDD = 30. Higher HDD = more heating demand = more gas consumption.
  • CDD = max(0, average daily temperature - 65). If the average temp is 95F, CDD = 30. Higher CDD = more cooling demand = more gas-fired power generation.

The weekly and 14-day HDD/CDD forecasts are the most closely watched numbers in the gas market. When the forecast model updates show a cold snap (higher-than-expected HDD), gas prices spike. When the cold snap moderates in the next model run, prices drop. Intraday gas trading is largely a game of weather model interpretation.

The Polar Vortex Trade

The most extreme natural gas price events are caused by polar vortex disruptions — when the jet stream buckles and sends Arctic air deep into the US. The most famous examples:

  • February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri): Texas froze. Gas demand spiked as heating maxed out. Simultaneously, gas production froze (wellheads, pipelines). Henry Hub spiked from $2.70 to $23.86 in a week — a 780% move. Local Texas hub prices hit $200-400/MMBtu.
  • January 2014 (Polar Vortex): Henry Hub jumped from $4.30 to $6.50 in two weeks. Localized prices in New England hit $35/MMBtu due to pipeline constraints.
  • February 2019: A milder polar vortex event pushed gas from $2.70 to $3.60 (33% move).

The pattern is consistent: polar vortex events simultaneously increase demand (heating) and reduce supply (freeze-offs). This supply-demand double squeeze produces the most violent price spikes in any commodity market.

Weather Impact Formula
Price Impact = (Forecast HDD - Expected HDD) x Population-Weighted Sensitivity x Storage Context

Weather Model Whipsaw Risk

Weather models update every 6-12 hours, and each update can shift the 7-14 day forecast significantly. Gas prices often whipsaw on consecutive model runs: the 06Z GFS shows a cold blast, gas spikes 5%. The 12Z ECMWF moderates the cold, gas drops 3%. The next day's 00Z GFS confirms the cold, gas spikes again. This is not a market for the faint-hearted. Many experienced gas traders use small position sizes and wide stops specifically because of weather model volatility.

Quiz: Weather Trading

Question: It is early January. Storage is 10% below the 5-year average. The latest GFS and ECMWF models both show a polar vortex disruption sending Arctic air into the eastern US for the next 10 days. Henry Hub is at $3.80. What is the setup?

Answer: This is a high-conviction bullish setup with extreme upside potential. Below-average storage means less buffer. Both major models agreeing on a polar vortex reduces weather model uncertainty (usually they disagree). Eastern US is the most population-dense region, so the HDD impact is maximized. The target is $5.00-6.00 in a normal cold snap, $8-10+ if production freeze-offs occur (supply disruption). However, position size conservatively because if the polar vortex fails to materialize or models reverse, the drop will be equally violent. Use options (call spreads) to cap downside risk while maintaining upside exposure.

LNG Export Dynamics

LNG — Connecting Gas Markets Globally

Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG) is transforming natural gas from a regional commodity into a global one. By cooling gas to -260F (-162C), it shrinks to 1/600th of its volume, making it possible to ship by tanker anywhere in the world. This is the most important structural change in the gas market in a generation.

How LNG Changes the Game

Before LNG After LNG
Gas was pipeline-only (regional) Gas can be shipped globally (like oil)
US gas was stranded (oversupplied, cheap) US exports 12+ Bcf/d, supporting Henry Hub prices
Asian/European buyers dependent on pipeline suppliers (Russia, Algeria) Diverse supply options, reduced dependence on any one supplier
Price differentials of $15-20/MMBtu between regions Narrowing differentials (still $5-15 gap)
Henry Hub price unaffected by global events European/Asian demand now pulls on US gas supply (price linkage)

US LNG Export Capacity

The US went from zero LNG exports in 2015 to the world's largest LNG exporter in 2023, surpassing Qatar and Australia. Key export terminals: Sabine Pass (LA), Cameron (LA), Freeport (TX), Corpus Christi (TX), and Elba Island (GA). Total capacity exceeds 14 Bcf/day and is expected to reach 20+ Bcf/day by 2028 as new projects come online.

For gas traders, this means Henry Hub is now structurally supported by global LNG demand. A cold winter in Europe or a heat wave in Asia can pull US gas prices higher via increased LNG exports. This is a fundamental shift from the pre-2016 era when Henry Hub traded in isolation.

Russia-Europe Energy Dynamics

The 2022 Russia-Ukraine war permanently reshaped European gas markets. Russia supplied ~40% of Europe's gas via pipelines (Nord Stream, Yamal, Brotherhood). The Nord Stream explosions and voluntary EU sanctions reduced Russian pipeline gas to near zero, creating a structural deficit that Europe filled with LNG — primarily from the US and Qatar.

Quiz: LNG Market Dynamics

Question: European TTF gas is at EUR 45/MWh (~$14/MMBtu). Henry Hub is at $3.50/MMBtu. LNG shipping and liquefaction costs are approximately $3.50/MMBtu. Is there an arbitrage opportunity?

Answer: Yes, a large one. Delivered cost of US LNG to Europe: $3.50 (Henry Hub) + $3.50 (liquefaction + shipping) = $7.00/MMBtu. European TTF is at $14.00/MMBtu. The arbitrage spread is $7.00/MMBtu — a massive margin. This is why every available LNG cargo is being diverted to Europe. This arbitrage also supports Henry Hub prices: increased LNG export demand tightens the US domestic market. The arbitrage will narrow as more LNG capacity comes online (2025-2028) and European demand adjusts.

Extreme Volatility Patterns

Why Natural Gas Is the Volatility King

Natural gas has the highest annualized volatility of any major commodity — typically 40-60% compared to 25-35% for crude oil and 15-20% for gold. In extreme years, gas volatility exceeds 80%. This creates both enormous opportunity and enormous risk.

Surviving Natgas Volatility

Rules for trading the most volatile commodity:

  1. Position size: Never risk more than 2% of your account on a single natgas trade. One bad day can move 10%.
  2. Use options: Buy calls instead of going long futures. Your maximum loss is the premium paid, regardless of how far gas moves against you.
  3. Micro contracts: Trade /QG (2,500 MMBtu, 1/4 size of /NG). Reduces dollar exposure proportionally.
  4. Stop discipline: Use wide stops (at least $0.15-0.25 from entry) because natgas whipsaws intraday. Tight stops get blown out by noise.
  5. Avoid Friday afternoon: Weekend weather model uncertainty can cause Monday gaps of $0.30+.
Natgas Risk Rule
Max Position Size = (2% of Account) / (ATR x Contract Size) = Number of Contracts
Quiz: Volatility Management

Question: You have a $50,000 account and want to trade natural gas futures. The 14-day ATR is $0.18/MMBtu. How many standard contracts (/NG) can you trade using the 2% risk rule?

Answer: Max risk = 2% x $50,000 = $1,000. Dollar risk per contract = ATR x contract size = $0.18 x 10,000 = $1,800 per contract. Since $1,800 > $1,000, you cannot trade even ONE standard contract within the 2% rule. You should trade micro contracts (/QG at 2,500 MMBtu): Dollar risk = $0.18 x 2,500 = $450 per micro. You can trade 2 micro contracts ($900 risk). This is why natgas is not suitable for small accounts — even with micro contracts, the volatility demands significant capital.

The UNG Death Spiral

UNG is the most popular natural gas ETF and also one of the worst investments in history. Since its inception in 2007, UNG has lost approximately 97% of its value — not because gas prices crashed 97%, but because chronic contango destroys value every month. When UNG rolls its expiring front-month contract into the next month, it consistently sells low and buys high. Over 15+ years, this roll cost has been devastating. UNG should only be used for short-term trades (1-5 days) during weather events or storage surprises. It is never a long-term hold.

The Best Natgas Exposure for Beginners

For beginners who want natural gas exposure:

  • Best equity pick: EQT — the largest pure-play US gas producer. High beta to gas prices, but also hedges 60%+ of production. Less volatile than futures.
  • Best infrastructure pick: LNG (Cheniere) — makes money on export fees regardless of gas price level. Lower beta but more stable earnings.
  • Best ETF: FCG (First Trust Natural Gas) — basket of gas producers. Diversified, pays dividends, no contango risk.
  • Avoid: UNG and BOIL for any holding period longer than a few days.
Quiz: Vehicle Selection

Question: You believe natural gas prices will rise significantly over the next 3 months due to a colder-than-expected winter. You have $20,000 to invest. Compare UNG vs EQT as your vehicle.

Answer: EQT is clearly superior. Over 3 months, UNG would lose 5-10% of its value to contango roll costs alone, even if gas prices are flat. If gas rises 30%, UNG might only rise 20% after contango drag. EQT, on the other hand, would rise 40-50% on a 30% gas move (operational leverage), plus you collect any dividend. EQT also has a natural floor from its hedging program (if gas drops, their hedges provide cash flow support). The only scenario where UNG wins is a massive, sudden spike over 1-3 days (e.g., polar vortex) where contango has not had time to erode value. For a 3-month thesis, EQT every time.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways — Part 3

  • Henry Hub is the US natural gas benchmark ($3-6/MMBtu typical range). Gas is far more volatile than oil (40-60% annualized vol vs 25-35%).
  • Seasonality dominates: withdrawal season (Nov-Mar) is bullish, shoulder season (Apr-May) is the bottom, injection season builds winter supply.
  • The EIA Storage Report (Thursday 10:30 AM ET) is the weekly catalyst. Trade the deviation from consensus, not the headline number.
  • Weather (HDD/CDD forecasts) is the primary short-term driver. Polar vortex events can produce 100%+ moves in days.
  • LNG exports have linked US gas to global markets. European TTF and Asian JKM prices now pull on Henry Hub via LNG arbitrage.
  • Avoid UNG for anything except 1-5 day trades. Chronic contango has destroyed 97% of its value since inception. Use EQT, FCG, or micro futures instead.
  • Position size conservatively: 2% risk rule means most retail accounts can only trade 1-2 micro contracts (/QG) safely.
