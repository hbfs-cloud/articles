WTI vs Brent — The Two Benchmarks

Understanding Oil Benchmarks

There are over 160 different crude oil blends traded globally, but pricing for all of them revolves around two benchmarks: West Texas Intermediate (WTI) and Brent Crude. These are the reference prices — when someone says "oil is at $80," they mean one of these two.

The distinction matters because WTI and Brent often trade at different prices, and the spread between them contains information about global supply dynamics, transportation bottlenecks, and regional supply/demand imbalances.

Why Brent Trades at a Premium

Brent crude typically costs $2-5 more per barrel than WTI. The reason is logistics. Brent is produced near the sea and can be shipped anywhere in the world. WTI is produced inland and must be piped to the Gulf Coast or Cushing before it can be exported. This transportation cost and pipeline capacity constraint creates a structural discount for WTI.

However, the spread is not constant. When US inventories at Cushing are extremely high (as in 2020), WTI can trade at massive discounts to Brent. When Cushing is nearly empty, WTI can trade at a premium. The WTI-Brent spread is itself a traded instrument — hedge funds actively trade this spread as a pure logistics and geopolitics play.

Quiz: Oil Benchmarks

Question: WTI is at $78 and Brent is at $83. The spread is $5. Cushing inventory data shows stocks dropping rapidly. What do you expect will happen to the spread?

Answer: The spread should narrow (WTI should rise relative to Brent). Cushing is the delivery point for WTI futures. When Cushing inventories drop, WTI becomes scarcer at its delivery point, pushing its price higher. The WTI discount narrows because the logistics bottleneck eases. In extreme cases (Cushing near tank bottoms), WTI can trade at a premium to Brent.

OPEC and Its Influence

The OPEC Cartel

OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) is the most powerful cartel in the world. Founded in 1960, it coordinates production levels among its 13 member states to influence global oil prices. Together with Russia and other allies (OPEC+), they control roughly 40% of global oil production and 80% of proven reserves.

How OPEC Works

OPEC's power comes from its ability to restrict supply. When oil prices are too low, members agree to cut production. When prices are too high (risking demand destruction), they increase production. The mechanics are simple — the diplomacy is not.

The key meeting is the OPEC+ ministerial conference, held roughly every 2 months. The outcome — production cut, increase, or rollover — moves oil prices by $3-10/barrel instantly. Traders position ahead of these meetings based on leaks, rhetoric, and geopolitical context.

Saudi Arabia — The Swing Producer

Saudi Arabia is the only country in the world with significant spare capacity (2-3 million barrels per day that can be brought online within 90 days). This makes them the swing producer — the country that can single-handedly move the global supply balance. When Saudi Arabia cuts, prices rise. When Saudi Arabia floods the market (as in 2014 and 2020), prices collapse.

The Saudi strategy depends on their fiscal breakeven oil price — the price they need to balance their budget. Estimates range from $80-90/barrel. Below that, they are burning reserves. This creates a natural floor: Saudi Arabia is motivated to keep oil above their breakeven price.

OPEC+ vs. US Shale — The New Rivalry

The shale revolution changed the oil market permanently. US production went from 5 million barrels/day in 2008 to 13 million in 2024 — making the US the world's largest oil producer. OPEC's share of global supply declined, and their ability to set prices was diminished.

Quiz: OPEC Mechanics

Question: OPEC announces a surprise 2M bbl/day production cut effective immediately. WTI is at $72. What is the likely price reaction and why?

Answer: WTI would likely jump $5-10 (to $77-82) within hours. A 2M bbl/day cut represents roughly 2% of global supply — a significant reduction. The market prices in the tighter supply balance, reduced inventories over coming months, and the signal that OPEC is defending a price floor. The magnitude depends on demand outlook, but $5-10 is typical for a cut of this size. The curve would also shift into backwardation as near-term supply tightens.

Key Drivers of Oil Prices

What Moves Oil

Oil prices are driven by a complex web of factors. Here is the hierarchy of importance, from most to least impactful:

1

OPEC Decisions

Production cuts or increases are the single biggest driver. A 1M bbl/d cut can move prices $5-10.

2

Geopolitical Risk

Wars, sanctions, and chokepoint threats (Hormuz, Suez, Malacca). Can add $5-20 risk premium overnight.

3

Inventory Data

Weekly EIA reports (Wed) and API reports (Tue night). Unexpected builds or draws move prices $1-3.

4

Global Demand

China PMI, global GDP growth, air travel data, truck freight volumes. Structural demand shifts take months to price.

5

US Dollar

Oil is priced in USD. A stronger dollar makes oil more expensive for foreign buyers, reducing demand. Inverse correlation of ~-0.5.

6

Rig Counts

Baker Hughes rig count (weekly, Friday). Rising rig counts = more future supply = bearish. Lagged indicator (3-6 month delay).

The Oil-Dollar Inverse Correlation

Because oil is denominated in US dollars, there is a persistent inverse correlation between the dollar index (DXY) and oil prices. When the dollar strengthens, oil becomes more expensive in local currencies for non-US buyers, reducing demand at the margin. When the dollar weakens, oil effectively becomes cheaper for the rest of the world, boosting demand.

Trading the Oil-Dollar Link

The correlation is not perfect (about -0.5 over rolling 1-year windows), but it is persistent enough to be useful:

  • If you are long oil and the dollar is strengthening, your trade faces a headwind.
  • The best oil longs happen when oil fundamentals are bullish AND the dollar is weakening. Double tailwind.
  • Watch the Fed. Hawkish Fed = stronger dollar = headwind for oil. Dovish Fed = weaker dollar = tailwind for oil.

Quiz: Oil Price Drivers

Question: It is July. China PMI came in at 51.5 (expansion). The US dollar just weakened 2% on dovish Fed minutes. EIA reported a 5M barrel draw in crude inventories. What direction is oil going?

Answer: Strongly bullish. Three tailwinds align: (1) Summer driving season = peak seasonal demand. (2) China expanding = growing marginal demand. (3) Weak dollar = oil is cheaper for non-US buyers. (4) Large inventory draw = demand exceeding supply. All four factors support higher oil prices. This is the kind of confluence that can produce a $5-8 rally over a few weeks.

Reading EIA Reports

The EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report

The EIA (Energy Information Administration) weekly report is the most market-moving data release in the oil market. Published every Wednesday at 10:30 AM ET, it reports US crude oil inventories, production, imports, exports, and refinery utilization. Here is how to read it like a professional:

Key Data Points

Metric What It Tells You Bullish Signal Bearish Signal
Crude Inventories Change Weekly build or draw in US storage Large draw (>3M barrels) Large build (>3M barrels)
Cushing OK Stocks Inventories at the WTI delivery hub Below 25M barrels Above 45M barrels
US Production Domestic output (M bbl/d) Declining or flat Rising to new highs
Gasoline Inventories Product demand proxy Large draw (strong demand) Large build (weak demand)
Distillate Inventories Diesel/heating oil demand Below 5-year average Above 5-year average
Refinery Utilization % of capacity online >93% (high throughput) <85% (maintenance/demand issue)
Net Imports US dependence on foreign oil Declining (energy independence) Rising (growing dependence)

The API Preview

The American Petroleum Institute (API) publishes its own inventory report every Tuesday at 4:30 PM ET — the night before the EIA report. The API data is survey-based (voluntary reporting) while EIA is mandatory, so API is less accurate but gives the market a preview.

Trading strategy: if API shows a large draw but the market does not move much overnight, it may be pricing in the EIA confirmation. If EIA then shows an even larger draw, the move can be amplified. If EIA contradicts API (API draw but EIA build), the reversal can be violent.

Inventory Analysis Rule
If Actual Draw > Expected Draw AND Inventories < 5-Year Average = Strong Buy Signal
Quiz: EIA Report Analysis

Question: The EIA report shows: Crude inventories -4.2M (expected -1.5M), Cushing stocks -1.8M (to 22M barrels total), Gasoline inventories -2.1M, Refinery utilization 94.5%. What is the market reaction?

Answer: This is a very bullish report. (1) The crude draw was nearly 3x the expected — a major surprise. (2) Cushing at 22M is getting close to operational minimums (~20M), which tightens the WTI delivery market. (3) Gasoline draw signals strong demand. (4) Refinery utilization near maximum means refineries are running full-out to meet demand. Expect oil to rally $1.50-3.00 within the first hour of trading after release. The curve should shift further into backwardation.

Geopolitical Risk Premium

When Geopolitics Moves Oil

Oil is the most geopolitically sensitive commodity. Unlike gold (which rises on fear) or wheat (which rises on weather), oil prices respond to specific geopolitical events that threaten physical supply. The market assigns a risk premium — an extra $5-20/barrel above fundamental value — when supply disruption risks are elevated.

The Strait of Hormuz — Oil's Jugular

The Strait of Hormuz is a 21-mile-wide waterway between Iran and Oman through which 21% of global oil supply passes daily. Iran has repeatedly threatened to close it in response to sanctions or military action. In March 2026, renewed tensions over Iran's nuclear program have pushed the risk premium to an estimated $8-12/barrel.

A full closure of Hormuz would be catastrophic: 21 million barrels per day would be stranded. Saudi Arabia has limited bypass capacity via the East-West Pipeline (~5M bbl/d), but 16M bbl/d would be offline. Oil would spike to $150+ within days. This is why every oil trader monitors Iran rhetoric closely.

The current Iran/Hormuz tensions (as of March 2026) represent the most significant geopolitical risk premium in oil since the 2022 Russia-Ukraine escalation.

Trading Geopolitical Events

Rule of thumb for geopolitical oil spikes: buy the rumor, sell the news — unless there is actual supply disruption. Most geopolitical scares fade within 2-4 weeks because the feared disruption does not materialize. However, when actual supply is taken offline (Russia 2022, Kuwait 1990), the rally can last months. The key question is always: "Are actual barrels being removed from the market, or is this just fear?"

Quiz: Geopolitical Risk

Question: Iran announces military exercises in the Strait of Hormuz. Oil spikes 5% overnight. No actual shipping disruption has occurred. Should you buy, sell, or hold?

Answer: This is likely a sell (or at least, do not chase the spike). Military exercises are a show of force, not an actual blockade. The 5% spike is a fear premium that will likely fade over 1-2 weeks if no disruption materializes. Historically, most Hormuz scares revert to prior levels within 10-15 trading days. However, if subsequent news reports actual mine-laying, tanker attacks, or insurance companies pulling coverage from Gulf shipping, the calculus changes entirely and the premium may be justified. Watch insurance premiums and tanker routing changes as the real signal.

XLE vs USO — Why Stocks Beat Futures ETFs

For most investors, energy stocks (XLE) are a better oil vehicle than futures ETFs (USO). Here is why:

  • No roll cost: Stocks do not expire. No contango drag.
  • Dividends: XLE yields ~3.5%. USO pays zero.
  • Leverage to oil: Energy stocks move roughly 1.5-2x the percentage move in oil (operational leverage). If oil rises 10%, XLE might rise 15-20%.
  • Risk management: Companies hedge their own production. ExxonMobil hedges 40-60% of next year's production, providing earnings stability.

The main downside: stock-specific risk. A company can miss earnings, have an oil spill, or face regulatory issues that decouples it from oil. XLE diversifies this across 25+ companies.

Oil Trading Hours

WTI futures trade nearly 24 hours (Sunday 6PM to Friday 5PM ET, with a 1-hour daily break). Most volume concentrates between 9:00 AM and 2:30 PM ET, with a spike at 10:30 AM when EIA data drops. Brent futures trade on ICE from 8:00 PM to 6:00 PM ET. If you trade oil ETFs (USO, XLE), remember they only trade during US market hours (9:30 AM - 4:00 PM ET) — you will miss overnight moves.

Quiz: Oil Trading Vehicle Selection

Question: You believe oil will rise 20% over the next 6 months due to OPEC cuts and strong demand. You have $10,000 to invest. Which vehicle maximizes your risk-adjusted return?

Answer: XLE is the optimal choice for a 6-month bullish oil view with $10,000. Reasons: (1) XLE gives ~1.5x leverage to oil moves through operational leverage, so a 20% oil rally could translate to 30% for XLE. (2) You collect ~1.75% in dividends over 6 months. (3) No contango risk (unlike USO). (4) Very liquid options if you want to add leverage via call spreads. (5) Diversified across 25+ energy companies, reducing single-stock risk. A futures position would be too leveraged for $10,000 on a 6-month horizon (margin calls during pullbacks would be brutal). USO would lose 3-5% to contango over 6 months, reducing your net return.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways — Part 2

  • WTI and Brent are the two global oil benchmarks. Brent typically trades at a $2-5 premium due to seaborne logistics. The WTI-Brent spread is itself a tradeable instrument.
  • OPEC+ controls ~40% of global production. Saudi Arabia is the swing producer with 3M bbl/d spare capacity. OPEC decisions are the single biggest price driver.
  • Six key drivers: OPEC decisions, geopolitics, EIA inventory data, global demand (China PMI), US dollar strength, and rig counts.
  • The EIA weekly report (Wednesday 10:30 AM ET) is the most market-moving oil data release. Watch crude draws vs. expectations and Cushing levels.
  • The Strait of Hormuz handles 21% of global oil supply. Iran/Hormuz tensions in 2026 are the top geopolitical risk, adding $8-12 risk premium.
  • For most investors, energy stocks (XLE) beat futures ETFs (USO) due to no roll cost, dividends, and operational leverage to oil.
