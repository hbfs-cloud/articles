Crop Cycles & Growing Seasons

The Agricultural Calendar

Unlike energy or metals, agricultural commodities are governed by biological time. You can only plant once, grow once, and harvest once per year (in most climates). This creates a rigid annual cycle that drives predictable price patterns — and creates enormous risk when weather disrupts the cycle.

US Crop Calendar (Northern Hemisphere)

Crop Planting Growing Harvest Critical Weather Window
Corn Apr-May Jun-Aug Sep-Nov July (pollination — heat kills yields)
Soybeans May-Jun Jul-Sep Sep-Nov August (pod fill — drought kills yields)
Winter Wheat Sep-Oct (prev year) Mar-May Jun-Jul April-May (spring moisture critical)
Spring Wheat Apr-May Jun-Aug Aug-Sep June-July (heat/drought in Dakotas/Montana)
Cotton Apr-May Jun-Sep Oct-Dec Aug-Sep (West Texas drought)

The Weather Premium

Agricultural commodity prices typically build a weather premium during the growing season — an extra $0.30-0.50/bushel in corn or $1.00/bushel in soybeans that reflects the risk of a weather-related yield loss. This premium is highest from May through August when crops are most vulnerable.

If weather is favorable, the premium evaporates at harvest and prices drop — the classic "harvest pressure" pattern. If weather is poor (drought, excessive rain, early frost), the premium expands dramatically and prices spike. Understanding this cycle is the foundation of agricultural commodity trading.

The Southern Hemisphere Offset

Brazil and Argentina — the world's second and third largest grain producers — plant and harvest on an opposite schedule (their summer is our winter). This creates a natural offset: when the US crop is harvested (October), South American planting is just beginning. Problems in one hemisphere can be partially offset by the other — but not always.

Quiz: Crop Calendar

Question: It is mid-July. A severe heat wave hits the US Corn Belt with temperatures above 100F for 10 consecutive days. Corn is in the pollination stage. What is the likely price impact?

Answer: Extremely bullish. Corn pollination is the most temperature-sensitive phase of the crop. Temperatures above 95F during pollination can reduce yields by 1-2% per day of extreme heat. Ten days above 100F could reduce national corn yields by 10-20%. Corn futures would likely rally $0.50-1.50/bushel (8-25%) within a week. The 2012 drought, which hit during pollination, pushed corn from $5.50 to $8.40 (53% rally). This is the highest-impact weather event in grain markets.

Grain Markets

Wheat, Corn, and Soybeans

The "Big Three" grains dominate agricultural commodity trading. Together, they represent over 60% of global crop production and are the most actively traded agricultural futures contracts.

Wheat (ZW)

The geopolitical grain. Russia and Ukraine supply 30% of global exports. Price spikes during wars and droughts. Three grades: Soft Red Winter (SRW), Hard Red Winter (HRW), Spring Wheat.

Corn (ZC)

America's crop. The US produces 35% of world corn. Used for animal feed (40%), ethanol (35%), food (10%), exports (15%). The most liquid ag contract. $5-7/bushel range.

Soybeans (ZS)

The China-dependent crop. China imports 60% of globally traded soybeans for animal feed. Brazil and US compete for Chinese demand. Crush margin (oil + meal) drives profitability.

The Soybean Crush

Soybeans are unique because they are processed (crushed) into two products: soybean oil (cooking oil, biodiesel) and soybean meal (animal feed). The spread between the value of these products and the cost of soybeans is called the crush margin:

Crush Margin = (Soybean Meal Value + Soybean Oil Value) - Soybean Cost

When crush margins are high, processors run at full capacity, increasing demand for soybeans. When crush margins compress, processors slow down. The crush spread is one of the most actively traded spreads in agriculture. Processors hedge it daily.

Quiz: Grain Markets

Question: China unexpectedly cancels 2 million tonnes of US soybean orders and shifts to Brazil. What is the impact on US soybean futures?

Answer: Bearish for US soybeans (ZS drops $0.20-0.50/bushel). China is the dominant buyer of US soybeans — a cancellation of 2M tonnes is significant. The market would price in reduced US exports, higher US ending stocks, and potentially lower soybean prices. However, this is bullish for Brazilian soybeans, and because the two markets are linked (they compete for Chinese demand), the impact may be partially offset. Watch: (1) whether China replaces with Brazilian beans (likely), (2) whether US farmers switch acreage to corn next season, (3) whether the cancellation is political (trade war signal) or logistical.

USDA Reports — The WASDE

The Most Important Report in Agriculture

The WASDE (World Agricultural Supply and Demand Estimates) is published by the USDA on the second Tuesday or Wednesday of each month at 12:00 PM ET. It is the single most market-moving event in agricultural commodities — the equivalent of the EIA report for oil.

WASDE Key Data Points

Metric What It Measures Bullish Signal Bearish Signal
Production Estimate Expected crop output (bushels) Lower than previous estimate Higher than previous estimate
Yield per Acre Bushels produced per planted acre Below trend yield (weather damage) Above trend yield (ideal growing season)
Ending Stocks Surplus remaining at end of marketing year Below 5% stocks-to-use ratio Above 15% stocks-to-use ratio
Exports Projected foreign sales Higher than expected (strong demand) Lower than expected (weak demand)
World Supply Global production from all countries Major producer shortfall (Russia, Brazil) Record global production
Stocks-to-Use Ratio Ending stocks / total demand Below 10% (tight supply) Above 20% (ample supply)

Other Key USDA Reports

  • Crop Progress (weekly, Monday): Reports planting progress, crop condition ratings (% Good/Excellent). A decline in Good/Excellent ratings is bullish.
  • Prospective Plantings (March 31): First estimate of what farmers intend to plant. A surprise shift from corn to soybeans (or vice versa) moves both markets.
  • Grain Stocks (quarterly): Actual count of grain in storage. Can reveal discrepancies with WASDE estimates.
  • Export Sales (weekly, Thursday): Actual export bookings. Large sales to China move soybean prices.
The Supply Balance Equation
Ending Stocks = Beginning Stocks + Production - Domestic Use - Exports
Quiz: WASDE Analysis

Question: The August WASDE report revises US corn yield from 181 bu/acre to 174 bu/acre. Production drops from 15.3B to 14.7B bushels. Ending stocks fall from 2.0B to 1.4B bushels. What is the market reaction?

Answer: This is a major bullish surprise. A 7 bu/acre yield revision is enormous — typically only seen in drought years. The 600M bushel production drop and 30% reduction in ending stocks flips the supply outlook from comfortable to tight. Stocks-to-use ratio drops from ~13% to ~9.5% — approaching the danger zone. Corn futures would gap up $0.30-0.60/bushel (5-10%) on the day of the report and likely continue higher over the following weeks. This is the kind of WASDE report that triggers multi-week rallies as the market reprices the entire forward curve.

Weather Risk in Agriculture

When Weather Destroys Crops

Weather is to agriculture what geopolitics is to oil — the single most powerful short-term price driver, and the one that creates the most extreme moves. Unlike industrial commodities, you cannot substitute your way out of a drought. If the corn crop fails, there is no alternative for 6-12 months.

The Climate Change Factor

Climate change is making agricultural weather events more frequent and more extreme. Key trends:

  • More frequent droughts: The US Corn Belt, Brazil's Cerrado, and Australia's wheat regions are all experiencing more frequent and severe droughts. The probability of a 2012-type drought has roughly doubled since 1980.
  • More intense heat waves: Global temperatures have risen ~1.2C since pre-industrial times. Each additional degree significantly reduces grain yields during critical growth phases.
  • Shifting growing zones: Traditional growing regions are becoming less reliable. Some northern areas (Canada, Russia) are gaining growing season, but soil quality is often poor.
  • More variable rainfall: Both droughts and floods are becoming more common. The same region can experience drought in July and flooding in September.

For traders, this means: agricultural volatility is increasing structurally. Weather premiums should be priced higher than historical averages, and tail risk events (crop failures) are becoming more common.

Quiz: Weather Risk Assessment

Question: NOAA forecasts a La Nina pattern developing (associated with dry conditions in Brazil and wet conditions in the US Midwest). How does this affect your agricultural positioning?

Answer: La Nina creates a mixed bag: Bearish for US corn/soybeans (wet weather in the Midwest generally means good growing conditions and higher yields), but bullish for Brazilian coffee and soybeans (dry conditions in Brazil threaten the safrinha corn crop and coffee yields). The net trade: consider going long coffee futures or Brazilian coffee ETFs, and potentially short US corn if the growing season outlook is favorable. Also watch Argentine soybeans — La Nina often brings drought to Argentina as well, which is bullish for global soybean prices even if the US crop is good.

Soft Commodities

Coffee, Cocoa, Sugar & Cotton

Soft commodities are the "luxury" agricultural products — the crops that are consumed but not essential for survival. They trade on ICE and are characterized by extreme supply concentration (often one or two countries dominate production) and high price volatility.

The Cocoa Crisis of 2024-2026

Cocoa experienced the most extreme price move of any major commodity in recent history: from $2,800/tonne in early 2024 to over $10,000/tonne by late 2024 — a 260% surge. The cause was a perfect storm:

  • Swollen Shoot Virus: A disease that kills cocoa trees spread through West African plantations. Unlike a drought (one season), disease destroys trees permanently, taking 3-5 years to replant.
  • El Nino weather: Drought in West Africa during the critical flowering and pod development phases.
  • Aging trees: Many West African cocoa plantations are 30-40 years old with declining yields. Farmers cannot afford to replant.
  • Concentrated supply: 60% of global cocoa comes from two countries. When both have problems simultaneously, there is nowhere to substitute.

The lesson for traders: when supply is this concentrated and the disruption is structural (not temporary), price moves can be far larger than anyone expects. The same dynamic could happen to coffee (Brazil drought) or vanilla (Madagascar cyclone).

Coffee — The World's Second Most Traded Commodity

By value, coffee is the second most traded commodity after crude oil. More than 2 billion cups are consumed daily worldwide. The market has two main contracts: Arabica (higher quality, traded in New York) and Robusta (cheaper, traded in London).

The Coffee Frost Risk

The single most feared event in coffee markets is a Brazilian frost. Brazil's coffee-growing regions (Minas Gerais, Sao Paulo) are far enough south that winter frosts can damage or kill coffee trees. The legendary 1975 "Black Frost" destroyed 70% of Brazil's coffee trees and sent prices up 300%+ over the following year. Even a moderate frost can push Arabica futures up 20-30% in days. Frost risk peaks in June-August (Brazilian winter). Professional coffee traders monitor Brazilian weather forecasts obsessively during these months.

Quiz: Soft Commodities

Question: A frost warning is issued for Brazil's Minas Gerais coffee region. It is July (peak frost risk month). Coffee futures are at $2.50/lb. What do you expect?

Answer: Coffee would spike sharply on the warning alone — potentially $0.15-0.30/lb (6-12%) within hours. If the frost actually hits and damages trees, the move could extend to $0.50+/lb over the following week. The key variable is severity: a light frost (few hours below 0C) damages current-year cherries but trees survive. A hard frost (extended sub-zero temperatures) kills branches or entire trees, affecting production for 2-3 years. The market initially prices the worst case and then adjusts as crop assessments come in. If you are already long, hold and add on dips. If you are flat, this is a difficult entry because the initial spike is fast — consider buying call options to limit risk if the frost does not materialize.

Food Inflation Dynamics

When Crop Prices Hit Your Grocery Bill

Agricultural commodity prices feed directly into food inflation — though with a lag and amplification. When wheat prices double, bread prices do not double (wheat is only 5-10% of the final cost of a loaf of bread), but the inflationary signal propagates through the entire food chain: animal feed (corn, soybean meal) raises meat prices, sugar and coffee raise beverage prices, and vegetable oils affect everything from cooking to cosmetics.

Food Price Transmission
Commodity Spike → Producer Prices (1-3 months) → Wholesale Prices (3-6 months) → Retail Prices (6-12 months)

Food Security and Geopolitics

Agricultural commodity prices are not just a trading opportunity — they are a geopolitical weapon. Countries that depend on food imports are vulnerable to supply disruptions:

  • Egypt: World's largest wheat importer. The 2011 Arab Spring was triggered partly by wheat price spikes that made bread unaffordable. When wheat doubled in 2022 (Russia-Ukraine war), Egypt faced a fiscal crisis.
  • India: Banned wheat and rice exports multiple times (2022-2024) to protect domestic food security, exacerbating global shortages.
  • China: Building massive grain reserves (estimated 50-70% of global corn and wheat stocks) as insurance against supply disruptions and as a strategic tool.

For traders, this means: export bans and import surges by major countries can move agricultural commodity prices more than weather events. Watch government policy as closely as weather forecasts.

Quiz: Food Inflation

Question: Corn prices rise 30% due to a US drought. How does this flow through to consumer prices?

Answer: Corn transmits to consumers through multiple channels: (1) Animal feed: Corn is 40% of chicken/hog feed. A 30% corn increase raises feed costs ~12%, which eventually raises meat prices 3-5% (farmers absorb some, pass some). (2) Ethanol: 35% of US corn goes to ethanol. Higher corn = higher ethanol = slightly higher gasoline blend prices. (3) High-fructose corn syrup: Used in beverages and processed food. Higher corn = higher HFCS prices. (4) CPI impact: Food is ~13% of US CPI. A 30% corn spike might add 0.1-0.2% to headline CPI with a 3-6 month lag. The transmission is real but attenuated — it takes time, and food companies absorb margin compression before passing costs to consumers.

Agribusiness Stocks vs Commodity ETFs

Like energy, agribusiness stocks (MOO) often outperform commodity ETFs (DBA) because they do not suffer contango drag and benefit from operational leverage. Key picks:

  • Deere (DE): World's largest farm equipment maker. Benefits from high crop prices (farmers invest in equipment when profitable). Good proxy for the entire ag cycle.
  • Archer-Daniels-Midland (ADM): Processes and trades agricultural commodities globally. Benefits from high trading volumes and volatile prices (more arbitrage opportunities).
  • Bunge (BG): Major grain trader and processor. Brazilian operations give exposure to South American crops.
  • Corteva (CTVA): Seeds and crop protection chemicals. Benefits when farmers plant aggressively (high crop prices incentivize maximum planting).
Quiz: Agricultural Investment

Question: You want broad agricultural exposure for the next 12 months as an inflation hedge. Compare DBA vs MOO.

Answer: MOO is likely the better choice for a 12-month hold. DBA (commodity ETF) holds futures contracts on 10+ agricultural commodities and suffers from contango drag (~2-4% per year). Over 12 months, this is significant. MOO (agribusiness stock ETF) holds companies like Deere, ADM, and Bunge that benefit from high agricultural prices but also earn money in other ways (equipment services, trading margins). MOO pays a ~1.5% dividend, versus zero for DBA. MOO also benefits from operational leverage: when crop prices rise, farmer profitability increases, equipment sales rise, and seed/chemical companies see higher volumes. The main risk: MOO has equity market risk (could fall in a broad market selloff even if crop prices rise). For a pure commodity hedge, DBA is more direct but less capital-efficient.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways — Part 5

  • Agricultural commodities follow rigid annual crop cycles. The weather premium builds May-August and evaporates at harvest if weather is favorable.
  • The WASDE report (monthly USDA) is the key data release. Watch yield-per-acre revisions and stocks-to-use ratios. Below 10% stocks-to-use = tight market.
  • Weather is the dominant short-term driver. Corn pollination (July) is the single highest-risk event. Drought during pollination can produce 50%+ price rallies.
  • Soft commodities (coffee, cocoa, sugar) have extreme supply concentration risk. 60% of cocoa from two West African countries. 35% of coffee from Brazil. One drought can move prices 100%+.
  • Climate change is increasing agricultural volatility structurally. Droughts, floods, and heat waves are becoming more frequent and severe. Weather premiums should be priced higher.
  • Agribusiness stocks (MOO, DE, ADM) typically outperform commodity ETFs (DBA) over 12+ month holding periods due to no contango, dividends, and operational leverage.
  • Food inflation transmits with a 3-12 month lag and is attenuated. A 30% commodity spike adds ~0.1-0.2% to headline CPI.
