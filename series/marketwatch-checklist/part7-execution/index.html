Entry Mechanics

Entry Timing: Market, Limit, or Scale In?

You found a great setup. The chart is clean, the thesis is solid, risk management checks out. Now comes the part where most traders blow it: how and when to actually click Buy.

Three approaches, each with a clear use case. Using the wrong one is like driving a nail with a screwdriver — the tool works, just not for this job.

Order Type Best For Risk When to Use
Market Order High-conviction breakouts with volume Slippage on low-float stocks Breakout confirmed on 15m+ close, volume > 2x avg
Limit Order Pullback entries at defined support May never fill if momentum accelerates Pullback to EMA 20, retest of breakout level
Scaling In High-conviction trades with uncertain timing More commission, complexity Multi-day setups, position trades, uncertain entry zone

The "Free Look" Principle

Limit orders at support give you what professional poker players call a "free look." If your level holds, you get filled at the best price. If it breaks, you were never in the trade. You only pay when the market proves you right.

Market orders are the opposite: you pay first, then find out if you were right. Use them only when the opportunity cost of missing the trade outweighs the cost of slippage.

The Entry Decision Rule
Breakout + Volume = Market Order  |  Pullback + Support = Limit Order  |  Uncertain + High Conviction = Scale In
Time of Day

Why Time of Day Matters More Than You Think

Not all hours are created equal. The same setup at 9:35 AM and at 10:15 AM has a completely different probability profile. Intraday volume distribution tells the story.

The First 30 Minutes Trap. 9:30–10:00 AM is where amateurs get slaughtered. Wide spreads, gap fills, stop hunts, and institutional order flow create the illusion of direction. The move you see at 9:35 is often fully reversed by 10:15. Unless you are scalping with Level 2, do not enter positions before 10:00 AM.

The Two Power Windows

10:00 – 11:00 AM (Morning Session)

The opening chaos is settled. Real institutional direction emerges. Breakouts that happen here on volume tend to hold. This is your primary entry window for swing trades.

Best for: Breakout entries, momentum continuation, gap-and-go setups that survived the first 30 minutes.

3:00 – 4:00 PM (Power Hour)

Institutional rebalancing, MOC (Market-on-Close) orders, and end-of-day positioning create the second-highest volume window. Closing prices carry weight for daily chart signals.

Best for: Confirming daily breakouts, taking profits, entering positions for next-day gaps.

The Midday Desert

11:30 AM – 2:30 PM is the "lunch lull." Volume drops 40-60%, spreads widen, and moves tend to be noise rather than signal. If nothing happened in the morning session, nothing is likely to happen here. Use this time for research, not trading.

Position Building

The 50-30-20 Scaling Rule

Going all-in at once is a binary bet. Scaling in converts a single decision into a series of smaller decisions, each informed by new information. Here is the rule we use for every position above 3% portfolio weight.

1

Tranche 1 — 50%

Initial entry at your planned level. This is your "thesis test." If the stock immediately moves against you, your loss is half of what it would have been.

Entry signal confirmed

2

Tranche 2 — 30%

Add after the first confirmation: price holds above entry for 1-2 days, volume supports the move, no bearish reversal candle.

Thesis confirmed by price

3

Tranche 3 — 20%

Final add on strength. Price approaching TP1 zone and you want full exposure for the TP2 leg. Only if the trend is clearly accelerating.

Momentum acceleration

Never scale into a losing position. Adding to losers (averaging down) is the single most common way retail traders turn small losses into account-destroying losses. Each tranche should be added at a higher price than the last — you are paying for confirmation, not hoping for a bounce.

Exit Framework

Three Exit Strategies

You need to know exactly how you will exit before you enter. Not "I'll figure it out" — a specific, written plan for every position. Here are the three exit frameworks we use.

Take-Profit Rules (TP1 / TP2)

Take Profit Framework
TP1 = 1:1 R/R → Scale out 50%   |   TP2 = 1:2 R/R → Trail the rest with ATR stop

At TP1, you have recovered your risk. The remaining 50% of the position is now a "free trade" — the worst outcome from here is breakeven on the total position. This is the psychological unlock that allows you to hold for the bigger move.

Level Action Position After Stop Management
Entry Full position (100%) 100% Initial ATR stop (1.5x ATR below entry)
TP1 (1:1 move) Sell 50% 50% Move stop to breakeven
TP2 (1:2 move) Trail remaining 50% Trailing ATR trailing stop (2x ATR below high)
Trail Stop Hit Exit remaining position 0% —

Time-Based Exits

A setup that does not work within its expected timeframe is a setup that is not working. Period. Holding longer is not patience — it is hope, and hope is not a strategy.

5-Day Rule

If a swing trade has not reached TP1 within 5 trading days, reduce position by 50% or exit entirely. The setup was either wrong or too early — either way, your capital is dead money.

10-Day Hard Exit

If a swing trade has not reached TP1 within 10 trading days, exit the full position regardless of P/L. Capital tied up in a non-performing trade has an opportunity cost. Redeploy.

Stop Management & ATR Trailing

Static stops get you out too early in volatile stocks and too late in quiet ones. ATR-based stops adapt to the stock's actual behavior.

Phase Stop Calculation Purpose
Initial (Entry to TP1) Entry - 1.5x ATR(14) Give the trade room to breathe without excessive risk
Breakeven (After TP1) Entry price Eliminate risk on remaining position
Trailing (After TP2) Highest close - 2x ATR(14) Ride the trend while protecting profits

The ATR advantage. A stock with ATR of $2 and a stock with ATR of $0.50 both get "1.5x ATR" stops — but the first gets $3 of breathing room and the second gets $0.75. The stop respects the stock's volatility personality, not your arbitrary dollar amount.

Psychology

Why Cutting Winners Is Harder Than Cutting Losers

Behavioral finance research (Kahneman, Tversky) shows that the pain of losing $1 is 2.5x more intense than the pleasure of gaining $1. This creates a paradox:

Cutting Losers (Easier Than You Think)

Losing trades trigger pain avoidance. Your brain wants the pain to stop. If you have a mechanical stop, the decision is made for you. Most traders actually cut losers reasonably well.

Cutting Winners (The Real Problem)

A winning trade triggers fear of regret. "What if it goes higher after I sell?" This fear is so powerful that traders sell winners too early (securing the pleasure) and hold losers too long (avoiding the pain of realizing a loss).

The Antidote: Pre-Commitment

The only reliable way to beat the disposition effect is to commit to your exit plan before you enter. Write down TP1, TP2, and your trailing method. When the price hits those levels, execute mechanically. No "let me re-evaluate." No "but the trend looks strong." The plan was made when you were rational. Execute it.

This is why the TP1/TP2 framework works: selling 50% at TP1 satisfies your brain's need to "lock in gains," while the remaining 50% with a trailing stop lets the position run without requiring a new decision.

Special Situations

Earnings, Weekends & Broken Theses

Earnings Plays

An earnings report can move a stock 10-20% in either direction overnight. No chart pattern, no indicator, no technical signal can predict the outcome. Here is how we handle positions going into earnings.

Situation Action Rationale
Position profitable, earnings in <5 days Close 75-100% before the report You are converting a skill-based trade into a coin flip. Take the win.
Position at breakeven, earnings in <5 days Close 100% Zero edge holding through binary events. Redeploy capital.
Position losing, earnings in <5 days Close 100% Holding a loser through earnings is "hoping for a miracle." Exit.
No position, want to play earnings Use options only (defined risk) If you must gamble, cap the downside. Straddles or small directional bets only.

The "Thesis Broken" Exit

Sometimes the reason you entered a trade fundamentally changes. A customer concentration risk materializes. A CEO resigns. A regulatory ruling changes the industry landscape. When the thesis breaks, ignore your chart.

When fundamentals change, technicals are meaningless. The chart showed a perfect ascending triangle — then the FDA rejected their drug application. The chart showed a bullish flag — then they reported 40% revenue miss. Your support level does not matter when the reason you bought the stock no longer exists. Exit at market. Immediately.

  • CEO/CFO sudden departure — especially if no successor named. Exit same day.
  • Revenue miss > 10% — the market was fundamentally wrong about the business trajectory.
  • Guidance cut or withdrawn — management is telling you they do not know what is coming.
  • Regulatory adverse decision — FDA rejection, antitrust action, new tax/tariff.
  • Major customer loss — if one customer is > 20% of revenue and leaves, thesis is dead.

Friday Afternoon Position Management

Weekends introduce 64 hours of unhedgeable risk. Geopolitical events, executive scandals, natural disasters — anything can happen while the market is closed and you cannot exit.

Profitable Positions

If a trade is near TP1, consider taking partial profits Friday afternoon. Locking in gains before a 2-day blackout is smart risk management, not cowardice.

Losing Positions

If a trade is within 1 ATR of your stop on Friday at 3 PM, close it. Monday gap-downs blow through stops. The extra day is not worth the gap risk.

New Positions

Do not open new swing positions on Friday afternoon. The immediate 64-hour exposure with no exit option is unnecessary risk for no additional edge.

Measuring Execution Quality

Good execution is not just about making money. Track these five dimensions weekly to identify patterns in your execution that are costing you returns.

Metric Measures Target
Entry Accuracy % of entries within planned zone (not chased) > 80%
TP Discipline % of TP1 levels where you actually took profit > 90%
Stop Respect % of stops honored (not moved/removed) 100%
Time Management % of trades exited within time rules > 85%
Sizing Accuracy % of positions within planned sizing (ATR-based) > 90%
The Checklist

The 8-Item Execution Checklist

Before every Buy and every Sell, run through this checklist. Not mentally — physically check each item. Pilots do not skip the pre-flight checklist because they "feel confident about the engine." Neither should you.

Before Clicking "Buy"

1
Entry zone confirmed? Price is within the planned entry zone, not chased above it. If the stock has already moved 3%+ past your level, you missed it.
2
Time window valid? Is it between 10:00-11:00 AM or 3:00-4:00 PM? If not, is there a specific catalyst justifying entry outside these windows?
3
Stop calculated and set? Your ATR-based stop is defined, the order is placed (or ready to place). No "I'll set it later." Now.
4
Position size correct? Risk per trade is within 1-2% of total portfolio. Size calculated from stop distance, not from "how many shares feel right."
5
TP1 and TP2 defined? You know exactly where you will sell the first 50% and how you will trail the rest. Written down, not "in your head."
6
Earnings check done? No earnings report within 5 trading days. If there is one, this is a binary bet, not a trade. Walk away.
7
Correlation check? Does this trade duplicate sector or factor exposure you already have? Adding a 4th semiconductor stock is not diversification.
8
Thesis stated in one sentence? If you cannot explain why you are buying this stock in one clear sentence, you do not understand the trade well enough to take it.

Before Clicking "Sell"

1
Which exit rule triggered? TP1 hit, TP2 trail stop hit, time-based exit, thesis broken, or stop loss. Name it. "I feel like selling" is not a rule.
2
Partial or full exit? At TP1, sell 50%. At stop or thesis-broken, sell 100%. At time-based exit, sell 50% or 100% depending on price action. Know the amount.
3
Stop updated after partial? If selling at TP1, have you moved the stop to breakeven on the remaining position? This must happen simultaneously.
4
Friday afternoon? If it is Friday after 3 PM and the position is near your stop, close it. Do not carry weekend gap risk on a marginal position.
5
Journal entry ready? Before or immediately after the sell, log: entry price, exit price, R multiple, what worked, what didn't. This feeds the retrospective.
6
Emotional state check. Are you selling because the plan says to, or because you are scared/greedy/bored? If the latter, step away from the screen for 10 minutes first.
7
Limit order or market? For non-urgent exits (TP1, time-based), use limit orders. For urgent exits (thesis broken, stop hit), use market orders. Match urgency to order type.
8
Capital redeployment plan? Where does the freed capital go? Back to cash, next setup in the pipeline, or increase existing position? Idle capital has a cost.

Part 7 Takeaways

  • Match order type to situation — market orders for confirmed breakouts, limit orders for pullbacks, scaling for uncertain timing.
  • Trade the power windows — 10:00-11:00 AM for entries, 3:00-4:00 PM for confirmations. Avoid the first 30 minutes and the lunch lull.
  • Scale in 50-30-20 — each tranche is bought at a higher price, paying for confirmation rather than averaging into hope.
  • TP1 at 1:1, sell 50%, move stop to breakeven — this single rule converts your remaining position into a free trade.
  • Time kills trades — no TP1 in 5 days means reduce; no TP1 in 10 days means exit. Dead money has an opportunity cost.
  • When the thesis breaks, ignore the chart — fundamentals override technicals. Exit at market, no questions.
  • Run the 8-item checklist every time — before every Buy and every Sell. Physically, not mentally. No exceptions.
