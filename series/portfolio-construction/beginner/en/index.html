How Many Stocks?

How Many Stocks Should You Own?

In Simple Terms

Think of your portfolio like a basket of eggs. If you put all your eggs in one basket and that basket drops, you lose everything. But if you have too many baskets, you cannot keep an eye on all of them. The sweet spot depends on how much money you have and how much time you can spend.

> $50,00015-254-7%Institutional-style portfolio. Beyond 25, adding stocks does not reduce risk further.

Key Takeaway: The 90% Rule

Academic research shows that 90% of the benefit of diversification is achieved with just 15-20 stocks from different industries. Beyond that, you are adding complexity without meaningfully reducing risk. With 30+ stocks, you are basically copying an index fund — so you might as well just buy an ETF instead (which is cheaper and easier).

How to invest while you sleep (seriously)

In Simple Terms: What Is an ETF?

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) is like a pre-made basket of stocks you can buy in one click. Imagine going to a grocery store: instead of buying 500 individual items one by one, you buy a single "variety pack" that contains a tiny piece of each one. For example, an S&P 500 ETF owns shares in all 500 of America's largest companies. It is the simplest and most effective way to invest for 90% of people.

What Is DCA? (Dollar Cost Averaging)

In Simple Terms

DCA means investing a fixed amount of money on a regular schedule, no matter what the market is doing. Instead of trying to guess the "perfect" time to invest $6,000, you invest $500 every month for 12 months. When prices are low, your $500 buys more shares. When prices are high, you buy fewer. Over time, this averages out to a good price — and you never have to agonize about timing.

Your average purchase price was $472 even though the final price was $500. You took advantage of the dips in February and March to buy more shares at lower prices. This is the magic of DCA: market dips become your friend instead of your enemy.

But Wait — Is It Better to Invest Everything at Once?

Statistically, investing everything at once ("lump sum") beats DCA about 2 out of 3 times, because markets tend to go up over time. However, DCA is psychologically much easier — it prevents you from investing your entire savings right before a crash, and it stops you from panicking and selling at the bottom. For beginners, DCA is almost always the smarter choice.

How to Set Up Automatic Investing

Most brokers let you set up automatic recurring investments. Here is how:

  1. Pick your ETF (e.g., VOO or VTI)
  2. Set a schedule (e.g., every 1st of the month)
  3. Choose your amount (e.g., $200/month)
  4. Enable fractional shares (so you can invest exact dollar amounts)
  5. Forget about it — let it run automatically

On Fidelity, this is called "Automatic Investments." On Schwab, it is "Automatic Investing." On Robinhood, it is "Recurring Investments." All major brokers support this feature at no extra cost.

The "Total World" ETF Trap

A "Total World" ETF is NOT what you think. Despite the name "World" or "Total International," funds like VT (Vanguard Total World) are still ~60% US stocks. That means you are far more exposed to the US economy than the name suggests.

In Simple Terms:

  • VT (Total World) = ~60% US, 7% Japan, 4% UK, 3% China
  • The top 10 companies (Apple, Microsoft, NVIDIA...) = ~20% of the total
  • If US tech drops 30%, your "World" ETF loses ~15-20%
  • Owning VTI (Total US) + VT (Total World) = ~80% US stocks! That is NOT real diversification

Real Diversification Means:

Owning investments that do not all move in the same direction at the same time. For example: US stocks + international stocks + bonds + gold. When stocks crash, bonds and gold often go up. That is true diversification. A simple combo: VTI + VXUS + BND + GLD covers all four bases.

Key Takeaway:

A single "Total World" ETF is not enough diversification on its own. Add at least a bond ETF (BND) and a gold ETF (GLD) to truly spread your risk across different asset classes.

Reading a Stock Chart

How to Read a Stock Chart
The 4 things to check on any chart before investing (5 minutes is all you need)

In Simple Terms

A stock chart shows you where the price has been and gives you clues about where it might be going. You do not need to be an expert — just learn these 4 things and you will be ahead of most beginners. Use a free tool like TradingView.com to follow along.

1

The Trend: Is the Stock Going Up or Down?

Look at the moving averages (the smooth lines on a chart). The two most important are the 50-day and 200-day moving averages. If the price is above both lines, the trend is up (bullish). If it is below both, the trend is down (bearish). You generally want to buy stocks in an uptrend and avoid those in a downtrend. On TradingView, type "MA" in the indicators box and add 50 and 200.

2

The Volume: How Many People Are Trading?

Volume is shown as bars at the bottom of the chart. It tells you how many shares were traded. High volume + rising price = real, healthy move. Low volume + rising price = weak move that might not last. Without volume behind it, a price move is unlikely to last.

3

Support & Resistance: The Floor and the Ceiling

A support is a price level where the stock tends to stop falling and bounce back (like a floor). A resistance is a price level the stock has trouble breaking above (like a ceiling). When a stock finally breaks above resistance with high volume, that is often a great buy signal (called a "breakout").

4

RSI: Is the Stock Overbought or Oversold?

The RSI (Relative Strength Index) is a number from 0 to 100. Above 70 means the stock has gone up a lot recently and might pull back (overbought). Below 30 means it has dropped a lot and might bounce (oversold). Between 40-65 is the sweet spot for buying. It helps you gauge whether a stock has moved too far too fast, or whether it has been beaten down enough to warrant a closer look.

Key Takeaway: Your 5-Minute Chart Checklist

  • Is the price above the 50-day and 200-day moving averages? (Trend is UP)
  • Is volume increasing along with the price? (Move is REAL)
  • Is the stock near support (good entry) or near resistance (be careful)?
  • Is the RSI between 30 and 70? (Not overheated or crashing)

If all 4 check out, you have a healthy stock worth considering!

Dividends Explained Simply

What Is a Dividend?

In Simple Terms:

Imagine you co-own a bakery. At the end of the year, the bakery made $100,000 in profit. The manager decides to give you a share of that profit: that is the dividend. In the stock market, it works the same way: when a company makes money, it can distribute some of it to its shareholders.

The Ex-Dividend Date Trap

Classic beginner mistake: Buying a stock right before the dividend to get "free money." It does not work that way.

In Simple Terms:

On the day a dividend is paid, the stock price drops by the exact amount of the dividend. Example: Apple is at $200 and pays a $0.96 dividend. The next day, the stock opens at ~$199.04. You receive $0.96 in cash, but your shares are worth $0.96 less. Your total wealth has not changed — it is a transfer, not a gain.

3 Things to Know About Dividends

1

Dividend Yield

This is the annual dividend divided by the stock price. 2-5% = good. >8% = be careful — a very high yield often means the stock price has crashed (the company is in trouble). An unusually high yield is often a red flag that the company's stock price has crashed.

2

Dividend Aristocrats

These are companies that have increased their dividend every year for 25+ years straight: Coca-Cola, Johnson & Johnson, Procter & Gamble... Their track record of consistent payouts makes them among the most reliable investments you can find. For easy access, look at the ETFs SCHD or VYM which hold dozens of dividend-paying companies in one fund.

3

DRIP (Dividend Reinvestment)

Instead of receiving the dividend as cash, you can automatically reinvest it by buying more shares. Snowball effect: those new shares generate their own dividends. Turn on DRIP in your brokerage settings — Fidelity, Schwab, and Robinhood all offer it for free!

Key Takeaway:

Dividends are a bonus, not free money. The stock price drops by the amount paid. Prioritize companies that consistently increase their dividend (aristocrats like KO, JNJ, PG) over those with the highest yield. For beginners, a dividend ETF like SCHD or VYM is the simplest way to start.

Common Mistakes
!
10 Common Mistakes That Cost Beginners Money
Every mistake here has cost millions of real people thousands of dollars

In Simple Terms

The stock market is not hard to understand, but it is easy to make emotional mistakes. These are the traps that catch almost every new investor at some point. Read them now so you recognize them when they happen to you.

No Stop-Loss

"It will come back up!" — No, sometimes it does not. Some stocks dropped 90% and never recovered (SPCE, WISH). A stop-loss is like a seatbelt: set it at -8% to -10% and it automatically sells before a small loss becomes a disaster.

FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out)

Buying a stock AFTER it has already gone up 50% because "everyone is talking about it." By the time it is in the news, the early buyers are selling to YOU. If you missed the train, wait for the next one.

Averaging Down Blindly

"It dropped 30%, so I will buy more!" — if the company's fundamentals are getting worse, you are just doubling down on a losing bet. Only buy more if your original reason for buying is still valid.

Over-Trading

Buying and selling 10 times a week = fees + taxes + stress + bad decisions. Studies show the most active traders underperform by 6.5% per year. Less is more.

Ignoring the Economic Calendar

Opening a position on the day of an inflation report (CPI) or a Federal Reserve meeting (FOMC) is like playing roulette. These events can move the market 3% in 5 minutes.

Following Social Media "Gurus"

Many stock influencers on YouTube and TikTok are paid to promote the stocks they talk about. Always do your own research. If it sounds too good to be true, it usually is.

No Diversification

Putting 100% in one sector (e.g., all tech stocks in 2022) = -33% when that sector crashes. Spread your money across at least 3-4 different industries.

Investing Money You Cannot Lose

Your rent money, emergency fund, and debt payments are OFF LIMITS. Only invest money you could lose without it affecting your daily life. Have 3-6 months of expenses saved FIRST.

Ignoring Taxes

In the US, short-term capital gains (held < 1 year) are taxed as regular income (up to 37%). Long-term gains (held > 1 year) are taxed at only 0-20%. Use tax-advantaged accounts (Roth IRA, 401k) whenever possible! In the UK, use your ISA. In France, use your PEA.

Revenge Trading

Losing $500 and immediately trying to "win it back" by making a bigger trade is the fastest way to turn a small loss into a devastating one. After a loss, take at least 24 hours off before making another trade.

Key Takeaway: The Golden Rules

  • Always use a stop-loss (your seatbelt)
  • Never invest money you need for bills or emergencies
  • Do not chase stocks that already went up 50%
  • Less trading = better returns (counterintuitive but proven)
  • Use tax-advantaged accounts first

Currency Risk (The Hidden Cost of International Investing)

In Simple Terms:

When you invest in international stocks (through ETFs like VXUS, VWO, or EFA), those companies earn money in foreign currencies (euros, yen, pounds...). If the US dollar gets stronger, those foreign earnings are worth less in dollar terms — even if the stock price went up in its local market. A +10% gain on a European stock can shrink to +2% or less once you convert back to dollars if the exchange rate moved against you.

ScenarioYour Stock's Gain (Local Currency)What You Actually Get (in USD)
Dollar weakens+10%+15% (bonus!)
Dollar stable+10%+10% (normal)
Dollar strengthens+10%+2% (ouch!)
Key Takeaway:

Over the long term (10+ years), currency fluctuations tend to even out. Do not worry about it if you are investing for 10+ years. If your time horizon is short (<2 years), look for "currency-hedged" ETFs (like HEFA instead of EFA) that protect against this risk. For most beginners with a long-term plan, just keep investing and ignore the noise.

Using AI to Manage Your Portfolio

AI: Your Smart Assistant

AI is a powerful analysis tool, but not an oracle. It can organize your ideas, spot imbalances in your portfolio, and explain concepts. However, it cannot make decisions for you or predict where the market is going.
Important security note: Never share your real account numbers, passwords, or banking credentials with an AI chatbot. Only give it the names of your stocks and your allocation percentages — that's all the AI needs to help you.

3 Essential Prompts to Get Started

Check your portfolio

Copy and paste this into Claude, ChatGPT, or Perplexity, replacing the brackets:

"Here is my portfolio: [LIST OF STOCKS AND % OF EACH]. Tell me simply: 1) Am I too concentrated in one sector? 2) If the market drops 20%, roughly how much would I lose? 3) What could I add to reduce risk? Explain as if I'm a beginner."
In simple terms: This prompt asks the AI to check whether your portfolio is too concentrated in one sector and to suggest improvements.

Know when to rebalance

Use this prompt once a month:

"Originally, I wanted to have [DESIRED SPLIT, e.g.: 50% global ETF, 30% tech, 20% bonds]. Now it has become [CURRENT SPLIT]. Do I need to rebalance? If so, what should I sell and buy? Keep in mind that selling can trigger capital gains taxes."
In simple terms: Over time, some stocks grow faster than others and your portfolio drifts away from your target allocation. Rebalancing means selling a bit of what has grown too much and buying more of what is underweight.

Morning summary

Best used in Perplexity (which has internet access):

"I own these stocks: [LIST]. Summarize in 2 minutes what happened last night and this morning that could impact my investments. Is there anything important today (earnings reports, economic data)? Tell me if I should be concerned or not."
In simple terms: This prompt gives you a personalized 2-minute summary of financial news, filtered to only what affects your specific holdings.

What AI Can NOT Do

AI is a powerful tool, but it has important limitations you need to know:

1. It doesn't know live prices. Most chatbots (Claude, ChatGPT) work with data that is several months old. Only Perplexity has real-time internet access. For current prices, always use your broker or Yahoo Finance.

2. It can make math mistakes. Percentages, compound returns, ratios... AI sometimes gets basic arithmetic wrong. Always double-check important numbers with a calculator.

3. It tends to recommend the same stocks. Since it's trained on historical data, it tends to suggest famous companies that did well in the past (Apple, Microsoft...). That doesn't guarantee future performance.

4. It's not a financial advisor. No chatbot replaces a licensed financial advisor (SEC/FINRA in the US). AI doesn't know your personal situation, your taxes, or your life plans.

3 Key Moments to Use AI

1

Every morning (2 min)

Use Prompt #3 in Perplexity for a quick summary. If everything looks fine, close it and move on. You don't need to watch your portfolio all day!

2

Once a month (10 min)

Use Prompt #1 to check that your portfolio is still balanced. If any position is more than 5% off your target, use Prompt #2 to decide if you should rebalance.

3

Before every purchase (5 min)

Before adding a new position, ask AI: "I already own [LIST]. If I add [NEW STOCK], does it improve or hurt my diversification?"

Key Takeaway

  • AI is an analysis tool, not an oracle — it helps you think, not predict
  • Start with Perplexity (free, internet access) for your daily summaries
  • Use Claude or ChatGPT for deeper analysis (audits, rebalancing)
  • Never share your banking credentials or passwords with a chatbot
  • Always verify calculations — AI can make mistakes with numbers
  • Cross-reference sources — ask 2 different chatbots and compare the answers

3 Strategies Based on Your Goal

In Simple Terms

The 3 portfolios above are generic templates. In real life, your portfolio should match your personal goal. Here are the 3 most common goals:

Monthly Income
The idea: Buy stocks that pay regular dividends so you receive cash every month or quarter, without selling your shares.

Examples: SCHD, VYM (dividend ETFs), O/Realty Income (monthly dividend REIT)
How much do you need? Between $150,000 and $800,000 to receive $500-$3,000/month

The bottom line: You buy shares in companies that pay regular dividends. The more shares you accumulate, the more passive income you receive — without having to sell anything.

Retirement Savings
The idea: Invest a small amount every month for 20-30 years and let compound interest do the heavy lifting.

What to buy: A total market ETF (VOO or VTI) inside a Roth IRA. That is it.
How much: Starting from $100/month. The key is never stopping.

The bottom line: Growth starts slow but accelerates dramatically thanks to compound interest. At $100/month for 25 years at 7%/yr = ~$81,000 from only $30,000 invested. A Roth IRA is ideal: you pay tax now, but everything grows and is withdrawn tax-free after age 59½.

Children's College Fund
The idea: Set aside $150-$300/month from the day your child is born to have $50-$100K in 18 years.

What to buy: Mostly stock ETFs (80%) at the start, then gradually shift to more bonds as college gets closer.
Where: A 529 Plan — grows tax-free AND withdrawals are tax-free for education expenses.

The bottom line: At $150/month for 18 years at 7%/yr, you will have about $65,000 from only $32,000 invested. The secret: start early. The younger the child, the more time compound interest has to work. Bonus: unused 529 money can now be rolled over to a Roth IRA (up to $35K, thanks to the SECURE 2.0 Act).

Key Takeaway

  • Define your goal before picking your investments
  • A retirement portfolio (20-30 years) and an income portfolio (right now) are completely different
  • The longer your time horizon, the more risk you can take
  • DCA (investing a fixed amount every month) is the simplest and most effective method
Can You Live Off the Stock Market?

The Question Everyone Asks

In Simple Terms:

Yes, it is possible, but it requires much more money than most people think. The idea is simple: you have a large pile of money invested, and you withdraw a small percentage each year to cover your living expenses.

The 4% Rule: You can withdraw 4% of your portfolio per year without running out of money over 30 years. Think of it like a fruit tree: you pick the fruit (4%) but the tree keeps growing.

Warning:

  • Markets do not go up every year: some years you will lose 20-30%. Enduring that when it is your only income is extremely stressful
  • No job = no employer health insurance (ACA marketplace is expensive), no 401(k) match, no safety net
  • Inflation eats your purchasing power: $4,000 today = ~$2,900 in 15 years

The Realistic Advice:

The best approach: keep earning income (job, freelance, rental properties) and use the stock market to supplement your income and build wealth over time. That is what 95% of "full-time investors" actually do in practice. The stock market is an incredible tool for long-term wealth building, not a quick salary replacement. Check out r/financialindependence (the FIRE movement) for realistic stories from people who achieved financial independence.

Key Takeaway:

Living off the stock market requires $600K-$1.5M+ in capital. That is a 15-25 year goal, not a 6-month plan. In the meantime, invest consistently (DCA) and let time and compound interest do the heavy lifting for you.

8 Questions to Test Your Knowledge

Click each question to reveal the answer. 6/8 = you are ready to start investing!

1. What is an ETF in one sentence?

It is a basket of stocks you can buy in a single click. Instead of buying Apple, Microsoft, and Google separately, you buy one ETF that contains all three (and hundreds more). It is the simplest and least risky way to invest in the stock market.

2. A "Total World" ETF gives you truly global diversification. True or False?

FALSE! Despite the name, a Total World ETF like VT is still about 60% US stocks. For real global diversification, you need to pair it with dedicated international (VXUS) and emerging market (VWO) exposure, plus non-stock assets like bonds (BND) and gold (GLD).

3. If a stock pays a $1 dividend, you earn $1. True or False?

FALSE! On the ex-dividend date, the stock price drops by $1. You receive $1 in cash, but your shares are worth $1 less. It is a transfer within your portfolio, not a net gain. Your total wealth stays the same.

4. What is DCA?

Dollar Cost Averaging = investing the same amount at regular intervals (e.g., $200 every month). The advantage: you buy fewer shares when prices are high and more shares when prices are low. No need to guess the perfect time to invest!

5. How much money do you need to live off the stock market at $4,000/month?

About $1,200,000 invested (using the 4% rule). That is a lot! The stock market is an excellent tool for building wealth over the long term, but it is not a quick salary replacement. Start by investing regularly and let time do the heavy lifting.

6. Why should you never trust stock numbers given by ChatGPT or Claude?

Because AI can make up numbers (this is called "hallucination"). It might tell you a company has a P/E of 18.7 with complete confidence... when it is totally wrong. Always verify on Yahoo Finance or TradingView.

7. Is there a hidden risk when US investors buy international stocks?

Yes: currency risk. When the US dollar gets stronger, your foreign stock holdings lose value in dollar terms. A +10% gain in a European stock could become just +2% after converting back to dollars. Over the long run (10+ years), this tends to even out, but it is good to know.

8. Earning 10% per year in the stock market: normal or exceptional?

It is the historical average! The S&P 500 has returned about 10% per year over the last 100 years. Warren Buffett does ~20%/year and is considered a genius. If someone promises you 30%+ per year, it is almost certainly a scam.
